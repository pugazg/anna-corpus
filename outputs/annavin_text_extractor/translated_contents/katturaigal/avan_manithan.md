## English Translation

"Where is that mad dog? It was barking away until now. Where has it run? Did the dog flee the moment it saw the whip?"

Gopalar shouted angrily. Alagan stood submissively before him, one of his master's many servants. There was a whip in the master's hand and anger boiling upon his face. But the "mad dog" alone was not before him.

The mad dog was not even a dog. He was another of the master's servants. "The wages you give me are not enough to fill my stomach," he had pleaded. His pay was ten rupees a month.

"Not enough for your stomach? Who would pay more than ten rupees for the work you do?" the master threatened.

"What work have I ever said I would not do? I labour like an ox," he pleaded further.

"Do not think this false display of sorrow will work. I am not the man to fall for it," the master said firmly.

"Where else can I go and speak of my hardship if I cannot tell you? I swear, sir, the wages you give are not enough for food," he explained, choking with emotion.

"The wages are not enough? What am I to do about that?" the master replied angrily again.

"What other way do I have, sir?" the servant begged.

"A way? Go and steal somewhere," the master mocked.

He could no longer contain his anger, and words poured rapidly from him:

"Yes, I must go and steal. I must work here, labour like an ox, remain hungry at night and hungry by day—or else I must steal. Yes, sir, that is what I must do. If I toil for sinners like you, who do not possess even a mustard-seed of compassion in your hearts, I must steal merely to fill my stomach."

The poor man had somehow said it all. Afterwards, even he was astonished that he had managed to speak so much. He was a cowardly man; his friends even mocked him as a mute insect. Yet that day, somehow, he spoke at length.

The master's anger rose to his head. He called another servant and ordered him to bring the whip. The man went inside to fetch it. The master was impatient and himself rushed into the house for the whip. The poor man who had spoken in a trembling rush saw this, became afraid, and fled.

The master returned with the whip. "Where is that mad dog?" he demanded.

The other servant said that the man had run away.

"You blockhead! Why did you let that dog escape?" the master abused him.

Inside, the mistress called "Raja." Raja was wandering in the backyard and did not come after one call or two. So she called loudly, "Raja! Raja!"

A beautiful little pet dog came running swiftly. In a single bound it leapt upon the mistress reclining on the sofa and nuzzled her. She wiped its face, kissed it, and cooed, "Raja! Where did you go?"

The dog bearing the title "Raja" sat joyfully upon the sofa. The human being given the title "dog" walked in a frightened, hurried stride towards his hut. His name was Namasivayam. The master had taken up a whip to beat a human being.

The mistress gives biscuits to the dog.

"Where does this happen?" you ask.

Why? Is your town a place without poor people? Look around. You can see a human treated like a dog and a dog celebrated as a "king."

You can only look. You cannot ask why. You will feel like asking many questions: "What injustice is this? You abuse a human being as a dog, while fondling a dog as a king and showing it affection. You do not give the man enough food for his stomach, while the mistress gives biscuits to the dog. Can such a perversion be right?"

But you cannot ask. If you do ask, what will you call it? What else? That is revolution.

Treat a human being as human. A person living the life of an animal must be given liberation and a life worthy of the name. You watch him labour like an ox, wander like a dog, and waste away, while you play with dogs and cats. He is human. You turn him into a dog. He is thinking.

May Day is the day that stirs this thought, the day on which people must resolve that human beings shall live as human beings.

## Translator's Notes

The source explicitly identifies the closing reflection with May Day, but its final date reads 17 November 1946. English preserves both rather than inventing a correction. The story's repeated animal labels expose class dehumanisation; they are translated only within that critical structure.

## English Translation

In literature, we encounter and delight in many heroic deeds that reveal the ancient greatness of the Tamils. We praise them, saying, "What courage and ability they possessed!" Just as they were champions of valour, they lived and spread their fame in every field in a manner that allowed one to say, "The Tamils have no equal among other peoples." Yet when we speak of the Tamils' great and dignified life, some ask, "Where is the evidence?" If we point and say, "Look here in the *Silappathikaram*, the *Kurunthogai*, the *Natrinai*, the *Akam*, and the *Puram*," they laugh aloud and say, "Are these not literature? Show it to us in history."

Only then do we look upward in bewilderment and ask, "A history book?" Whichever of the books around us we take up, we discover that it is literature rich in imagination rather than a chronicle saying that a particular event occurred on a particular day of a particular month in a particular year.

We are reduced to silence for want of historical evidence.

When we investigate why our ancestors, who prepared everything for the generations that followed, did not create history, one truth becomes apparent.

There is little flavour in the method of writing, "After this man came that man..." It also requires great effort. One is tempted to think that perhaps they abandoned it for that reason.

Though writing history in this manner is troublesome and less pleasing, today we see that it is immensely useful. Because people in Western countries possess a strong historical consciousness, many different kinds of histories have arisen there.

Someone has gathered details about the kings who ruled England and behaved like buffoons and written a large book called *The History of England's Buffoons*. There is also a volume that examines and describes in detail the story of how every street in London, now one of the world's great cities, developed; the changes undergone by every house; the eating houses patronised by the poets, literary scholars, political leaders, and social workers who lived there; and even the streets along which they habitually walked.

In America, a writer has produced an entire book about the single day on which Abraham Lincoln was shot.

But because no comparable historical book exists in Tamil Nadu, there are people who refuse to believe whatever we say.

Does this mean the ancient Tamils left no written history at all? They did. Abundant fragments of history are available to us here and there within their literature. Those historical fragments reveal not merely the lives of individuals but the life of the Tamil people themselves. Is that sufficient? In present circumstances, it is not.

At least now, therefore, we must undertake the effort to create and preserve our history in a distinct historical form and method. Creating a wealth of historical writing is one of the many means by which Tamil Nadu can advance.

In that respect, multilingual scholar K. Appadurai has rendered an outstanding service to Tamil Nadu.

His book entitled *Battlefields of South India* has taken a first step in the effort to remedy and fill Tamil Nadu's historical deficiency.

The book gathers, organizes, and presents engagingly detailed information about all the wars fought in valour-filled Tamil Nadu, from ancient times through the resistance to British imperialism.

Whether it is a war of the Sangam age, a war fought in the time of the Chalukyas, a battle waged with only spear and sword, or the Vellore struggle against cannon, the book contains an account of it.

By arranging those accounts into a sequence resembling a continuous epic, the book avoids giving us the feeling that we are merely reading reports of battles and instead brings the history of Tamil Nadu before our mind's eye.

In his preface to this heroic history, the author says: "Even today in Tamil Nadu, research into Tamil history, Tamil culture, and Tamil literature remains a field toward which rulers, newspapers, educational institutions, colleges, and universities show no concern and upon which they do not even cast an eye." This condition must change. Only then can new achievements be derived from ancient beginnings.

We must not think of history as some ritual that inscribes what has passed. We must regard it as a worthy effort that guides life. It is history that joins the three times: past, present, and future. If our efforts are to enter that field, many more books like *Battlefields of South India* must appear.

*Battlefields of South India* is not merely a list reporting wars. It is a fine history of the Tamil people.

At the beginning of the book, under four subdivisions—"The Martial Tradition," "The Call of the Heavens," "Connections with the Wider World," and "Connections with the North"—the author delineates the way of life of Tamils who lived in a past beyond the farthest reach of our imagination and establishes their distinctiveness and excellence.

In the subdivisions that follow, he describes every war fought during the period from the Sangam age through resistance to British imperialism.

The author fully understands the great truth that research must be undertaken from a neutral position, without likes or dislikes, and follows that path throughout the book.

The effort to create and use terms such as `அகழ்வாராய்ச்சி` (archaeological excavation), `தளர்ச்சிக் கூறு` (factor of decline), and `வளர்ச்சிக்கூறு` (factor of growth) in various places enriches the language with new vocabulary.

We must also praise the publishers of this fine book.

They have published it on good paper and to a high standard, in a form comparable with editions produced abroad.

It is the duty of Tamils to gather information about the battlefields of nonviolent struggle that constitute the heroic political history of Tamil Nadu from the time of the Marudhu Pandiyars to the present, and thereby inspire the land.

## Translator's Notes

The book title is rendered as *Battlefields of South India*. Names of classical anthologies are transliterated. Four internal section titles are translated conservatively; `வானவிளிப்பு`, particularly, is retained in the terminology ledger because its precise editorial sense should be checked against the book. The final `அறப்போர்க்களங்கள்` refers to arenas of principled or nonviolent political struggle and is distinguished from literal military battlefields.

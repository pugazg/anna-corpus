## English Translation

Such was the awakening! Such were the lessons he studied.

Our king had sat within a golden palace. White men who left their own country and came from town to town to make a living selling soap gradually established their domination. Then they pounced upon our country, dragged the king into a quarrel, made war upon him, defeated and imprisoned him, carried him out of the country, and tormented him in captivity until he died.

This was one of the lessons the young man studied. We can imagine the furious feeling such a lesson must have stirred in his youthful heart.

Ratnagiri Fort lies very far away. It was in that fort, on the Indian landmass, that our king was imprisoned by the English who had come to earn a living through trade.

This too was a lesson he studied. How could it fail to create hatred of the white rulers? He was a young man: a pure mind, a carefree age, and a feeling that refused restraint all had their dwelling in him. The history of his motherland's enslavement sank deep into his mind. Fire arose in his speech and breath.

He read further:

“Our powerful commander skilfully withstood the attack of the white men. They possessed many weapons of modern design. We possessed the courage inherited through generations. Our commander struck. He struck with such force that the invading white men were bewildered. But alas! A ruinous bullet flying from somewhere killed him, and our army scattered. Otherwise, this country would not have fallen into white hands.”

Thus the student's mind became a furnace suited to the liberation struggle. Sparks of revolution flew from his eyes. He assumed the aspect of war while still a student.

Yes, and he died in that same martial aspect. He was murdered.

Thirty-two years old, yet in the position of an uncrowned king. Alas, bullets pierced his whole body. Not while he stood upon a battlefield, but while he conferred with his colleagues on the administration of the country, he was brutally killed.

Aung San appeared like a mighty flash of lightning in Burma, then surrounded by slavery, and disappeared. He died leaving an unforgettable pain that can never pass.

While still at school, Aung San's ability made him a student leader. The strike he conducted then threw the Burmese government into confusion and opened the eyes of the Burmese people. They said: “Here a warrior has appeared, a man who can lead the liberation army and shatter Burma's chains of slavery.”

Burma fell under white domination long after the Indian landmass. The British fought three wars to seize it. The uprisings that followed caused them even greater trouble than the wars. The fire of freedom had been covered but not extinguished; it remained an ember beneath ash. One young man's passionate speech was enough to turn it once more into flame.

Aung San entered law college. Yet his commitment to national liberation left him without the disposition to continue his studies. Whenever he opened his books, the country's decline appeared before his eyes. How, then, could he study, obtain a degree, enter some occupation and merely carry the burden of a family?

He refused to remain a caged parrot. Like a sailor who trusts that a ship without a mast, though driven wherever the waves strike, may somehow reach harbour in the end, Aung San resolved to explore every available path until he found a way to Burma's liberation.

Burma called. Could a school confine him in such a condition? The bars parted; the parrot flew, though the hawk continued to pursue it.

The Burmese liberation struggle assumed a form found in Western lands. It did not take the governmental and constitutional form seen here in our subcontinent.

The Burmese believed that the rulers who came carrying rifles to attack them must be attacked and driven away with whatever weapons were available. This is why the life stories of Burma's freedom fighters make one's hair stand on end.

Determined to begin a liberation war, Aung San left school and went to receive military training.

At that time, Japanese agents driven by the ambition to rule Asia had established “camps” in Burma, Malaya and elsewhere. They moved about in such disguises as dentists, fruit sellers, medicine vendors and hotelkeepers, provoking internal disturbances. Aung San obtained military training through them at Hainan, believing it necessary for national liberation.

The World War began, and Aung San received his opportunity. He held the foremost position among the thirty young men who guided the Japanese army advancing towards Burma.

The Japanese assessed Aung San merely as a boy who would run ahead of them. They danced without knowing head from foot. Only later did they learn that their estimate was wrong. They imagined they had found a sturdy bull and could use Aung San to deceive the Burmese people.

The British troops retreated, and Aung San rejoiced. “See it run—the British army that ruled my country until now, the force that ruined Burma,” he thought with delight.

Then he saw a Japanese man patting his back and saying, “Younger brother, we admire your courage and heroism. From now on we shall unite and rule this country together.” A thought arose: “The old curse has departed. But this newcomer...?”

How could Aung San's mind be satisfied merely because the British had withdrawn? Seeing Japanese arrogance increase, his heart seethed. Convinced that Burma had fallen into their grip, the Japanese began their repression. Aung San's blood boiled.

Before, he had been a heroic youth. Now he was the commander of a heroic army. In the Burmese ministry established under Japanese supervision, Aung San was military chief. He possessed a great trained and armed force, ready to rush wherever his pointing finger directed.

Aung San smiled. A new plan was ready. He joined the British, drove out the Japanese, and removed the new danger confronting Burma.

Had Aung San been merely an isolated individual, the British might have seated him somewhere after conferring a knighthood. But what did they see? Aung San arrived at the head of a parade of many thousands of soldiers. Several million Burmese were prepared to accept his word as law.

The white rulers knew the art of bending very well. Here in India they placed Shah Nawaz and Sehgal in the dock and tried them for performing the very kind of work for which they now welcomed Aung San into Government House. Lord Mountbatten, now Governor-General of Hindustan, made an agreement with him and recognised his army as the Burmese National Army.

The freedom fighter Aung San became Burma's peerless leader. Thereafter, each of his efforts brought astonishing success. If he called a strike, all Burma obeyed. His army gathered, while his judgement and administrative skill grew.

The British recognised that their hold could no longer endure. They granted the Burmese people a plan to establish a constituent assembly empowered to prepare a charter of independence. The constituent assembly was in session when, alas, shameless criminals dared to drink the life of the man who had given it life.

U Saw, arrested on suspicion, his companions, and Ba Maw, also named in the article as a suspect, were all older than Aung San. They had planted their feet more deeply in the mire of politics and had themselves suffered imprisonment and exile. They had been leaders when Aung San was still a youth whose moustache had scarcely darkened. His lightning rise kindled jealousy in their minds.

U Saw had been arrested during the war and later released by the British. If Burma's liberation alone had been his aim upon returning, he need not have reassembled his former party. It became openly apparent that he sought to destroy Aung San's influence.

When Britain prepared its plan for Burmese independence, Aung San accepted it, while U Saw and Ba Maw refused. Did those who rejected it attempt to gather the people behind them? They did not. They did not even appear in the constituent assembly election. Why would they? Their plan had become something else.

While the victorious Aung San and his ministers conferred in triumph, the murderers poured down a rain of bullets and killed Burma's incomparable warrior. The article declares that no event elsewhere equalled the horror of the Burmese massacre, and that even the cruel Tsar had not committed murder in this manner. No one failed to shed tears for Aung San's tragic end.

His wife reportedly said: “My only work in life now is to raise these three children and bear my sorrow.” For her, she said, the whole world had gone dark.

Yes. Aung San's party and the law will surely eliminate the band that betrayed the man who offered himself to Burma's liberation. Aung San will become a symbol in the pages of Burmese history, and indeed in the history of the world.

Yet whatever may be done, when his wife thinks of the end of the husband who feared no prison, led an army and obtained Burma's liberation within a few years, will her eyes not become pools of tears? That lady became the embodiment of a grief that cannot be wiped away.

*Dravida Nadu*, 27 July 1947.

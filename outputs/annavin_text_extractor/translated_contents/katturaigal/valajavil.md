வடார்க்காடு மாவட்டத்தைச் சேர்ந்த வாலாஜாபேட்டைக்கு வந்தார்.

அங்கு அவர் முனிசிபல் வாசகசாலையைத் திறந்து வைக்கவே
அழைக்கப்பட்டிருந்தார், நகரசபையாரால். அந்த அழைப்பை ஏற்று அவர்
வருகிறார் என்றவுடன், தி.மு.கழகத்தினர் கூடினர்; சிந்தித்தனர்.

மக்களுக்குக் கொடுத்த வாக்குறுதிகளைக் காற்றிலே பறக்கவிட்டு
விட்டு, மந்திரியாகிவிட்டார் மாணிக்கவேலர் - இது மக்களுக்குச் செய்யும்
மாபெரும் துரோகம் என்பதைக் காட்ட வேண்டும் என்று தீர்மானித்தனர்
பொதுச் செயலாளரின் அனுமதியும், ஆலோசனையும் கோரினர்.

பிறகு பொதுக்கூட்டம் நடத்தவும் முடிவு செய்யப்பட்டது.

செயற்குழு முடிந்தது. சுழற்காற்றெனத் தோழர்கள் சுற்றிச் சுற்றிப்

ஆளுக்கொரு பணியேற்று ஓயாதுழைத்தனர். ஒருவர் கறுப்புத் துணிகளை

## English Translation

Achariyar's intrigue exposed

Seven movement comrades arrested

Lathi charge, prison and prosecution

On the evening of Sunday, 27 April 1952, Minister Manickavelar came to Walajapet in North Arcot district.

The municipal council had invited him to open the municipal reading room. As soon as it became known that he had accepted the invitation and was coming, members of the DMK assembled and deliberated.

They resolved that the people must be shown the enormity of Manickavelar's betrayal: he had cast to the winds the promises he had made them and become a minister. They sought the General Secretary's permission and counsel.

At an emergency meeting of the Walajapet DMK executive held at three in the afternoon on Saturday, 26 April, in the presence of comrade Thillai Villalan, it was decided to show Manickavelar black flags. It was also decided to hold a public meeting after the black-flag demonstration.

The executive meeting ended. The comrades worked everywhere like a whirlwind. Every young man of the movement in Walajapet took up a task and laboured without rest. One brought black cloth. Another brought old cloth, dyed it black and hung it to dry. A third kept sewing black flags. One comrade wrote notices on placards. We cannot forget the great effort with which the comrades divided the work among themselves and properly completed the tasks each had accepted.

The movement's office in Walajapet was only a tiny, colourful hut. That day the hut became a fortress of heroes.

Like warriors coming to a camp to receive their weapons, comrades arrived continually to seek instructions.

By five that evening the news had reached comrades in nearby Ranipet, Arcot and elsewhere. They began coming in person to learn the details of the event planned for the following day.

Night arrived. Only then were the posters ready. The comrades immediately took a share each and pasted them so that they were everywhere. At dawn, the people of Walajapet awoke to the faces of those posters.

Some were astonished, some angry and many delighted when they learned of the black-flag demonstration. The movement's young men, ready for anything, worked at full strength on preparations to welcome the minister with black flags that evening.

At the municipality, people inspected whether the garland prepared for the minister was in order. At the very same time, in that little hut, others counted the black flags, checked how many pasted notices had been torn down, and asked whether more notices had been put up in all those places. Meanwhile, a lorry carrying the Reserve Police came to a halt at the police station.

"Where is that movement secretary? Bring him here," ordered a police officer. Constables scattered in every direction. Unable to find the comrades, they returned exhausted.

The minister was to arrive in the evening. In the morning a proclamation was beaten through the streets. Did it announce, "The minister is coming; bring jasmine garlands"? No. It declared: "Do not assemble. Anyone who remains in defiance will be committing an offence under the law and will be punished."

Townspeople who did not know the details awakened to what was happening; those who understood smiled knowingly and mocked the crooked intelligence of the rulers. There was scarcely a mouth in Walajapet that day that did not ask what disgrace it was to forbid people from walking outside because a minister was coming. Ministers exist for the people; the people do not exist for ministers. There was no thinking person there who failed to ask who benefited by hiding a minister from the people's eyes.

The government had imposed a ban. When this news reached the comrades' ears, a proud vigour appeared in their stride. They possessed hearts of steel; naturally theirs was the gait of lions. Those who knew the character of movement comrades could not but praise them.

The moment the minister announced his visit came an order meant to intimidate the people. Then troops arrived to drive them away.

What could a ban and an armed force do to young men prepared to accept any loss or hardship for their ideal? They therefore stood on both sides of the Walajapet trunk road for a mile, flags in hand, like an army drawn up in formation. Each bore a flag and waited, determined to show the minister the betrayal he had committed against the people.

Although the police formed a "Chakravyuha," placing one man every three yards, they could not conceal the black flags.

The minister arrived at half past five.

Everywhere rose the cry, "Go back! Go back!" and its echo. The thunder of "Manickavelar, betrayer of the people, go back!" entered the minister's ears and bored into his mind. That was why his face first contracted, then darkened, and finally reddened.

The people's voice did not stop at demanding that the minister go back. They shouted, "Resign your ministerial office!" and "Down with Achariyar's intrigue!" Minister Manickavelar was shaken to see black flags spread like a sea. He had to travel floating through that dark ocean.

Even as he went to the speaking platform, comrades approached to place black flags in his own hand.

There too they raised slogans that made the path of his betrayal unmistakably clear.

Though the troops protected him, their cries tortured him.

The minister poured out many words in anger. But the people would not let his speech be heard. They continued to roar, "Go back!"

When the meeting ended and word spread that he was returning to Madras, they waited with black flags along that road as well. On seeing his car, they displayed the flags and expressed their contempt.

The police did not fail to display their own "skill." That evening they arrested comrades K. D. S. Mani and T. Sabapathi. Later they detained and severely mistreated two comrades, Chinnappan and Kachapalayam. At several places they also used their lathis, thus pretending to have performed their duty.

At eight that night, despite the prohibition order and in defiance of it, a DMK public meeting was held. Three comrades, Anbezhilan, Iraniyadasan and Thagarathinam, were arrested.

Every movement comrade worked tremendously to make the black-flag demonstration a success. Arcot Ponnambalam and his comrades; friends from Ranipet; Kanchipuram Ethiraj, Sambandam and his comrades; Chengalpattu district secretary comrade C. V. M. Annamalai; Walajapet Varadarasan and Munusami; and Dravidar Kazhagam comrade Ilanguppan all conducted the black-flag reception splendidly. Thillai Villalan had come from headquarters to participate.

What took place that day at Walajapet was a black-flag demonstration that placed Minister Manickavelar before the people's court and delivered its verdict. We commend and wholeheartedly congratulate everyone who made the minister understand the people's judgement.

(*Dravida Nadu*, 4 May 1952)

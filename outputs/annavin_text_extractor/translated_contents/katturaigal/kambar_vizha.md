## English Translation

“Wha... what... is that, da? Hey... who are you talking to? I’m your father, da... hey!”

How utterly degraded! The drunk picks a needless quarrel and abuses as he pleases. The curves in his body exceed those in his speech. Though unable to rise if he falls, he announces himself ready for heroic battle. And the figure he imagines has challenged him is merely a lamp-post!

His sight and speech both malfunction. Why?

A celebration! A festival!

Is this a festival? Instead of saying that some ill-natured fellow is staggering drunk, you call it a celebration and a festival. How strange! At a festival, should not faces and hearts blossom as family, relatives, and friends exchange loving words and mingle happily? Some drunk takes a coconut or palmyra tree as his “companion” and lies measuring the street; how can you say he celebrates a festival? Is this the conduct of cultured people? The country’s decay dances before us.

If drunken antics are called the product of a festival, everyone seeking good character will laugh as I have done. Yet festivals are repeatedly turned into frenzied revelry. Whatever name a festival bears, this performance appears. The unconcerned laugh. Those who see in it a mirror of the nation feel shame and sorrow. We sigh and admonish: “See the revelry of drink! See how the drinker wanders stripped of humanity! Understand that liquor destroys reason! Alas, the country has reached this state.”

A festival is a day when joy flowers. For some, all life is a festival. For many others, every festival becomes frenzy. For the majority, it resembles lightning that tears through darkness and vanishes. “We suffer always. Today alone let every hardship be wiped away, or at least forgotten, and let us be happy. Tomorrow our hereditary property, sorrow, will still await us. Let today alone be a sacred day. We shall wear the best we possess, prepare and eat what is available, forget even past wrongs, renew friendship, rejoice ourselves, and wish the same joy for everyone.” A festival is a holiday from endless trouble, one day of rest for the ceaselessly harried mind. For those whose lives are filled with pain, it can mean only this.

In such circumstances, how distressing it is to see unstable people consume intoxicants and create useless spectacles at a festival. One naturally asks why the country has sunk so low and whether our ancestors behaved like this.

Artists loudly tell us that the culture of ancient Tamils may be seen in Kambar’s *Ramayanam*. Poets portray in their works either what existed or what they believe ought to exist. They may observe the present and write, “It is like this, but this is not good; it should be otherwise.” Such works are praised as golden books because they enable people to understand ideas capable of saving a nation.

Ask anyone what a festival should be, and they will say it should not be the frenzy demonstrated by the wastrel rolling in the street. What was Kambar’s view? How did he wish a festival to be? That is the subject of this essay.

Art lovers may say, “Kambar wrote about Kakutstha; he did not compose an explanatory treatise on festivals.” I know. Yet his “sacred book” contains details of the daily lives of kings of kings. It is not merely a record of the war between righteousness and unrighteousness. He photographs life, from the monkey’s play to the peacock’s dance. Therefore his book must contain what happened during festivals. He would either record what occurred or portray what he thought ought to have occurred. Either would serve our inquiry.

A festival is a joyful day. Among joyful occasions, none equals a wedding. Even the wedding day itself brings burdensome work, but delight wells up when the marriage is confirmed, when preparations begin, and above all when people set out to witness it. They acquire a festival mood. We see it even today: trunks and boxes brought out, clothes and ornaments arranged, the elder brother called, the younger searched for, jasmine given to the wife, the son embraced, laughter and adornment, coins tossed about and notes folded. How many kinds of delight fill a family setting out for a wedding?

Yet we also hear conversations like this:

“Sir, that fellow came drunk, grabbed Kuppi by the hand, and sang something incoherently.”

“Where?”

“At Villupuram Junction. We had to change trains. In the interval the fellow went to the shop and came back after drinking.”

“What a rogue!”

“He never thought what people would say if he drank and danced like this while going to a wedding.”

“Did he really? Why not call his elder brother and tell him?”

“As though you do not know that spectacle! His brother took Akilandam with him and went to the bazaar.”

“He too?”

“If I recount everything, my stomach will burn. I regret going with them.”

Even this disgusts sensible people. How much greater would their anger be if the report were that not only men but women drank, rioted, performed erotic dances, lost control, and did all manner of things? We would condemn it as the conduct of degraded people.

Very well. Kambar now invites us to witness a wedding. Let us go.

It is no ordinary marriage. Rama, who broke the bow, is marrying Sita of teeth like jasmine buds: the princess of Mithila weds the prince of Kosala. It is a royal wedding, not one in an ordinary family. The company setting out includes Emperor Dasaratha and his retinue, minor kings, fish-eyed women, musicians, dancers, golden vines, flowering vines, and many other “varieties.” Kambar enters this company and shows us its scenes.

Let us see a few. Through Kambar’s hand-camera, let us discover what this joy-filled festival was like. Is he not a splendid cameraman, unequalled in close-ups? But does his close-up show a swaying mango? No. It shows something else resembling one!

To witness the incomparable sight of Sriman Narayana incarnated as Rama marrying Sita, incarnation of Mahalakshmi, Emperor Dasaratha departs surrounded by those among Ayodhya’s people favoured by the court. In the *Bala Kandam* of the *Kamba Ramayanam*, we now enter the chapter called *Undattu Padalam*.

*Undattu Padalam*: what a title! Eating, drinking, and dancing. What do they consume that makes them dance? Who consumes it and dances?

Delighted by King Janaka’s message that Rama’s wedding would occur in Mithila, Dasaratha announced the joyful news to the town, as though saying, “May the world receive the bliss I have received,” and set out for Mithila surrounded by the four divisions of his army. Did he take only those forces? Would Kambar permit that? An army of smiling faces also departed. Would Kambar, who drinks deeply of inner love poetry, leave the beautiful women of Ayodhya yearning while the men travelled? They too came.

Beginning with the chapter on departure, proceeding through chapters on landscapes, flower gathering, and water play, Kambar lets men and women sport in the evening, flower gardens, and pools, delighting himself and his connoisseurs. Then the poet who came to write a sacred work reaches the *Undattu Padalam*: he tells how they drank liquor and revelled.

You may have heard people say:

“Sir, he drinks a little now and then, only high-quality stuff.”

“However much the king drinks, he never says even one confused word.”

“That fellow drinks toddy pot after pot and rolls in the street without even knowing his clothes have come off. It is horrible to see.”

The supposedly sacred book is said to bring comfort in this life and residence in heaven in the next merely by being read or heard. The presence within it of a section about drinking and dancing is itself obscene. Could those revellers not at least have been travellers or forest dwellers?

Kambar might have written that common people, delighted at the news of Rama’s wedding, drank and danced; that Dasaratha, passing on his way to the wedding, advised them, “Liquor destroys reason. Is drink needed for joy? This is not the good path”; that they overcame intoxication, bowed to the king, and begged forgiveness; and that he blessed them, saying, “You acted with deficient understanding, but once you recognized your fault, you agreed to reform. You now possess mature understanding. Live long.” Readers might then learn both that liquor ruins the mind and that a king should reform his people.

What did the great poet do instead? He says the drunken revellers were the sacred people accompanying Dasaratha. If they behaved so, what condition must mountain and forest dwellers have been in? The thought confounds the mind.

Having identified those honoured by the king of kings as drunkards, did he stop there? He might have said they drank, became dazed, and collapsed; that the moon shone as though laughing at their impaired reason; that the sun struck them with its rays in anger, saying, “If even these people behave so, what condition will the unlearned reach?”; that they rose rubbing their eyes as if seeking forgiveness; that, ashamed to witness God’s wedding with liquor upon their breath, they bathed, rinsed their mouths, and completed their morning duties. But Kambar instead gives close-up after close-up.

Look!

They poured fresh liquor into beautiful golden bowls and began to drink.

Who?

Women!

What misfortune! Who were these women drinking liquor? Women whose flower-adorned hair spread fragrance.

That alone is disgusting enough. Women of royal households, travelling to Raghava’s wedding, drink at a stopping place on the road! Was it necessary, in a book meant to explain divine virtue, to write of women drinking? Was this the state of women in Ayodhya, abode of every virtue, when the Lord was incarnate? Did they lie drunk by the roadside?

Devotees, poets seeking culture, honourable admirers of artistic flavour, noble comrades campaigning for prohibition: the women of Ayodhya reportedly lived like this in the Lord’s age. Kambar says so. Is it right? Is it something of which to be proud, evidence of ancient glory, the mark of a sacred work? Think patiently.

The women drank liquor, not honey. Kambar did not stop there. He explains why. He leads his connoisseurs to the summit of obscenity.

The women did not drink without reason. They sought one pleasure in order to enjoy another. They did not regard liquor as an end in itself but as an aid to something else. The liquor poured into golden bowls was an entrance ticket to the land of sensual pleasure. Have you heard the explanation?

Kambar does not say the women, delighted by the Lord’s wedding, mistook liquor for milk. He says they drank so they might delight in the battle of sexual union. What experienced operators! How much practice and training must be required to use this method even while travelling!

Kambar says those ornamented women poured liquor into golden bowls and drank, intending to enjoy the battle of love upon beds thickly strewn with flowers. Is this a sacred story or an epic campaigning against prohibition? Thoughtful people place women who drink and riot among the degraded. Where would they place women who drink because they believe it will increase skill in erotic play? Where would they find them? Kambar assigns this “quality” to the women of Ayodhya.

“Alamu, take one drink.”

“No... sister... enough.”

“Shame, foolish girl! Drink it. The body will be very tired and ruined otherwise. Here, drink. Quickly. Ah, someone seems to be coming.”

Where can such talk be heard? The ugly claim that drink is useful and improves skill in erotic play might be heard among fallen women and wicked men in brothels of a town without laws against prostitution.

Kambar not only places women who drank for delight in erotic play within Rama’s Ayodhya; he composes chapter after chapter about the excellence of their journey to the Lord’s wedding.

Devotees, if not art connoisseurs, may angrily say, “Do not speak too much. Kambar never wrote anything like this.” I must also face the connoisseurs’ anger, though for another reason. They may think: “Kambar beautifully blends flower-beds, women, wine, erotic battle, and delight into a cocktail, but this madman, apparently incapable of appreciating it, grows angry.” Both groups will blame me. But am I the culprit? Here is verse six of the *Undattu Padalam*. See for yourselves:

> Women whose hair breathed floral fragrance,  
> Their thoughts fixed on the proud delight born  
> In the battle enacted on flower-swollen beds,  
> Drank fresh liquor poured into lovely golden bowls  
> As though it were nectar made by the gods.

`பூக்கமழ் ஓதியர்`: women with hair fragrant with flowers.

`போது பொங்கிய சேக்கையின்`: upon beds densely spread with flowers.

`விளை செரு`: the battle of sexual union enacted there.

`செருக்கும் சிந்தையார்`: desiring to experience its delight.

`ஆக்கிய அமுது என`: like nectar made by the gods.

`அம்பொன் வள்ளத்து`: in beautiful golden bowls.

`வாக்கிய பசுநறை`: fresh liquor poured there.

`மாந்தல் மேயினார்`: they began to drink.

Art connoisseurs delight in drinking Kambar’s flavour even more than the women of Ayodhya delighted. Let them. But those who desire good character in the country should consider: is this a sacred story? Is this the way to rejoice? Is this festival conduct? If women behaved so in the holy golden age of Raghava, was it an age of culture?

People say, “Though Kambar wrote a northern story, he blended Tamil Nadu into it. He shows the Ganga as the Kaveri and displays Tamil culture.” Tamil culture! See its quality: the “skilful” women who seek liquor as an aid to erotic battle are Tamils. What hardness of heart is needed to say this, and what indifference to hear it!

To those who argue that even if they drank liquor then, it was of a different kind, I say: Kambar unfolds verse after verse showing the women of Ayodhya committing every distorted antic caused by drink that we see today.

Verse six says they drank; verse seven says the same; verse eight describes reddened eyes; verse nine notes lust inflamed by liquor. Then comes their loss of control, their babbling, and their obscene antics. Thus, in sixty-six verses of the *Undattu Padalam*, the man who came to write a sacred story about the relation between the individual soul and the Supreme explains drunken frenzy.

Today we hear people say:

“Hey, the fellow is very drunk. Look, he sees his own shadow, thinks it another man, and speaks to it!”

“Look, he does not even know the glass is empty. He picks it up thinking there is still beer. He is over the limit!”

In the same manner, Kambar composed many verses about the “excellence” of those great ladies.

People now say of a drunk: “In his frenzy he wore upon his head what belongs around the waist and put around his neck what should be on his feet. He looked horrible.” Every sensible person says such degradation must end.

Read verse sixteen and the uproar made by these “virtuous women” in drunken frenzy is enough to make us vomit. It was sung by Kambanattalvar, not some insignificant scribbler. Would you like to see the verse? You lack the good fortune to see the women themselves. Even if you had seen them, there would have been danger!

Liquor had risen to one woman’s head. She no longer knew where ornaments belonged. She took the ornament from her waist and placed a flower garland there, then took a golden ornament and began fastening it in her hair. Many such displays occurred:

> A woman whose slender waist swayed like lightning,  
> Her sweet speech faltering like pale nectar,  
> Removed the coloured girdle from her intimate waist,  
> Wound a flower garland there, and with a golden ari-chain  
> Began adorning her curling hair.

Consider the meaning:

`மின்னென நுடங்குகின்ற`: moving like lightning.

`மருங்குலாள் ஒருத்தி`: a woman possessing a waist.

`வெள்ளை இன் அமிழ்து அனைய இன்சொல்`: sweet speech like pale, delicious nectar.

`இடை தடுமாறி`: faltering midway.

`அல்குல் வண்ண மேகலையை நீக்கி`: removing the coloured girdle worn near her private parts.

`மலர்த்தொடை அல்குல் சூழ்ந்தாள்`: winding the flower garland around that place.

`பொன்னரி மாலை கொண்டு`: taking the ornament called the golden ari-chain.

`புரிகுழல் புனையலுற்றாள்`: beginning to place it upon her curling hair.

The boundary of obscenity! We have no further business there. Danger! Turn back and begin to think. Is this a sacred story, Tamil culture, and enjoyment of epic art? Is this what the Kambar festival celebrates?

(*Dravida Nadu*, 12-1-1947)

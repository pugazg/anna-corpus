## English Translation

After the General Council meeting ended on 14 December 1952, a great public meeting was held on behalf of the Thanjavur DMK under the chairmanship of Comrade S. Bethanna. The public gathered in numbers Thanjavur had never before witnessed at this meeting honouring the newly elected General Secretary. Expressing gratitude for the proud reception accorded to him, Comrade Annadurai said:

“Out of affection for me, you have adorned me here with garlands of flowers and garlands of kind words. I offer you my gratitude. I shall not regard the flower garlands you have given here as having been given to me; I shall regard them as given to the foremost worker of the movement we are nurturing.

“The younger brothers who spoke before me mentioned Nehru, who left after calling this spectacle ‘nonsense.’ He ought to have seen it. Ours is a very ordinary movement, according to ‘them,’ and I am a very ordinary worker in that very ordinary movement. Pandit Nehru, who flung the word ‘nonsense’ at the love you show me, ought to see you gathered here in your tens of thousands. I look as far as the eye can reach, and everywhere I see only heads.

“Why have you assembled in such numbers? You have come from a desire to know what the members of this Kazhagam will say and what we think about the country's decline. Is this ‘nonsense’? If hundreds of thousands gather to understand a political party's principles, is that to be judged ‘nonsense’? Are these flower garlands nonsense? Are the people gathered here nonsense? No, no. This is nationalism! Consciousness of one's people! An immense yearning for liberation! Yet Pandit Nehru calls it ‘Childish, Nonsense.’

“He has not seen these immense gatherings and does not know the extent of the consciousness growing among us. The younger brothers who spoke before me explained with historical evidence what fate befell all those who called things ‘nonsense,’ and condemned them sharply.

“I considered instead what made Nehru utter such a word. He had regarded us as a very ordinary movement. He belongs to a circle that considered even speaking about us beneath its status. Today such a mind sees us and says ‘nonsense.’ Rather than grow angry because he said it, I wish to point out that our problem has grown enough to make him say it—enough to spill into his thoughts.

“The Pandit is a very great man who did not even wish to think about us. He has now been brought to the point of saying ‘nonsense.’ He has never seen great gatherings like this and does not know our growth. When he visits, breakfast is in Mylapore, lunch in Triplicane and dinner in an Adyar establishment. Would the people there tell him the truth about us? Even if they mentioned us, they would end by saying, ‘It is some nonsense.’

“I read in the newspapers that on the morning of the day when Pandit Nehru flung the heated phrase ‘Childish, Nonsense’ at the evening meeting, he visited the Bala Mandir. I also hear that he frequently spends time fondling children nowadays. It seems the sight of children singing and dancing at the Bala Mandir had not faded from his mind. That is why he uttered this phrase in the manner of a child. He is a very great man, but has spilled out a very ordinary expression. Let us forgive him.

“Our Kazhagam is a three-year-old child. Just as it has now daubed tar upon the face of ‘Hindi,’ when this child grows it will not hesitate to smear soot upon the faces of the Marwaris. What will the Pandit call it then? Perhaps ‘Boyish Frank’! When it grows further into a young man, we shall make him look upon its deeds and call it a ‘youthful - emcumbrance’—a youthful nuisance.

“I do not think the Pandit who called us ‘nonsense’ has forgotten. If he has, I remind him: rulers have always used language like this upon seeing a growing desire for liberation. The Pandit, moreover, has reached the age of greying beard and walking stick. He must not forget. ‘Oldman's Wisdom’ will be of no use! Yet this great man has flung a very ordinary expression. A very great person; a very ordinary word. Let us forgive and forget.

“We shall not remain children forever. Before his own eyes we shall grow into boys and young men. We must prepare ourselves for that growth. Every branch of the Kazhagam must be a fortress of steel. There must not be a single hamlet or village without a branch. Wherever one goes, either the DMK flag or the Dravidar Kazhagam flag must fly. Let that condition arise. Give it six months; after that, I shall make him recognise it for himself.

“Why does he fail to respect us now that we have grown into a great movement? He is prevented from learning about us. A blackout is imposed. The Vindhyas stand across the way, preventing knowledge of our problems. Beyond them, many know nothing about us.

“We need not feel wounded by this. What is our position now compared with before? We have grown today beyond yesterday; tomorrow we shall grow beyond today. Those who saw us yesterday find themselves compelled to say something when they see us today. Their immeasurable arrogance forces them to vomit something.

“I use the word ‘vomit’ in its proper sense. What cannot be digested is vomited. They cannot digest matters concerning us, and so they vomit. How could they digest them? Would Mylapore and Triplicane describe us to the Pandit as a ‘great movement’? Even if he asked, ‘What is this?’ would they not answer that it was some ‘nonsense’? How could our cause be digested there? That is why I do not say he spoke; I say he vomited.

“We are very, very ordinary people, but our growth is not ordinary. Bullock-cart drivers, small shopkeepers, spirited college students, heroic farmers and workers have all gathered. I challenge Pandit Nehru's spies: can they deny it?

“These people were not brought together by singing devotional hymns. We are not glittering millionaires. If we decide to raise twenty-five thousand rupees for cyclone relief, we must stage plays in ten towns. Do the taunts others fling while we put on theatrical make-up not pierce our hearts? They do, but we remain committed and work.

“Are we people who can cry ‘Here!’ and throw open a treasury? Are we people who point to the Lord of All and claim, ‘This is the cure for every disease’? Do we preach upon the relationship between the Supreme Soul and the individual soul? We have no financial power, no newspaper power and no power of divine grace. We are exceedingly ordinary. Why, then, does our Kazhagam grow? Why does it shine as a beautiful painting?

“This very gathering forms that beautiful painting, but Nehru has no time to see it. Our enemies' degrading words have never made us wither; they have only made us grow. From our earliest days, who has not cursed us? How many have reviled us? How many called us insolent? Thousands said, ‘Just watch—they are going to disappear.’ Their prophecy proved false. We have not disappeared.

“What is the reason? We possess the vital force to withstand our enemies' arrows, resist and grow. Has history seen another movement grow while overcoming so much opposition? In Periyar's words, ‘the diamond-hard body split into two,’ but does it not now display greater strength and distinction than before?

“Those who asked, ‘Do you know what will happen if it splits in two?’—I call upon you now. Can you deny its present splendour and strength? Can you show from history another movement that divided in this way and grew? What is the secret? Think about it. A people has become conscious of itself. This truth and this vital force must be understood.

“A ship upon the sea. A whirling cyclone. A school of sharks advancing with open mouths. Sharks, cyclone, raging storm, turbulent sea and a ship in distress. The captain lies bound upon the deck, a black cloth over his eyes and his hands and feet tied. The vessel pitches and reels. Those on deck, seeing the danger, fear what may happen in half a second.

“The captain senses the peril. He bites through his bonds, frees his hands, removes the covering from his eyes and looks upon the sea. The ship is in agony and the sea calls. Water enters through a breach on one side. Sea, vessel, pitching and waves—and the captain aboard the leaking ship! He looks ahead and cries: ‘Alas, why am I no longer blindfolded? How can I bear to see this horror?’ He laments, looks pityingly upon those around him, and collapses beneath unbearable grief.

“Periyar removed the ‘black cloth’ tied across our eyes. We looked ahead and saw danger and cruelty. We raised the collapsed captain and cried, ‘Come, let us set out for the shore!’ We urged him, ‘Set forth; the ship is ready!’ There was no response.

“In distress, we climbed upon a catamaran. Stretching both hands toward those aboard the ship, we lovingly call, ‘The whirlpool will smash it into a hundred pieces. Come, come! Somehow we can reach the shore.’ This is the DMK's work.

“From the catamaran we look toward the shore, where you—honourable people—extend your hands. Not merely you, not the Communist Party or Congress: the government must understand this scene. Only then will the meaning of the cry ‘The Dravidian land for the Dravidians’ become clear. That cry is the philosophy of government by a people.

“It is an exalted ideal. Corpses may fall and families may be destroyed in attaining it. But the force that has set out for it will not hesitate to overcome obstacles, give its life and recover its homeland. We must strengthen the ranks that declare: ‘If a river of blood is required, we are ready to provide it. If we must walk through a field of corpses, we are ready for that too.’

“Sweat and labour are not enough. Merely baring our chests to the spear of repression is not enough. There must be regiment after regiment. As soon as the call comes, after shedding a single drop of tears, wave after wave must set forth in ever greater numbers.

“Every branch must become a camp prepared for such a righteous struggle and stand as a fortress. Each person must march as a warrior; everyone must become a commander. How many are ready?”

The whole gathering raised its hands and cried, “Ready! Ready!”

“I am delighted. There are no swords in these hands, just as there is no deceit in these minds. Is this childishness? No, no! A very great man, not understanding it, uttered a very ordinary expression. Let us forget; let us forgive.

“We are not Vadapathimangalam, Dharmapuram or Thiruvavaduthurai. We have no seven-storey mansions. The ground itself is our jewelled carpet, and our hands are our pillows. Yet we grow. Such is the vital force of our principle, and the strength of the force supporting it increases. Let us nurture it. Let us live!”

*Dravida Nadu*, 21 December 1952

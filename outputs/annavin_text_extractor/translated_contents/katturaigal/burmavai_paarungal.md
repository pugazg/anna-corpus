## English Translation

Burma has today obtained political freedom. There is a difference between its condition under the grip of British imperialism and its condition today. A new stage has arisen, not in deterioration but in progress.

Had that country's intellectuals worked to chart a sound path towards the welfare of the Burmese people, there would have been no room for the internal disorder now prevailing in Burma. Murder, robbery, and similar acts have taken first place in its political life only because every national leader directs his effort towards securing all power for himself. If both the right and the left acted with the people's welfare foremost, the Burmese people need never have suffered their present wretched life.

The Burmese people are mere pleasure seekers, a people disposed to regard idleness itself as happiness. Women, drink, gambling, intoxicants, and the like are what most Burmese desire.

Nor has Burma's history during the past 120 years been distinguished. Burma has steadily declined amid perpetual disputes and conflict. In 1826, Arakan and Tenasserim were annexed to India. In 1885, the Burmese king Thibaw was driven from the country, and Burma was made part of the British Empire.

Rice, teak, and oil are the principal products found there. The country became an excellent hunting ground for white exploitation. Industry fell into British hands; the Chinese captured commerce. The Chettiars, who conducted moneylending, later became Burma's landed proprietors.

Anyone can imagine the resentment this condition must have produced in the Burmese person living in Burma. The white man controlled industry, the Chinese commerce, and Tamils owned the land. In the country where they were born and raised, the Burmese became labourers and attendants to foreigners. Would anyone possessing even a mustard seed of self-respect accept this? Would not those who valued self-respect and believed in a good life come forward to smash this condition, this degraded existence, to pieces?

Even so, the Burmese slept for a very long time. They tolerated others prospering through their labour, in the very land where they themselves were born, grew, and died, while they, the workers, remained deprived of life. How long could this comfortable existence for others continue without causing any difficulty for the exploiters? It too reached an end.

Fifteen or twenty years ago, some educated people, including some who had visited many Western countries and learned about the prosperity and systems of government found there, refused to continue living in accord with British imperialism. They began to speak against it. They explained to the world how foreigners, including the English, had ruined their country's wealth. Beyond that, they could do little. The very people on whose behalf they spoke lacked the strength of mind that would have enabled the leaders to continue acting.

The Englishman's embrace, which draws opponents close, together with the snares of titles, offices, and affection, confined Burmese leaders in the freedom struggle to the work of throwing words.

For rebelling against imperialism, the late Aung San, the present Prime Minister Thakin Nu, and several other prominent men became guests of the government in prison in 1938. There they obtained the opportunity to learn about communist doctrines and the course of China's internal conflict. The heroic deeds of the leader of the Chinese Communist Party became deeply imprinted upon their minds. His books too were smuggled into the prison.

Then came the Japanese invasion. Aung San and his comrades joined the Japanese and undertook the task of freeing Burma from the imperial grip. At that moment, the communists, rejecting Aung San's course, united with the British. Weapons of war were supplied liberally to the communists.

It was then that the Burmese people awoke and came to understand their true condition. Whether through full understanding or not, most Burmese people now appear to belong to the left.

After Japan was defeated, Burma obtained national freedom. Aung San, leader of the Anti-Fascist party, assumed office as Prime Minister. A fire of jealousy arose in the hearts of former political performers: "Can a mere boy who appeared only yesterday possess such influence? Is he to sit upon the seat of government? Are 'honourable men' like us to submit to his command?"

They conspired. Aung San and his comrades were shot dead. Within seconds their lives departed, and their bodies sank into a mire of blood.

Thakin Nu then assumed the office of Prime Minister. He too could not remain in power for long. Last Monday, his ministry relinquished office. At the request of the head of state, he himself became leader of the interim government. He has now become Prime Minister once again and will shortly select the other ministers. During the two years when Burma was under Japanese control, he had served as its Prime Minister.

Last week, while former Foreign Minister U Tin Tut was travelling in a motorcar, a bomb was thrown into the vehicle. He died in hospital as a result. He had been a minister in Aung San's cabinet. After Aung San and his comrades were killed, attempts were made upon this man's life. He had already escaped two assassination attempts.

Martial law is in force in Burma today. Among many who hold office, one can expect only the petty satisfaction of frightening people with armed force and then claiming that the country's peace has been preserved.

Peace created by fear is not genuine peace, nor can it endure. Resistance may become a volcano at any moment. Peace can be established only by carefully investigating the grievance whose removal causes people to rise in anger, identifying it, and remedying it.

Those seated in authority everywhere forget this elementary lesson and act otherwise. Peace supplied by the police and army may satisfy people in power for the moment. Yet we do not read in history that rulers in any country have thereby won the people's love.

Perhaps it is the harm produced by thoughts that change according to circumstance: hearts melt enough to shed tears at the sight of the poor and humble, but when guns and batons stand in support, eyes redden and hearts turn to iron. Be that as it may.

One cannot say that Burma's condition will soon improve. We are compelled to conclude that it will be very difficult for the good crop called democracy to grow in that soil. The same degraded condition persists in all subject countries caught in the grip of white imperial powers. When will all this end and a condition arise in which people can live as human beings?

The disorder there has assumed a form that strikes at our own stomachs: at present we will not receive Burmese rice. As a result of the disorder, the fields that produce fine paddy there are being ruined. If this is the state of the fields, one can understand the condition of other industries without further explanation.

The Burmese disorder has not harmed only those who are not Burmese; it harms the Burmese too. This is the reward the people pay for the ruinous conduct of leaders who wrongly imagine that the responsibility of guiding them belongs solely to themselves.

Unless the people's education and knowledge of affairs rise, democracy cannot blossom. The learned saying that leaders arise in accordance with the development of the people's understanding has proved true in Burma's case. Let Burma's condition serve as a valuable lesson to us.

*Dravida Nadu*, 26 September 1948.

## English Translation

“Alas, my son! Are you dead?” cries a mother on hearing that her son has been killed by a bullet. If the place where the bullet took her son's life were at least a battlefield, she might be able to endure her grief to some extent. But if her son lies dead in the middle of his own town, will a mother's heart not melt like wax in a flame?

Mothers who lost their sons wept in many European countries when fierce war raged there. There was no war upon Indian soil. Yet during the past fifteen days, mothers have been made to wail aloud not only in great cities such as Calcutta, Bombay and Madras, but even in peaceful towns such as Kanchipuram and Cheyyar. Bodies lay upon the roads.

The rulers will say that severe rioting occurred, officials were attacked, shops were looted and buildings were burned, and that firing therefore became necessary. Can that explanation produce peace or tranquillity? What requires explanation is why disorder and unrest reached a level at which firing was deemed necessary. Is it enough merely to issue the warning, “Beware! Severe action will be taken if disturbances occur”? The rulers must investigate why a disposition toward unrest grows and how it can be removed.

If such events occurred even in a free country, the people's anguish would be many times greater. Even if a life of happiness flowed like a river under alien rule, people with feeling would not accept foreign government. There is no need to explain how much more deeply the people will be wounded when an alien administration maintains peace through baton charges and gunfire.

If the government still regards India merely as a lackey and an errand boy, disappointment will only increase. India is a place where hundreds of millions live with minds buffeted by waves of discontent. At present anyone, at any instant and for any cause, can kindle a commotion here. Such is the country's sense of indignation. These are no longer people who walk bent over. They have learned to stand upright, to ask “Why?” and to condemn what they consider wrong. The longing for freedom seen throughout the world burns here in leaping flames. The minds of young people in particular have become furnaces throwing out sparks of anger. India today is a volcano.

As long as the government refuses to recognize this, clings to yesterday's notion that it can somehow rule through repression and emergency laws, and loses itself in that delusion, it will encounter only disappointment. The cause of repeated disturbances is discontent. Without attempting to prevent discontent, recognize its growth or remove it, the government waits until it has flared and disorder has appeared in every quarter. It then sings that it is preserving law and peace, using the sound of gunshots as lavish accompaniment. This produces no benefit and wins no praise.

Riots may be suppressed in this manner, but bitterness will increase rather than diminish. Disturbances arise from bitterness of mind. Instead of seeking a means of removing it, the rulers suppress unrest through baton charges and firing and thereby intensify the existing bitterness. And these rulers are a government of foreign masters, so the bitterness has multiplied still further. The method must change. The government must recognize that circumstances have changed.

The sea roared first. Naval ratings went on strike. They did not rise in revolt merely because of dissatisfaction with working conditions and pay, but because they seethed at the way their white superiors treated them. If even in these civilized times racially arrogant officers spoke degradingly to our people serving in the navy, calling them sons of dogs and coolies, why would there not be a roar upon the sea?

This racial arrogance must be eradicated. The government should have properly instructed those officers: “Do not behave arrogantly. This country is not ours. Our very rule over India contradicts the civilized principles of the international order. Today we are here in the capacity of trustees, or guardians of a minor, not as victorious conquerors. Nor can we claim such superiority over Indians in knowledge, ability or civilization as to justify degrading them.

“As a result of the varna-ashrama order, the people of that land were called chandala, base-born, sinful and Sudra by caste-proud people. Having become accustomed through long endurance to those insults, they tolerated for a time our calling them coolies and slaves. This is an age of struggle for rights. Today they will not continue listening to such arrogant speech. Do not forget that conflicts among the peoples of India brought us here as officials; do not mistakenly imagine that we are supreme heroes and they contemptible cowards. Do not act arrogantly. Abandon racial conceit!”

The government ought to have explained this and cleansed the minds of white officers. When it should have declared that racial arrogance must disappear, is it proper for a highly placed government official instead to say, “Even if the Indian Navy has to be destroyed, I shall not hesitate; I will suppress this naval revolt”?

The naval revolt was suppressed. That was expected. But the entire country condemned the measures taken, and the matter was discussed in many parts of the world. With the same mouth with which Prime Minister Attlee announced that three ministers would leave for India to prepare a plan for Indian self-government, he had to announce that a mighty fleet was sailing toward India to suppress the strike and revolt among Bombay's naval ratings. Was that something to speak of with pride? A ship to suppress revolt, and then paper plans aboard another ship! The method of a government that does not understand the country's rising spirit of liberation is wrong.

As soon as unrest arose in the navy, the grievances should have been remedied and the public informed. Leaders of every political party in the country should then have been summoned, the issue explained to them, and their aid used to create calm and peace in the people's minds. The British government may imagine that official communiques can reassure the British public. How can it imagine the same concerning India? How can people whose passion for freedom has given them courage to sacrifice anything accept and be satisfied by statements from a foreign administration?

Such satisfaction can be created only through the country's own leaders. So long as the government remains alien, imagining that it can accomplish this by itself is sheer madness. After the Bombay incident, it should have used the country's party leaders to restore confidence among the public. Instead, it thought the problem could be solved through old conventions such as warnings and official statements. What was the result? Condemnation of the Bombay incident arose throughout the country.

That incident was the first cause of protest, but many other causes naturally joined it. When numerous forces of discontent become bound together simultaneously, the extent, nature and direction of unrest turn disastrous. The incident at Kanchipuram provides an example of this mingling of emotions.

Students set out to observe a hartal. Why? They had read reports of events in many places: the Bombay incident, the punishment of Rashid Ali and Burhanuddin, and the strikes, shop closures and processions held in various places to condemn such events. They therefore wished to conduct a hartal themselves.

After the Bombay incident, if a joint statement by the leaders of all parties had offered counsel preventing discontent from arising in the public mind, those students, who behaved peacefully on ordinary days, would not have wanted a hartal that day. Once they did, school administrators tried to uphold their authority while students tried to uphold their enthusiasm. The collision of those two forces transformed the hartal into a disturbance.

Even then, had there been a joint committee composed of leaders from several parties, disorder need not have grown; trouble could have been prevented at the outset. But because “His Majesty's Government” acted with the attitude that it could not possibly have others perform this work, it summoned officials to suppress the unrest. The unrest grew.

At first the students spoke of dissatisfaction over the Bombay incident and Burhanuddin's punishment. Various other grievances then attached themselves to that discussion.

One person says, “Yes, sir! Yarn is simply not available. And they call this a control! A fine control indeed, a control that takes our lives!” Immediately one part of the crowd begins shouting, “Down with control! Down with the Control Office!”

Another asks, “What is this gathering? Why all this shouting?”

Someone replies, “The schoolchildren are observing a hartal.” Meanwhile the cry “Down with control!” is heard. “What is that about?” the newcomer asks. He is told, “There is an agitation against this yarn control.” On hearing it, he says, “Yes, sir! It has become a great nuisance. When will this rationing end?” Another answers, “Only after it has left all of us half-starved and ruined our bodies.” Yet another says, “The stench turns one's stomach. Who knows where they found this ration rice?”

At once the crowd cries: “Down with rationing! Down with worm-eaten rice! Down with the system that gives one kind of ration to one class and another kind to another!” Part of the crowd begins heading toward the ration office. That was not the original purpose. But once one kind of dissatisfaction is expressed, other grievances seething in people's minds boil over. Drawing one another together like magnetic forces, they assume an enormous form.

Because so many kinds of dissatisfaction have not been removed from time to time, when they all unite on one occasion the disturbance reaches its peak. People then do things that in ordinary circumstances they would consider impermissible. Fear, compassion, consideration, reason, inquiry and calm judgment cannot show themselves. People are driven by a force that had remained hidden until then; they are in a state of frenzy. This is the nature of riots.

It is not enough for the government to think its sole duty is suppressing riots. It must prevent disturbances from beginning by preventing grievances from arising in the first place. An alien government cannot do that. Therefore this work must be undertaken with leaders of the country's various political parties. There will be no benefit if the government asks, “We rule; are party leaders to instruct the people how they should and should not behave?” The present administration is an alien government existing amid the opposition of the country's people. It must first justify its own presence!

The government may feel satisfied that it suppressed the disorder and that public unrest and fear have ended. Its officials may take pride in having put down the disturbances. But bodies were burned, medicine was applied to wounds, and scars remain as consequences of the suppressed tumult. The government must understand the nature of the pain this naturally creates in the people's minds.

Kanchipuram is an ordinary town, yet four people died there in firing. Cheyyar, a place without even a railway, saw one death. These are places where people behave humbly and fearfully before officials, yet officials encountered trouble there. Gandhi has spoken with distress about nonviolence being crushed during these disturbances. Even those who took part understand that stone-throwing, arson and the looting of shops during the riots inflicted loss and suffering upon ourselves.

Nevertheless, the government must not keep repeating only “riot, disorder, stone-throwing and assault” and satisfy itself that it has efficiently performed its duty. An inquiry must be conducted into the firing in various places, and the resulting wound in the public mind must be healed. The government reduces the problem to: “They rioted; we fired; they fell.”

Was firing necessary? Was the situation allowed to develop, or did it develop by itself? Did officials take suitable steps at the beginning to prevent and reduce the spirit of unrest? Did the government summon leaders of the public? These and similar questions must be investigated and explained. That alone is the way to establish peace in people's minds.

Name: Dayalan.

Age: fourteen.

Condition: died when a bullet struck his chest.

Merely conducting an inquest with these particulars cannot console the mother who cried, “Alas, my son!” If the anguish of not only the families of those shot dead but all the country's people is to be relieved, the government must obtain the cooperation of leaders from the various parties and give an assurance that disturbances will not arise and discontent will not be allowed to grow.

If it merely sings the old refrain that this is impossible, riots are bound to occur, and firing must inevitably follow, the people are bound to ask: “Should bodies fall in the middle of the road under a civilized government?” If the white ruler replies, “What can I do? Such is the situation,” the people of this country have no slogan left except: “White man, quit!”

Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 10 March 1946.

## Translator's Notes

The title is printed as “விசாரணை நடத்தூக!” in the OCR source; the evident modern reading “விசாரணை நடத்துக!” is used for the bilingual heading without altering the retained Tamil source block.

The article refers to the Royal Indian Navy uprising of February 1946 and names Rashid Ali and Burhanuddin as rendered by the Tamil source. Historical names and administrative terms such as *hartal*, “Control Office,” varna-ashrama and Swaraj are translated conservatively or retained where appropriate.

The OCR source contains scattered character and spacing errors, including “தடற்படைக்,” “ஷர்த்தால்,” and “தாட்பாடு.” These are not silently rewritten in the preserved Tamil source.

## English Translation

“Except for a few specified industries of fundamental importance, India is not moving toward government ownership of other industries,” Sir Percival Griffiths, an Englishman, has said in London.

He was formerly the leader of the European members' party in the Delhi Legislative Assembly. Readers can therefore weigh his words. Moreover, he spoke at the London Chamber of Commerce, addressing its East India section. He added that “in future, India will be more interested in arrangements like the Sheffield-Birla partnership.”

In other words, India is not going to adopt a programme that brings industries into public ownership according to socialist doctrine. The capitalist system will continue. More particularly, a partnership system will grow in which an Indian capitalist such as Birla and a white capitalist such as one from Sheffield jointly conduct an industry.

This must have delighted the white merchants assembled there. To those worrying that they had lost India and that their hunt for profit would therefore diminish, Sir Percival spoke as though saying: “Do not fear! Birla is here.” The white man was able to build the Viceroy's palace only by entering the field of trade. Hundreds of millions believed that after self-government his domination would be removed not only from politics but also from the economy that governs people's lives. Hundreds of thousands of young people believed that not merely British capitalist control but capitalism itself would collapse, and that every industry would be run for the people with a socialist purpose.

What will they think on hearing Sir Percival say that foreign and domestic capitalists will have an opportunity to become partners? Whom are they to blame? Should the Indian government not condemn this? If London gentlemen will continue hunting profit here through alliances with domestic magnates even after self-rule, what is to become of the hundreds of millions who have suffered and been broken for two centuries?

Congress knows that there are grieving people and agitated idealists of this kind. Perhaps merely to console them, it thought: “Even if we cannot offer a socialist programme, let us announce something bearing its appearance and restrain dissatisfaction.” Through the All India Congress Committee it issued a statement explaining the future pattern of industrial development and promising that the government itself would gradually undertake many industries.

At once the brown masters of industry flew into a rage. Crying angrily, “Ah! Can such things be said? What kind of tendency is this?” more than fifty capitalists from various parts of the country met in Bombay on 30 January. Their statement appeared on 2 February.

Its meaning was this: “If the committee's proposals are implemented, industry will not develop in the country. They will disrupt the existing industrial structure. Planning and working according to socialism at this time will damage the national economy and cause hardship and loss to those who have invested in industry. What is urgently required now is increased production. For that, people with demonstrated qualification and skill must conduct industry. Government management of major industries will not succeed. We therefore warn you.”

No socialism; do not touch the capitalist system; do not regulate our pursuit of profit; interfere with our domination and destruction will follow: this is the threat contained in the capitalists' warning.

In London, a white merchant tells that country's capitalists, “Do not fear; Indian capitalists will become our partners.”

In Bombay, Indian capitalists gather and pour fire upon Congress, which bears responsibility for government, merely because it has announced a plan carrying only the faintest shade of socialism, not even a scheme of the kind framed by communist or socialist parties. They speak of a terrible curse. This is the extent of their patriotism. They regard the country only as a forest in which to hunt profit. They seek companions for the hunt and enlist white men experienced in that occupation.

Gandhi said only, “Hindus and Muslims must not turn the country into a cremation ground through mutual hatred.” For that, a bullet from Godse killed him. The All India Congress Committee did not propose confiscating factories, bringing into government ownership the property of those who had amassed tens of millions in profit through capitalism, or having the state conduct every industry. It said only that the government should gradually operate industries while keeping the socialist ideal in view. Even this evokes the capitalists' fierce condemnation and curse.

Who were the eminent men responsible for this statement, who stood in the front rank of the capitalist camp and held the Bombay meeting? The source lists Sir Sorab Saklatvala, Sir Dalal, Tata, a name rendered as “Karu Sharab,” Sir Chandavarkar, Bhagwandas Mehta, Sir Wadia, “14.8. Master,” Ratanchand Hirachand, Ramdas “Iyasand,” “Bri Danukar,” “San. C. Chinai,” Sir Shantidas Askuran, Sir Visvesvaraya, Sir Homi Mehta, Sir Cowasji “Dabak,” Sir Joseph Kay, Sir Chunilal Mehta, Sir Gurunath Bewoor, “M. Bramble,” and Sir Rahimullah.

On one side stands the wrath of the magnates; on the other, at the same time, comes the assault of zealots for religious domination. Congress has been pushed between them. If it yields in fear to the magnates' fury, it will have committed an immense betrayal of the people.

The magnates never fail to exploit any opportunity or power.

Newspapers reported that Sir C. P. Ramaswami Iyer was departing for America immersed in Vedantic inquiry and intending to speak about Indian culture. Circumstances have now arisen that make the actual nature of his service perfectly clear.

In the first week of February, a banquet was held for Sir C. P. Ramaswami Iyer in San Francisco. By whom? Was it by American philosophers and priests? That is what it ought to have been, since Sir C. P. supposedly went to America to teach Vedanta. Yet the hosts were not Vedantins. Eyrhart, president of an American Federal Reserve Bank, and wealthy men from California gave the banquet.

One might suppose that they too perhaps had acquired an interest in Vedanta and wished to learn from a visitor from India about the inner meaning of the Upanishads or the excellence of the Agamas. At the banquet, however, the discussion concerned neither Vyasa, Vasishtha, Sankhya philosophy nor the subtleties of the sandhyavandanam. It concerned how many factories produced iron, cement, cloth and other goods, and how they were operated.

Was it merely worldly conversation? Not even that; it had a purpose. The capitalist hosts told Sir C. P., who had supposedly come for Vedantic inquiry: “We are ready to invest capital. Do industries to be started in India need money? We shall give it generously. If loans are wanted, we will lend; if partnership is proposed, we will join.”

One may ask what Sir C. P. could do about those gentlemen's desire for profit. Could he not have said, “Sirs, I did not come to turn Indian industry into hunting grounds for American capitalists”? Would he say it? Did he say it? No. He remained in San Francisco for a month, meeting and speaking with the capitalists, factory owners and moneylenders of the region.

Nor did he stop with speaking. He published this opinion in the press: “American financiers and industrial capitalists are prepared to invest money in establishing and operating factories in India. They have a great interest in this matter.”

Thus magnates in Bombay, London, San Francisco and elsewhere are sniffing about for a hunt for profit. Yet if Congress prepares even a plan with the slightest socialist tint, they spring upon it and call it a disastrous curse.

Young people and idealists trust that however fiercely the magnates may rage, they cannot prevent socialism, because Pandit Nehru will not fear this commotion. The Pandit has spoken many times in a way that made them consider his convictions radical, his purpose admirable, his resolve unshakable and his courage capable of breaking every opposition. It is therefore natural to expect him to disregard the threats of the greedy.

This is a time when the country is submerged in grief. Pandit Nehru is witnessing street scenes unlike any he has seen in his life. At such a moment the Bombay capitalists rage and threaten a terrible curse. That itself is merciless. But did they stop there? No. They dared to go personally to Pandit Nehru and state their objections. At what a time, and with what boldness!

On 17 February in New Delhi, the gentlemen who raised this voice of condemnation met Pandit Nehru. The source names Sir Modi, “K. M. Sharab,” Sir Shriram, Sir Goenka, Sir Mukherjee, Kasturbhai Lalbhai and T. M. Kattu. They conveyed their doubts and fears about the Congress Committee's programme and sought an explanation. Observe their speed. They reportedly conferred for a long time and obtained an assurance that the government would consider the views of those industrial rulers.

As though this were not enough, Pandit Nehru delivered a long speech on the subject in the Indian Parliament assembled in Delhi. Reading it, one might wonder whether the speaker was truly Pandit Nehru.

A resolution had been introduced declaring that this country's economic structure should follow a socialist pattern, that major industries should therefore be run by the government, and that agriculture should be reorganized through cooperative farming. Pandit Nehru gave a long speech arguing that this resolution was unnecessary. If Sir Modi, who had raged in Bombay and then met Nehru in New Delhi, praised the Pandit and declared himself perfectly satisfied and very happy, little more need be said about the nature of the speech.

The mover of the resolution argued that the government must operate every essential industry, because leaving them with private capitalists damages the public welfare, turns the poor into paupers, turns millionaires into multimillionaires, and creates terrifying economic inequality. He supported these points by quoting Congress resolutions and Pandit Nehru's own speeches. But the Pandit, seeking to dispel the millionaires' doubts and fears, spoke after reducing even the scent of idealism. The mental pain he experienced while delivering the speech must have exceeded the pain we feel when reading it.

He said that the All India Congress Committee's view was not final, that the government had not said it would accept it exactly, and that no such rule existed.

He himself was head of the government and head of the committee that prepared the statement which had stirred the capitalists' condemnation. Congress, he said, had for seventeen years maintained that industry should develop according to a socialist programme. There was no difficulty at the level of speech, but many complications arose in implementation: how far to proceed, what to begin first, how to begin and what consequences would result.

Could Sir Modi fail to rejoice on hearing this said on behalf of the government? He might even have felt pride mingled with delight that they had placed Pandit Nehru under compulsion to speak in this manner.

Nehru's speech conveyed these further views:

The programme issued by the Congress Committee is far removed from socialism.

It would be wrong for the government to waste money buying existing privately owned industrial concerns.

The government is not going to act hastily either to seize or disrupt the present system.

If the government is to undertake many industries, suitable administrators and experts will be required. At present it does not possess the necessary facilities.

The government does not wish to destroy the confidence of the industrial rulers. It needs their goodwill.

Rather than nationalizing industries now under capitalism, it would be preferable for the government to own industries established in the future. Rapidly developing science will provide within ten or fifteen years new powers not now present in industry. The government will prevent those powers from becoming private monopolies and use them to produce tremendous industrial growth.

The All India Congress Committee is not an administrative machine; it has merely published proposals. This Parliament and the government must discuss and shape those proposals before action can begin.

The government does not intend suddenly to alter the existing order.

These are the ideas found in Pandit Nehru's speech.

Sir Modi rejoiced. What of the people?

The raging magnates will exult, thinking that they have made the Pandit contradict himself through the Pandit's own mouth. What of the people?

22 February 1948.

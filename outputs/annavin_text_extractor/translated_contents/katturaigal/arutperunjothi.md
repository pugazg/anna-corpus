## English Translation

Sardar Vedarathnam and a few of his companions are said to have joined together some days ago to celebrate a festival for Vallalar.

They reportedly spoke in praise of the nobility of Vallalar's heart. With hearts melting in emotion, they urged the people to follow the righteous path he had shown.

We are glad. But does the virtue of walking in Vallalar's path arise merely from celebrating festivals of this kind?

If they have truly perceived Vallalar's heart and desire the country to stand by it, celebrating a festival alone is not enough. They must turn the principles he laid down into a practical programme. Do they do so? They do not. They celebrate only the festival with splendour. It is an empty game.

This article illustrates the conduct of such festival celebrants.

“Younger brother! Pour plenty of oil into the lamp. Twist the wick properly. Let the flame shine brightly.

“Have the dhoti thoroughly bleached. I must wear pure white clothing.

“Place plenty of sacred ash in the packet. The whole body must be covered with it. See whether there is sandalwood paste.

“Supervise the cooking carefully.

“Have the milk boiled properly. Make sure the fruit does not spoil.

“Ah! How wonderfully my lord has explained even this little detail!

> Squeeze the three fruits separately,
> strain them and unite them as one.

“Squeeze the three fruits, strain away every trace of pulp, and then combine their juices.

“What beauty! What clarity!

“What did he ever fail to explain correctly? From the juice of fruit to the nectar of the Supreme Being's grace, our lord explained how it might be obtained. The great one composed poetry in classical Tamil. *Arutperunjothi, Thanipperungkarunai*: Vast Light of Grace, Supreme and Singular Compassion!”

Speaking in this fashion, many devotees of Ramalinga Adigalar of Vadalur reverently put on pure white clothing and sacred ash. They lovingly celebrate a festival for Adigalar, sing the *Arutpa*, drink in its flavour and become ecstatic. Crying, “My sovereign, my lord, my God,” they stand in worship, their hearts melting and their physical bodies thrilling with rapture, and perform many acts of worship.

They go in procession through the streets. We see them celebrate the festival, teach and hear the doctrine of the Pure, Harmonious Sanmarga, read the songs, listen while learned people explain their meaning, and attain supreme bliss.

If devotion is accompanied by some financial means, they place Adigalar's sacred portrait in a palanquin or chariot and take it in procession. Those who possess devotion, money and some imagination feed the poor. Those who know the songs and also possess musical understanding, training and a sweet voice sing the *Arutpa* and provide the joy of music. All this is well and good.

What pains us is the vast difference between what the “devotees” do at the festival and how they act on every other day. Are there not multitudes whose festival day is devoted to the *Arutpa*—the Song of Grace—but whose remaining days are ruled by *marutpa*, the song of delusion? The Vast Light of Grace appears for one day and disappears; the very next moment, those who worship at Ramalingar's matchless feet enter darkness themselves and watch others enter it too. Is this the gratitude his followers show the noble one who bestowed the *Arutpa*?

Cast off the costume! Abandon the futile performance! Go quickly and proclaim his teaching to the world and to every heart! Let everyone understand the vital substance in all that the noble Ramalingar declared. This is the counsel we offer.

No gratitude that “devotees” can or should show Ramalinga Adigalar is greater than communicating to the world the truths he expressed, and leading people along the path those truths reveal.

A mother delights when she sees her child crawl, utter its baby speech and play before her. A victorious king rejoices when the country he rules becomes a worthy land, famine and disease depart, and the people live joyfully as though drinking milk and honey. In the same way, Adigalar would rejoice only upon seeing people who undertake the discipline of listening to his words, standing by them and helping others to stand by them.

If instead they continue to hold festivals, stage amusements, deliver hollow speeches and conduct useless performances, he would not be pleased. His heart would wither, and he would surely say: “Mindless people! Was it for you to do this that I gave the *Arutpa*? Is this a day for dancing, singing and noisy display? Is this the beauty of your claim to have understood me?”

If a painter who has laboured skilfully day and night to create a picture sees a small child tear it into tiny pieces, he will be distressed and regard the act as torture inflicted upon himself.

That is how it seems when we see these “noble people” conduct a Ramalingar festival with relish, yet make no effort to carry that spirit into society and give neither encouragement nor assistance to workers for social reform.

Why do they praise Ramalinga Adigalar? Is it because they imagine he will give them the key to the gates of heaven? Or is it because he rebuked the dull-witted people who corrupted society by introducing wicked ideas in the name of religion, pointed out, “This is evil, this is deceitful, this is wrong,” and laid down the Sanmarga, the true and righteous path?

Is their interest in the peel, the segment, the fibre, the sound it makes, the refuse or the juice? Where, gentlemen, should your attention lie?

Does “Arutperunjothi” mean lighting a thousand-candle lamp and admiring its beauty? If one is to understand the beauty of the *Arutpa*, does that mean merely singing it, explaining it and becoming enraptured? Or does it mean living by its principles and causing others to live by them? Which is necessary? Which is a festival? Which requires action? These are the questions we ask Ramalingar's devotees.

We ask them to consider to whom he spoke, what he said, why he said it and in what manner.

Addressing those who “dance because they are possessed by the demon called religion,” he called upon them to recognise “the ghostly hold of sectarian and religious paths in this renowned world” as the play of deranged children.

He said that the many paths called sects and religions had until then spread a road of repeated birth; that people had failed to know the noble path and, dying over and over, had entered dense darkness. Therefore, he said, “Listen: I proclaim the sacred path of grace.”

He addressed the people of the world who wandered in attachment to caste, religions, sectarian paths, the clamour of scriptures, disputes over lineage and primordial divisions. “All the scriptures merely utter confusion; they cannot show the way clearly like eyes,” he said.

He asked:

> You engage in futile controversy, calling them Vedas and Agamas;
> you do not know what the Vedas and Agamas have produced.
> Because they were spoken deceitfully, they did not reveal truth openly.
> What use are they?

He urged the people to throw “the rubbish of dark caste doctrines and scriptures” as manure upon barren land, and to cast delusive castes, sects, religions and ashrama customs into a pit and cover them with earth. For, he said, “the histories and doctrines that speak of the four varnas, ashrama observances and the rest are all children's games.”

Describing his own experience, he said, “I cast away caste, religion and sect; I withdrew from the rubbish of scripture.” He called for “every blind custom to be buried beneath the earth” and invited the people to enter the path of the Harmonious Sanmarga.

What do the loving followers whose hearts melted at that invitation now do? They plaster their houses, light lamps, turn the pages of a book, shout at the top of their voices, play all manner of games and celebrate a festival.

He said distinctions of caste and religion were the games of deranged children. Have these people cured that derangement? No!

He told them not to roll about in the rubbish of scripture. Have they stopped doing so? No!

Adigalar warned that the Vedas and Agamas were deceitful words. Have his devotees abandoned them? No!

He declared that the four-varna order must not remain. Have they abolished it? No!

He told them to cast delusive castes, sects and religions into a pit and cover them with earth. Have they done so? No!

What else did they do? They celebrated a festival. They lit lamps. They came in procession through the streets. They separately squeezed the three fruits! They prepared and drank the juice.

They chopped and cooked plantain, tender brinjal, drumstick and other vegetables. They ate the vegetable curry after saying, “Down with mustard!” They cried, “Arutperunjothi, Thanipperungkarunai”—“Vast Light of Grace, Supreme and Singular Compassion.” They read the *Arutpa*, grew tired and fell asleep!

Caste, false religion, the four-varna order and its rites and observances, scripture, Veda and Agama—everything Adigalar told them to abandon—will look upon the festival celebrants, laugh and thunder:

“Today is the festival of these futile people, but it is always our victory celebration. Today they read the *Arutpa*; from tomorrow they will bow at our feet. Today they became Ramalingar's devotees; from tomorrow they are our slaves.”

Alas! Alas! How many noble paths in this country have faded and died through being spoken of endlessly and only by the tongue! How many great people and holy servants have been killed here through the very act of being worshipped! How many words of grace have been praised and forgotten! Why were they born in this country? Why?

*Dravida Nadu*, 3 October 1948.

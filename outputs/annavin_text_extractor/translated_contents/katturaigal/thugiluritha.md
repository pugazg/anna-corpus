## English Translation

## A Babylonian Myth

### Deities Who No Longer Exist Today

“Gatekeeper! Come swiftly to the gate and open it. My lover is within. Open it so I may see him. Open it! If you refuse, I shall not remain idle. Know that the gate will be shattered to dust. I shall go on breaking every ancient gate. Gatekeeper, come! I shall raise the dead, send them into battle, and destroy the living. Know this: the army will be small and the dead numerous. Therefore open the gate at once.”

Unable to find her lover, the maiden had wandered everywhere until her body grew thin and she became distraught. She had heard that the place where he stayed had barriers, guards, venomous eyes, gates and savage customs. But once she knew he was there, she forgot everything and came. Finding the gate bolted, she struck it repeatedly. When no one came to open it, she cried, “Gatekeeper! Gatekeeper! Come swiftly and open the gate!”

How much material this alone would provide our connoisseurs! They would read and relish it repeatedly, divide every phrase, and swell with delight over its meaning, even if merely hearing it did not already transport them.

You have heard only of a lover running in search of his beloved. Here is novelty and wonder: the beloved herself wanders. Nor does she merely wander. Until she learns his whereabouts she searches everywhere, reaches the abode of the fiend, declares her longing, and reveals her anguish with poetic power. Do not ask whether the maiden knew poetry. Poetry blossoms in love. They would delight in declaring that this flowering vine was love's embodiment, love itself.

The story does not end with this sentiment. It grows.

The gatekeeper came, saw the woman, and did not open the gate. He ran to report the “news” to his mistress.

“Is she beauty embodied?”

“Yes, the very limit of beauty.”

“Does she cry and writhe in anguish?”

“Yes. Even stone would melt at the maiden's lament.”

“She stands asking that the gate be opened?”

“Yes. That sword-eyed woman vows she will smash it if it is not opened.”

“Watch what I do today to the woman who made that vow. Guard, do not open the gate!”

“But she is wailing.”

“That is music to me!”

“What if she stops wailing and begins to break the gate?”

“Yes, that man-killer may do so. Do one thing. Open the gate and bring her here. But let the wicked creature receive the honours customarily given here. Let that restless woman learn our grandeur and power.”

Thus spoke the guard and his mistress. The guard opened the gate and led the lamenting maiden into that enormous palace. It was pitch dark. Many kinds of birds flew in circles. Horrible sounds tore at the ears. Dust and stench were everywhere. Through this place the queen of beauty was led. Heedless of it all, she went on in the hope of seeing and obtaining the lord of her heart.

Had not the queen ordered that she be brought with the customary honours? The guard did not disobey. Her beautiful crown was seized as she entered. At the next gateway went her earrings; at the third, the ornament at her neck; at the fourth, many jewels; and at the fifth, her girdle. She neither became angry nor lamented these degradations. She cried only, “Go on! Take me where my lover is!” At the sixth gate even that lovely woman's garment was taken and her robe stripped away. Only then did she ask why.

She was dragged onward for a great distance and finally halted before an audience hall. There sat Allatu, queen of that realm. The unclothed beauty stood majestically before her.

“See how she stands like a lioness even after so many humiliations!” Allatu said scornfully.

“All these torments are not enough. I shall give her a lover: every cruel disease!” she roared.

She summoned Namtar, the evil god who brings disease, and ordered, “Touch her, god. Kindle disease throughout her body.” Namtar's destructive power filled the maiden's body with illness. Beauty became decay.

But did her suffering end with her? No. It spread across the earth, and production itself ceased.

How our poets would exult if they obtained such a “story”! If the beautiful woman took so long to pass through each gate, our master poets would require a hundred times longer to describe it. Had this tale fallen into the hands of Kambar, how long would it have taken her to cross the sixth threshold? He would have composed a hundred verses, inserted cries such as “Ah! What a wonder! A sight so rare!”, blended and kneaded the erotic sentiment, and declared that even if Hari, Hara and Brahma came to stop him, he could not enjoy happiness in this world or the next without composing at least twenty more verses. Fortunately, that unclothed woman never appeared in Kambar's imagination.

Before continuing, friends should consider whether this story resembles a combination of the imagined Savitri and Draupadi tales.

Seeing the dreadful condition of the good woman who went seeking her lord, not only did the fourfold world tremble; the gods too grew angry. For the woman subjected to such torment and disgrace was none other than the queen of heaven, one among many deities.

Such was even a heavenly queen's love. She wandered through many places seeking her lover. The last place she entered was the underworld. Its queen was Allatu, who not only stripped the heavenly queen but sent disease upon her. Why? The queen of the underworld held such jealousy and anger towards the queen of heaven. She took revenge upon the heavenly queen named Ishtar.

Afterward the greater gods suppressed this outrage and rescued the heavenly queen from the underworld.

Is this invention inferior by even a sesame seed to the ancient myths of our country? Who could say so? This myth contains in abundance the “nine sentiments” sought by our connoisseurs. It too is a story of gods, not mortals.

This is a Babylonian myth. Ishtar, queen of heaven, loves the divine figure Tammuz and goes to the underworld to obtain him. There the deceitful goddess Allatu humiliates her and inflicts disease upon her. As Ishtar languishes, the primal gods save and restore her.

Ishtar, Allatu and Tammuz were not merely epic characters. They were gods. The people of Babylonia built temples and worshipped them. There was no deficiency in ritual or sanctity. Today there is no Ishtar, Tammuz or trouble-making Allatu. Their temples, festivals, chariot processions and sacred hymns are gone. All have become former things.

When scholars told the Babylonians, “Do not ruin your minds by believing in such imaginary gods,” did the people believe them easily? Would anyone? They cursed: “Fool! You disparage our ancestors' gods, the greatness of our myths and the sanctity of worship. Is this proper? May you be ruined!” Yet their anger subsided; the momentum of new knowledge did not. Rational teaching did not cease until Ishtar, Allatu and the others departed from the people's minds and people could laugh, when reminded of them, that they arose from the mental confusion of remote ancestors.

There too, as here, such stories appeared contrary to reason when read plainly. Some tried saying that Ishtar represented power, Tammuz pleasure, and Allatu suffering, and that the tale revealed profound meaning when interpreted that way. But the young laughed.

“Storyteller, we can understand plainly enough the philosophy that power can wipe away suffering and obtain pleasure. Why must you drag us into a six-gated palace, stand an unclothed beauty before us, show us a hideous god, and torment us?” they asked, clapping and laughing.

Over time these “deities” lost esteem, and the people began seeking true knowledge. Across much of today's world, that long journey has reached belief in one God. Here, people insist that thirty-three crore gods must exist. If the obscenities in the stories about them are pointed out, faces redden and glare.

Why? Many friends mistakenly regard criticism of inventions as wicked criticism of religious feeling and become angry. They ask whether it is necessary. They imagine we harbour bitterness towards God and therefore write in this manner. These are needless accusations built upon a mistaken idea.

In countless countries people treated innumerable gods as objects of worship, held many festivals, and engaged in frenzied ritual. Because their faith was profound and they feared that changing custom would bring destruction both here and in the next world, whenever doubt arose about whether their beliefs were right, they adopted the rule: “Drive away the devil of doubt and seek the true guru's feet.” But new knowledge attacked with enough force to shatter fear and longing. Antiquity fought stubbornly with terrifying weapons, yet it was defeated. Before defeat, it threatened people with hymns, spells, strings of beads, skulls, cremation-ground earth, and every imaginable object.

Before the light of rational knowledge, many deities formed in ancient imagination vanished without trace. In those countries today, not only the young but even the old do not know the names of their former gods. Those deities were once considered mighty, demanded in sacrifice even the daughters of world-ruling kings, possessed sky-high towers and gem-bright halls, and sat surrounded by priests whose pointing fingers served as sceptres. Today they cannot be found however long one searches.

This does not mean ignorance and atheism run wild there, or gracious speech died and delusion grew. Those countries remain in a better condition than ours; they have not perished.

When building a multi-storeyed house, workers first use wooden supports or raise a mound of earth. As the floors rise, the method changes. Once the building is complete, no one preserves the original heap of timber or earth; it is removed, and people walk upstairs. When grain ripens, people do not ask, “Should we destroy the plant that gave us this grain?” They harvest it. They tread, thresh and beat the very crop that yielded red rice.

The harvest in the world of ideas is the same. Those who do not understand this grow weary and shout. Even they will eventually recognise that this process has occurred in many places across the earth; it is neither novel nor wicked nor the conduct of fools.

That is why it is necessary to explain that peoples in many parts of the world once possessed, just as we did, numerous splendid deities fashioned with imaginative power and epic taste. Everyone under the sceptre fell prostrate before them. But when rational knowledge was born, their authority disappeared, true spiritual knowledge arose, and belief in one God, without a mount to ride or wives to embrace, became established.

We also trust that this explanation will pulverise the false claim that nowhere else in the world did poets possess imagination and mental richness in the measure and variety of our poets of old.

**23 November 1947**

## Translator's Notes

The title compares Allatu to Dushasana of the *Mahabharata*, notorious for attempting to strip Draupadi. The Babylonian names are rendered as they appear conceptually in the article: Ishtar, Tammuz, Allatu and Namtar. Modern scholarship commonly uses Ereshkigal for the underworld queen in the descent myth, but the source's `அலாட்டு` is preserved in translation rather than silently replaced.

`Nine sentiments` refers to the traditional `navarasa` aesthetic scheme. Hari, Hara and Brahma denote Vishnu, Siva and Brahma. Kambar is the medieval Tamil poet of the *Kamba Ramayanam*. Savitri and Draupadi are invoked as comparative Indian mythic figures. `Thirty-three crore gods` is the familiar traditional expression translated literally in the source's argumentative context.

The article presents Babylonian religion as a stage in a universal rationalist history. Its historical and theological generalisations are translated as Anna's argument and are not independently endorsed or verified here.

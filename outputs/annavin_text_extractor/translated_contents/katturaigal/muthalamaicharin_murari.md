## English Translation

I went and saw;

my mind was bewildered!

I witnessed distress

and became troubled!

I am afraid!!

An astonishing experience has befallen our Chief Minister—like the enlightenment said to have come to the Buddha beneath the Bodhi tree. In a sorrowful voice he cries, “O white dove, bring me joy by ending my agitation! O white dove, show me the way out of this distress!” With a great sigh he has asked, “What are we to do?” In anguish he has wondered, “How are we going to carry on?” And in fear he has said, “When I see this situation, the future looks terrifying!”

He is the Chief Minister, yet enlightenment has dawned only now. The tale says that Valli, who had been happily shooing away the birds with cries of “Alolam! Alolam!”, became alarmed when she saw the elephant. Our Kumaraswami appears to be in just that condition.

Perhaps he grew tired of elegant Madras, where the sea breeze blows, wealthy men crowd the veranda and those who intimidate others in the party's name stand waiting. Whatever the reason, he went out to tour the countryside. Only after visiting the outlying districts, it seems, did he understand the situation and become distressed—like Valli, who had delighted in her bird-scaring song but shrieked when she saw the elephant.

This week the Chief Minister reportedly toured Madurai and Salem districts. What he saw frightened him. Distress produced agitation, and agitation produced fear. That is what he told newspaper reporters after returning to Madras.

The “fear” that has seized the gentleman from Rajapalayam is indeed pitiable. Servants ought to stand before him with folded hands; others ought to supervise his mills and industries; and he ought to practise striding grandly through his house with the two sides of his upper garment hanging loose. It is no wonder this hardship brought him distress, agitation and fear.

Yet every day reports have arrived—with photographs—from every corner of the country about cries of hunger and pitiful deaths. Now, like a Buddha who has discovered some new truth, he sings a mournful strain: “I travelled and saw; I was filled with sorrow!” That is what astonishes us.

We had thought Congress ministers suffered only from a shortage of experience. Through his verse of anguish, our Chief Minister now explains, “That is not all; we are similarly short of understanding.”

He toured the Madurai districts. The food situation, he says, is in disorder. The monsoon has done terrible damage. He is afraid—afraid that conditions may become utterly disastrous. He announces all this in anguish as though he had only just arrived from London and discovered the situation here.

One of the reporters listening to the Chief Minister's “Mukhari” must have been a mischievous fellow. Delicately, he posed a question: “Sir, without adequate assistance from the Central Government, we cannot save the people, can we?” Fortunately the reporter's question did not leave the minister speechless. As though calling out, “O white moon, come at this moment and bring delight by ending my agitation!”, he sang this troubled song while imagining the reporters to be the Central Government:

Central Government!

Central Government!

Famine closes in;

human beings die.

The sight of death

scorches the heart.

Central Government,

do not forget—come!

Remember the moment;

help us in time!

End our distress

and dispel our agitation!

Give proper aid

and avert death!

Do not leave us in torment;

I alone cannot bear it!!

The reporter had asked whether handling the situation would be easy without the Central Government's help.

The Chief Minister replied: “Unless it understands our position, regards us with sympathy, recognises the occasion and responsibly provides adequate aid of the proper kind, it will not be easy—it will be impossible—for this government to handle the present situation.”

The Chief Minister's Mukhari says only what we already knew: “The provincial government can do nothing.” We understand that better than the minister. But when we consider the condition of the Central Government in which he places his faith, we become more frightened than Kumaraswami himself.

Only now does Kumaraswami cry, “I went and saw; I am afraid.” If even he says so, who knows what great calamity awaits us and the people who witness starvation deaths every day?

The Chief Minister believes the famine can be relieved if the Central Government shows “sympathy.” But it is precisely when we learn what the Central Government he trusts is like that terror and bewilderment seize us.

On 12 December, the day before the Chief Minister disclosed his “new experience and intelligent opinion,” India's Food Minister spoke in Bombay—no, no, he issued a warning. The newspapers carried the news: “The Government of India has warned Madras.” In other words, the Indian Government has warned Madras.

What was the warning for? Was it this?

“Provincial Government, we have observed your lethargy. Though you know people are dying of hunger, you sleep without finding a remedy. Could you not have sent us even a word? Your drowsiness has exhausted our patience. Besides, you are forever quarrelling. Prakasam submits a list of corruption charges against you, while in the Legislative Assembly you conduct a street fight, each saying, ‘I am honourable; he is the consummate rogue!’ This is improper. We cannot tolerate your intoxicated speeches while you neglect the people's suffering. We warn that if you behave this way again, we shall take action.”

No, the warning was not of that kind. When a subordinate errs, it is natural for a superior to warn him. But the Congress high command, which calls itself the superior and has accumulated enormous powers, warned Madras in this fashion:

“Madras relies far too heavily upon the Central Government to save its people; therefore we warn Madras.”

What a story! Madras trusts the Central Government. Trusting it is supposedly wrong. Hence the warning!

The Honourable “Mahatma of Trees,” Munshi, delivered this warning on behalf of the Indian Government: the Premier of Madras trusts the Central Government!

The Central Government warns him, “Do not return here.”

The Chief Minister raises his Mukhari lament, while the Indian Government threatens, “Hey, boy! Go somewhere else!”

What a warning—a strange spectacle filled with absurdity! A woman in a mother's place does not warn her son when he asks for food. A mother cow does not hesitate to give milk to the calf bounding towards her. Even if she has not a handful of rice, a mother will beg to relieve her child's hunger; she will not stop and drive the child away. Even a cow will not repel the calf that seeks refuge with her. When a chick opens its mouth, even a bird will not peck it; it brings food from somewhere and feeds it.

Yet our Indian Government, which has amassed powers and facilities while pretending to be like a mother to her child, warns Madras.

The Chief Minister says hunger can be ended only if the Central Government shows sympathy.

Yes! Even if he wants to act, he has no authority to buy the desired commodity from wherever it is available. That “authority” belongs to the Central Government—to “Mother India”!

But Mother India behaves not merely like a stepmother, but like a human beast: she threatens and drives away the infant crying from hunger.

It is a tangled situation, a cruel and inescapable net that will turn the country into another Bengal.

“I foresee horror,” says the Chief Minister of Madras.

“What can I do? Do something!” says Mother India.

When will this wretched condition change? We ask the great souls who call “India” their mother: is this right?

The Chief Minister weeps, while the Indian Government, which possesses the authority to end his weeping, threatens him—is this right?

Think about it. What does it matter whether a government incapable of relieving hunger remains in this world or departs?

By clinging to a thoroughly wicked gang prepared to earn the name “sinners” who can see starvation without pity—

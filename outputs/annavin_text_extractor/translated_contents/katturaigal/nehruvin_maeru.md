## English Translation

Pandit Nehru has issued his announcement concerning the Third Five-Year Plan. The public had already expected such an announcement and was not surprised. Just as in the advanced countries, Pandit Nehru continues to show enthusiasm for making plan after plan here. He has no time even to tell the public in advance how many more Five-Year Plans are yet to be prepared.

Though the announcement of the Third Plan causes no surprise, the flashes of anger found in the statement do astonish us.

Nehru's attitude appears to be this: “Implementation of the Third Five-Year Plan is absolutely essential. No questions or doubts may arise about this plan drawn up for national progress. Even if doubts arise, expressing them is a grave sin, and those who do so must be shunned. Asking people to reflect upon the results of plans already implemented is treason. Experts express views about plans only because they do not want them carried out. Whatever their political association, such experts must be disregarded. Plans are constructed; there is no need to learn the public's opinion about them.”

For ten years he has proceeded as he pleased without asking the people's views. Why should he now feel any need to know them? Would anyone dare advise him to learn and act according to public opinion? Certainly not.

Democratic government ought to be of the people and for the people. Here, however, a leader elected under that system issues a plan and the country is expected merely to be satisfied on hearing it. If a condition in which there is nobody to question authority continues, democracy itself may be endangered. History offers many examples of the consequences of defects in democratic government.

Those who sing his praise provide many reasons why the people must listen to everything Pandit Nehru says. But the people wish to express views because the plans are supposedly made for them.

Nehru refuses to accept the natural truth that mistakes occur. His attainment of the highest political position does not make everything he thinks correct. Even the most ordinary people can offer suggestions about plans made for their welfare, but he fails to recognize this.

The Second Five-Year Plan approaches completion. Amid how many obstacles and difficulties has it been implemented? At many moments people even wondered what harms it might cause. Without the timely large-scale assistance of countries such as America, it might have failed altogether and the rulers would have lost their honour. Yet nobody can say that a plan completed amid so many troubles has produced its expected benefits.

Every country that came forward to aid implementation kept sending warnings: “Your calculations are mistaken; the plans are not honestly framed; money is wasted in the methods used to carry them out.” One may even say that no assisting country lacked a sincere wish that the plan succeed. Nevertheless, did the Second Plan yield the expected result? Even Pandit Nehru cannot say its full benefit was secured. He can say only that despite some defects in implementation, we are progressing in general.

Factories worthy of being called temples rise every day; wild rivers are harnessed; villages awaken to new life; the country appears with renewed beauty. Such development, we are told, cannot be shown by account books and statistics; the faces of the people themselves reveal it. This is the imagined spectacle with which Nehru wishes to demonstrate the country's excellence.

We do not deny that he wants the country to obtain such distinctions. But one who desires them must descend a little from his height and look at poor people confined in huts and suffering hunger. The plans are made for the poor and humble; he must develop the willingness to accept their suggestions and corrections. Emitting flames of anger at people who express views is neither just nor helpful to democracy.

Even if plans are framed by honest people, one cannot say that those people remain connected with the public. Inspecting land from an aeroplane and preparing agricultural schemes, or turning the pages of files and deciding how to mechanize industry, demonstrates the distance between planners and people. They forget that their decisions affect the lives of hundreds of millions.

The planners are intellectuals; that must be admitted. But it cannot be concealed that they are too distant to understand the people's joys and sorrows and unwilling to learn their true condition.

Announcements of plans appear suddenly. They receive insufficient debate. Planners greatly expect public cooperation in implementing what they provide, yet become angry when their plans offer the people tears and lamentation instead.

Government officials, encouraged by guardians of planning, continually publish statements: “Food production has increased; industry has advanced; schemes undertaken to remove the country's distress have begun to bear fruit.” We can see hunger-stricken people looking only at such statements while expecting plans to guide them toward a life worth living.

In a democracy, plans should be made only after every matter has been debated. They can benefit the people only when reasonable doubts may be expressed. Under proper democratic rule, people themselves should have the opportunity to devise plans, and rulers should implement the plans they give. The people may be unable to set schemes on paper with statistics, but they can state their needs and explain how those needs can be met. Had plans been prepared with public participation, many enormous mistakes later admitted by their own authors could have been avoided.

The rulers repeatedly say that expanding industry is the purpose of planning. Examining this claim reveals the truth. Economic inequality exists because the dominance of the wealthy prevents industrial enterprises from becoming common property and workers from receiving rights in them. We must ask whether the Five-Year Plans have served to remove inequality in that manner.

Motor cars run as signs of industrial expansion, while workers continue to suffer disease and misery. It is meaningless to call such scenes proof of national progress.

Industrial development involves many problems, including health and housing. The minister himself says even these could not be solved. Our legislative schemes resemble plans borrowed from Western practice, but our country lacks enough officials and a dedicated organization to implement them. Their purpose therefore remains unfulfilled. After Minister Nanda himself admits this, how can the public possess enthusiasm?

The government does not wish to attend to another industrial problem. On one side it repeatedly issues reports of substantial industrial progress; on the other, the cruelty of unemployment continues to grow. The ruling party fails to give it serious attention.

Many advocates shouted themselves hoarse that “the notable feature of the Second Five-Year Plan is the creation of more employment.” Yet within two years of its beginning, it became clear that employment could not be expected for eight million people. Three more years later, unemployment still grows. The number without work is estimated to have risen to sixty-three million.

At employment exchanges, the source reports that registered job-seekers increased between August 1957 and August 1958, though the first printed figure is damaged in OCR while the latter appears as 7,128,227. What does this rise demonstrate?

Unemployment cannot be called an urban problem alone. The cry that there is no work arises in villages too. Alongside it comes the demand for wages appropriate to the work.

The government says modern cultivation methods have been introduced in villages, small industrial enterprises begun, and cottage industries encouraged. According to these claims, should not villages appear newly polished? Instead, unemployment dances there. At conferences, officials boast that they did this and that for villages, but the people's wretched condition does not appear to have changed. Yet they pride themselves on having achieved wonders and expect the people to compose songs of victory in praise of those achievements.

Plans are drawn one after another. When implementation begins, many alterations are made out of fear that they will fail to produce results. Money is wasted through these many stages. Meanwhile the advice preached to the public is strange:

“Reduce what you eat. Work and work to increase production. Be satisfied by the statistics we give you.”

This propaganda has continued for ten years. The people obeyed everything they were told, yet their condition has plainly not risen. The government must understand that defective plans bring no good and attempt to remove their defects.

It failed to handle the food problem properly.

Economic inequality exists among different regions of the country. Consequently, even people who lack the courage to demand a separate country have begun demanding the rights of the South.

The foreign-exchange crisis continues.

There is not even an intention to procure essential goods for the people.

What other achievement can the government point to? Will people subjected to indescribable suffering refrain from asking what it has achieved?

We can emphasize one conclusion. So long as authority remains concentrated in the central government, plans implemented at a cost of crores will not yield results. Planning in Delhi must end, and plans must be framed at the state level.

The Delhi government should function only to establish connections between one state and another. It should possess no authority to make plans or distribute funds. Only when states are allowed to plan can schemes be designed to produce benefits. Only when Delhi's accumulated powers are transferred to the states can each state frame suitable plans according to its own available resources. State ministers must not be reduced to repeatedly travelling to Delhi to explain their needs and waiting for orders from central ministers.

Some time ago Pandit Nehru condemned the fascination with gigantic industries. We rejoiced that his thought was moving along a good path. If it continues, it will lead him to the correct conclusion that the Indian government as presently constituted is itself politically gigantic.

Whatever appears gigantic to Nehru in economics should appear the same in politics. Anything colossal creates fear rather than affection. That is why the people's cooperation and enthusiasm cannot be found.

Pandit Nehru must have the courage to distribute among the states the powers accumulated in the enormous Indian government. We assure him that such action would raise his stature many times higher.

Rashtrapati Bhavan and the Red Fort are not only famous; they stand as gigantic political structures. If Nehru attends to the lesson spoken by every stone that helped raise these celebrated and massive edifices, he will apply himself earnestly to building a new political arrangement. Within it, friendly countries will be joined by willing affection, and there will be no relation of master and slave.

Will the Pandit do this?

11 January 1959.

## Translator's Notes

`மேரு` refers to the cosmic Mount Meru and functions here as a metaphor for intimidating, over-centralized vastness. The English title retains “Meru” because the essay returns to the image of political gigantism in its conclusion.

The OCR of the employment statistics is internally damaged. It clearly compares registrations in August 1957 and August 1958, with the latter rendered as `7,128,227`; the English preserves the direction of the stated comparison without silently inventing the uncertain first number.

The Tamil source contains frequent OCR substitutions in `திட்டம்`, `நேரு`, `மாநிலம்`, and related words. Contextual meanings are translated, while the retained source block remains unchanged.

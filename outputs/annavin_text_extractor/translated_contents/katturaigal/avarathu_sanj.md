## English Translation

A palanquin! Ten or twelve poor men to carry it! Their cries of “Aah-ha! Ooh-ho!” to conceal their exhaustion! Men on horseback before and behind! A flag to announce, “He is coming, he is coming”; a drum; seven or eight carts holding the retinue and their belongings; and all the other trappings of ceremony. Even in this age there exists a “Supreme Brahman” who proceeds in such a procession!

He belongs to the direct succession of that merciful lord whose sacred lips blossom with the teaching: “Attachments and the promptings of karma bind us; only one who transcends them receives divine grace.” Yet suddenly he has developed unprecedented concern for this world and its material substance!

His legions of devotees reportedly went to him and complained: “There is no use if Your Holiness goes on speaking only about the next world. This world is steadily deteriorating. Atheists have spread everywhere. Unrighteousness dances openly. Rulers pay no respect to religious observance. We cannot simply let all this continue.” Reporting this appeal, the religious preceptor has sent a message to the world, not merely to this country.

*The Hindu* published that message prominently, neither abridging nor refuting it. The paper states that he issued the declaration from the Kodandaramaswami Temple in the village of Mudikondan.

At moments of great importance, statements from the American government issue from the White House and proclamations of the British government come from Downing Street. In the same manner, our spiritual leader has issued his message from the temple that serves as his headquarters.

“Cinemas have spread throughout the world.”

“Desire for earthly life has seized the people.”

“The practice of daily religious duties is disappearing.”

“Men and women have begun studying together in schools!”

“Vedic dharma is not being protected.”

He has poured many such grievances into the message. He also says that the government does nothing to stop these developments. In former times, we are told, there were kings who followed the path prescribed by religious sages. If grievances were taken to them, a remedy could be obtained. “But that no longer exists,” the spiritual luminary who issued the statement observes.

“The country has obtained self-rule. Accordingly, every person has become a king. It is therefore the government's duty to keep everyone on the proper path. To direct the government, one must enter politics,” the message says. He then continues. All sorts of people apparently go to him and complain every day: “The world is steadily deteriorating. The great one must take an interest. Your Holiness must become involved in government affairs.” They are said to trouble him greatly.

The Kamakoti Jagadguru Shankaracharya has issued this message announcing that people believe persons like him should take part in politics!

You will laugh on hearing it. Yet that “Supreme Brahman,” unconcerned about the slightest sense of shame or self-respect, has announced it.

At the very moment of making the announcement, however, fear or the anxiety “Can it be done? Is it possible?” seems to have intervened.

“People say that Acharya Swamigal should enter politics. He may do so. But then who will practise renunciation and preach devotion?” he says in the very next line of the same declaration.

We have heard it said that the Acharya is without attachment and builds a bridge between the Supreme Being and ordinary people. Yet this desire has arisen in one said to have renounced all earthly longings.

It is not simply the expression of this desire that makes us laugh most. When we see the reason for which he says his associates are troubling him to enter politics, mockery arises on one side and pain on the other.

The people, he says, have forgotten Maheshwara. They direct their minds toward the cinema. Men and women study together in colleges. Religious observance is being ruined!

“Can the people forget Mahesha's name? Can religious observance decline? Alas! Alas!” he panics and cries out.

The government, he says, must take measures to make people conduct themselves according to Vedic rites and to ensure that everything proceeds according to Sanatana Dharma. He insists that the government itself supervise these matters.

Shankaracharya travels in a palanquin. Milk and fruit are poured at his feet. Hunger is unknown to him; poverty cannot even approach him. Luxury and pleasure are his daily life. His anxiety is that religion and the Vedas are no longer honoured as before. Religion and scripture are the problems that command his thought. He declares that his multitudes of devotees ask him to take action, even by entering government, on their behalf.

At the very time when Shankaracharya is anxious about religion and God, the country has received another piece of news.

On the fourth of this month, the Madras High Court delivered judgment in a case.

A poor man accused of murder had been brought before the judges.

His motive for killing was not a long-standing feud. It was hunger. The cruelty of hunger made him a murderer. Those he killed were no strangers; they were his own children, two living portraits!

He was their father. He killed his own golden portraits.

Whom did he kill? An enemy? No.

One child was five years old and the other was two.

The poor man threw both children into a well.

It was not that he did not know how to fondle them and say, “Beautiful boy who came to banish desire! Portrait! Honeybee that hums while drinking from the flower! Image my heart awaited! Great blessing that came as a child into my lap!”

He had seen their tiny flower-like mouths smiling as they drank milk. He had watched with his own eyes when his beloved wife sang, “Essence of sugarcane! Fruit nectar! Close your eyes and sleep,” and lulled them to rest.

Children with golden faces and newly set sapphire eyes! He threw them into the well.

Garden flowers! Exquisite pictures! He picked them up and cast them away.

Golden moulds, his own children, became corpses.

Their little mouths that had babbled, “Appa! Appa!” screamed “Ah! Ah!” Their toddling legs thrashed. Even seeing this, that poor man became a beast.

He threw his bars of gold into the well.

He was their father, and he killed the children.

The reason was hunger. He came from the Malayalam country. His name was Kandan.

With his young wife and their two children, he came to Coimbatore district and wandered from place to place, not to steal, but to find work.

He roamed streets and villages everywhere asking whether work could be found.

He walked in front and his young wife followed, carrying one child in her arms and another on her shoulder. Choultries were their lodging houses and trees their homes of refuge from the sun.

They wandered, but found no work.

They starved not for one day or two, but for many days.

They could no longer bear the hunger. His wife became like a dry leaf. The tender young shoots withered and dried. They could endure the poverty no longer.

One night the poor man rose and tried to awaken his wife. She lay embracing the children.

He roused her, but she did not wake. He called, “Woman!” There was no answer. He shook her. Her body moved, but her eyes did not open. He called once, twice, ten times, many times. She did not open her eyes.

He was bewildered and his heart burned. He could not even bear the thought, “Has she died?” He did not cry, “What shall I do?” He knew they had starved for many days. He looked at the sky and sighed deeply. He lifted one child. A well stood nearby. He threw the child in. There was a great splash. The little parrot he had cradled, adorned, and raised sank beneath the water!

He returned for the other child. Now another great splash! The poor man's portrait became a corpse!

Corpses in the well! His wife below! Darkness ahead!

Soon afterward the poor man hung himself in order to become a corpse.

He tried to take his life. But even death betrayed him.

Neighbours heard the noise and came running. They saw the scene and wailed.

His wife, who had lain like a corpse in a faint brought on by hunger, awoke with a start. The children who had nestled against her were gone. She was bewildered. The cries rising nearby filled her with alarm. She rose and ran. She saw her husband, who had tried to become a corpse, and wailed: “Where are our children?”

He wept.

“Alas! Where are our precious ones?” she cried.

He did not speak. He looked toward the well.

“Sinner!” said those who had gathered.

“Alas!” screamed the mother.

The tears flowing from his eyes spoke. The heart of the woman who had borne and raised them throbbed. The motherly heart that had sung lullabies cried, “Alas!” He became a walking corpse.

That walking corpse was placed in the prisoner's dock. He did not deny the charge of murder. He looked at the judges, and his gaze became his advocate.

The judges themselves were moved.

The law required that walking corpse to receive imprisonment for life. But the judges reduced the sentence to one year and recommended clemency to the government.

What the judges wrote in their decision about this tearful scene is a portrait of present-day society:

“The cruelty of hunger has turned a father into a murderer. He also tried to destroy himself. This case reveals the poverty and cruel hunger prevailing in the country. Last week two similar cases came before us. In those cases too, because of poverty, parents killed their children and then attempted to hang themselves...”

The judges stated that cases like these appear frequently.

Hunger and poverty have assumed a monstrous form in the country and dance so fiercely that people kill their children.

A human being born to live becomes a corpse. He becomes a beast.

In this land of suffering, he struggles every second merely to carry life forward, struggles and struggles, and dies.

But Shankaracharya is anxious that devotional preaching should take place.

He travels in a palanquin. Religion has given him such a Kamakoti seat.

Poverty and want torment the people until they cry in despair, “Accursed Lord Shiva!” But Shankara, who knows nothing of hunger, is distressed that worship of Mahesha is not being performed properly.

This does not mean that Shankaracharya is unaware of the country's sufferings.

His mind may have grown numb from seeing the sweat of the poor palanquin bearers flow like a river while they are driven to run faster and faster. Yet he must know that monstrous events are multiplying, not through the eye of wisdom, but through the newspapers that publish his declarations.

Even so, the horrors in society have not captured his attention.

Death and murder appear ordinary.

Only the performance of *sandhyavandanam* and the decline in revenue from ritual offerings appear to be great problems.

The inequalities in society do not attract his mind.

Temple worship and the income of priests cause him concern.

There are people who proclaim Shankaracharya a divine birth. Others say, “No! No! He is an ordinary man. His sacred sports are endless!” That is not our question.

The poisonous condition of society is invisible to this great person who travels about calling himself a religious teacher.

But the moment he sees that the influence of the priests belonging to his own group is not rising, his voice grows loud. He knows how to raise both clamour and abuse.

This is the point that deserves thought.

Many matters that afflict the country become ordinary to him.

But the fact that his own group no longer possesses its former influence, and that this influence is declining, becomes momentous.

We are not therefore claiming that Shankaracharya ever possessed, or ought to possess, concern for public welfare.

Does this one simple matter not at least reveal what kind of people they are? Will those steeped in that same pool not understand it? That is our question.

Sanatana tradition, and especially the orthodoxy cherished by Brahmins, looks at the world solely through its own comforts and hardships.

It does not panic or lament when many people are injured. But if it suffers one small wound, it wants the whole world to cry out.

Shankaracharya is the guardian of this kind. That is why he offered one strange proposal in the same declaration:

“We shall support any party that believes in and worships God. Whichever party chants God's name 108 times every day, that is the party we shall support!”

One hundred and eight times every day! He will support the ascent to power of the party that chants God's name!

What a spectacle! What a marvel!

*(Dravida Nadu, 9 September 1951.)*

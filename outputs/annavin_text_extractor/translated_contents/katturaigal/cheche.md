## English Translation

An amusing incident! A few days ago, Premier Omandurar spoke in the strongest terms condemning the conduct of Public Works Department officials.

The Honourable Bhaktavatsalam is the minister in charge of that department.

Upon hearing the Premier speak so severely about his department, the Honourable Bhaktavatsalam offered no reply.

The *Mail* complained that it was improper for the Premier to speak critically in this fashion about a minister's department.

Like the story of beating a double rhythm to Ottakoothar's song, the Premier responded by reiterating his old accusation all over again.

What could people who read all this possibly think about the Public Works Department?

In the Legislative Assembly a member asked the Minister for Public Works, the Honourable Bhaktavatsalam: "The Premier spoke critically of the Public Works Department, and it appeared in the newspapers. What is your view of it?"

If the department truly deserved condemnation, the minister could have supported the Premier and said, "Yes, it is true! The scorching rebuke the Premier gave that department was justified. Let his condemnation reform it."

Or he could have said that the department was blameless and the Premier's statement incorrect, and thus defended his department's good name.

Doing neither, the Honourable Minister replied, "I did not read what appeared in the newspaper about it!"

What a spectacle! The speaker was the Premier. The subject spoken of was a department under this minister's control. Yet he says that he did not read what the newspapers reported about it!

Such is the minister's sense of responsibility.

Such is the Public Works Minister's sense of honour.

We urgently ask him to repair this attitude of his. A person seated in power on behalf of the people has a duty to discover what people think about his office and the work of his department. Only by finding that out can he assess the quality of his own service. What epithet are we to give a minister who makes no effort to learn whether what he does is just and acceptable to the people? Arrogant monarchs may imagine there is no need to know the people's opinion. Should an intelligent minister think so?

Are newspapers not there to reflect the people's mind? Is it a fitting answer, responsible speech, for one who has come to govern the country to say, "I did not read what the paper wrote about a matter concerning me"?

Did that member ask him what entertainment was showing at which cinema and whether he had seen it in the newspaper? Had that been the question, the minister might have thrust out his chest and said, "I do not read such things!" Then we too could happily have exclaimed, "Bravo! The minister gave him a proper rebuke."

But the member did not ask about a matter that could be dismissed. It was a matter that ought to have had the minister's attention: an opinion published in the press about his own department. Nor was it merely a newspaperman's opinion or that of the opposition. In the Legislative Assembly he was being asked about a view expressed by the Premier, a member of his own party. And what answer does this responsible minister give? "I did not read it!"

Such indifference! To the Honourable Minister these matters are evidently trivial. The Premier's words, words of condemnation concerning his own department, did not fall within his lofty gaze. Amid the Public Works Minister's many momentous duties, what significance could the Premier's rebuke possess? From drawing up schemes to build bridges to unveiling a singer's portrait, Bhaktavatsalam performs so very many tasks that the fact of the Premier criticising his department escaped his notice!

In this fine state of affairs, the Honourable Bhaktavatsalam is minister not only for Public Works but also for the Information Department, which exists to understand the people's mind and communicate the government's mind to them. Yet even he says that he did not read what appeared in the newspapers! Perhaps the minister thinks it enough to consult the almanac for an auspicious day to depart, open his diary to record receptions, and open the ledger to write down so much income and so much expenditure.

Minister, a minister's duties do not end there! Trying to understand the temper of the country's people is also an important duty. Your Premier spoke publicly about your department, and you say, "I did not read it." Do not be angry: mindful of your office, no one may ask you anything to your face. But everyone will say, "What on earth is this fellow doing without even reading this? Is he turning over mountains?" When you find some leisure, consider all the things people will say on hearing your reply.

"Why is there so little salt?"

"I didn't notice, sir."

"What other work does his lordship have? If you don't even notice whether the salt and spice are right, what are you doing in the kitchen?"

Minister, have you never spoken in this fashion to your cook?

When a chauffeur drove fast and was about to collide with another vehicle, have you not said:

"What is wrong with your eyes? Can't you even see what is coming towards you? Why come to drive a motorcar at all, you blind fellow?"

You treat the people of the country and the Premier with just such indifference. You have no concern for the people's mind or the Premier's considered opinion. You have no time to read the journals that publish them; you do not think of doing so or regard it as necessary. Perhaps you imagine that ministerial work forbids reading such things, lest your status be diminished. It is unbecoming, Minister! This conduct is unbecoming, improper, and unintelligent.

Do not dismiss it with the contemptuous thought, "Tsk, tsk! Must I read all this?" If you do, the people too will think, "Tsk, tsk! Must all these people be our ministers?" That would not be a good state of affairs. I say this as a friend: while carrying on your ministerial work, pay some attention to the country as well!

(*Dravida Nadu*, 25 July 1948)

## Translator's Notes

The title appears in the OCR as “சீ செச்சே! இதை எல்லாமா பயப்பது!”, but the article's closing refrain and the scan's argument establish the intended verb as படிப்பது (“to read”). The Tamil source is retained verbatim. “மராமத்து இலாக்கா” is rendered by its historical administrative equivalent, Public Works Department; “பிரசார இலாக்கா” is rendered Information Department in its governmental context.

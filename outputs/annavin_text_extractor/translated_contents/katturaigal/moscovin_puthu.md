## English Translation

“Return to them the respect they show you. If they err, rebuke them. If they work enthusiastically, never fail to praise them.”

“You are at headquarters. You are the heart of the party camps in every corner of the country. Form a clear picture in your mind of what is happening everywhere.”

“Complete the task you undertake. You have dedicated your life to the party. From now on, your life will be spent among the public; never forget it. Discipline! Unrelenting effort! Work always; do not yearn for rest.”

“Learn who loves you and who reviles you, but behave toward both with awareness of your responsibility. Showing hostility to some and excessive affection to others creates danger within a party.”

“Many will not suddenly begin praising you. Your reputation and advancement depend upon the service you render the party.”

Malenkov! When the question “Who can fill Stalin's place?” throbbed in every heart, his was an unfamiliar name. Today he occupies the great warrior's seat—as leader of twenty crore people and head of one of the world's great “camps.”

Those who know his life will understand the importance of the advice given above.

Malenkov has passed the age of bounding youth and is entering later life. Many do not know his biography, but it is clear how he grew capable of filling Stalin's place.

The words above were Malenkov's guiding mantras.

Countries that speak of democracy offer ways to scatter money, display pomp, obtain votes and thereby rise as “leaders.” The owner of an insurance company, one who amasses money through a garden of newspapers, a banker, an industrial magnate or landlord may become a leader and gain an opportunity to grasp the reins of government.

Russia is not such a country, where people hunt members' ballots to become leaders or display a habit of shaking platforms and become members of a legislative council through a party.

Only one who laboured for the party and used that labour to accomplish constructive tasks could live as a leader and receive praise. One had to protect the party, endure hardship, run about and work, and carry out the leadership committee's assignments. Only by labouring thus and earning the praise and good wishes of party comrades could one become foremost among them.

If jasmine grew among cactus plants, one could easily say, “This is cactus; this is jasmine.” But how can one jasmine distinguish itself within a jasmine garden? It is not easy. In the Russian Communist organisation, everyone is like jasmine. To shine in such a flower garden, opportunity alone is insufficient; labour is required.

Malenkov rose through such labour.

“He is the leader's right hand, so he has influence in the party.”

“Need a permit or licence? Catch hold of him and the business will be done, for he is the head of government's associate.”

“This man is my friend. Therefore, party members, I appoint him leader after me. Accept my heir.”

Comrade Malenkov was not made an “heir” in any of these ways. He did not become leader merely because Stalin favoured him. His labour, tireless effort and suffering for the party brought him to leadership of party and government.

He was a little schoolboy of twelve when the First World War began. He was fifteen when the Russian Revolution overthrew the Tsar. Thus he heard of, rather than personally endured, the Tsarist cruelties experienced by Stalin and Lenin. Yet today he fills Stalin's place.

It is natural for people's attachment and love to settle upon those who shed blood for the country, endured cruelty and languished in prison. Those who suffered such hardship and had such an opportunity can readily gain a place in public hearts.

Malenkov had no such opportunity. Yet he fills Stalin's place and is celebrated unanimously. His labour and the effort he devoted to building his homeland alone brought him here. He was not manufactured as a leader; he grew into one.

He spent his life within the machinery of the party and thereby earned the qualification to approach Stalin closely.

He joined the Communist Party in 1920, when the revolution had triumphed and new government blossomed. From then onward, the quality of his service within the party was high and excellent.

Georgy Maximilianovich Malenkov saw the great revolution at fifteen. He developed immeasurable affection for the party that conducted it with a sacred purpose. He joined the Communist Party and became a soldier in the Red Army, then studied engineering and graduated in 1925.

The young graduate did not intend merely to live by his education. Carrying his degree, he went directly to the Communist Party office. From that day, it became his home.

Having entered party headquarters, he established contact with each department. Through those coming and going, he learned about conditions throughout the Soviet Union. In 1930 he became Moscow district secretary. Within four years he was placed in a highly responsible headquarters post.

His work travelling from town to town, ability to complete assigned duties properly, and above all his concern for the homeland's welfare attracted Stalin and party members. He became one of those who formulated party policy.

When the Second World War began, he assumed responsibility as a member of the State Defence Committee. It was a time of great crisis. Germany, which had maintained friendly relations, suddenly began destroying Moscow. Nazi aircraft attacked daily.

Defeating the Nazis required the production of armaments and a rapid increase in aircraft manufacture. Malenkov courageously accepted responsibility for this department, taking charge of transport and industry. His engineering training now proved highly useful.

Factories that had produced public necessities were turned to accumulating war materiel. Aircraft were built rapidly. Nazi pilots destroyed wheat fields and great cities. Göring crowed and Hitler laughed at Russia's suffering. The world began saying joyfully, “Victory there; the Nazi army advances and may enter Moscow tomorrow.”

The Nazi forces advanced in delight. Allowing them to revel in “victory,” the great warrior Stalin concentrated upon producing military aircraft, tank forces and artillery.

Malenkov, administering that responsibility, said, “Do not be disturbed, leader.” Factories ran day and night and materiel accumulated.

Reports from commanders always said, “The equipment is insufficient.”

“What answer shall we send?” Stalin would ask.

“Say, ‘Very well,’” Malenkov would reply.

However the factories had to turn and however their workers had to labour, materiel continued accumulating. A great share of the credit for saving Russia from Nazi fanatics belongs to him. He could be called the father of the Russian air force; through him it became powerful. The Russian air force was a major cause of Berlin's fall. Red Army soldiers took twofold vengeance upon the Nazi group that had burned them.

After the war, Malenkov was chosen Deputy Chairman of the Council of Ministers. Although many were older, they selected him. He accepted the immense task of restoring Russia's economy after wartime devastation.

He worked greatly to create new industrial prosperity and restore people's pre-war standard of living.

In the Russian way of life, ensuring the proper distribution to every family of the goods it required was exceedingly difficult. Even a small error in that department was unbearable. He supervised it too.

So many responsibilities, all severe, yet he performed them with a smile.

Party comrades reportedly surrounded him constantly, and conversing and debating with them formed his recreation. That a man carrying such a workload allotted time for this too demonstrates his capacity for action. That is why today he leads one of the world's great new blocs.

Neither money, pomp nor publicity brought him to so distinguished a position. It was genuine service, elevated labour, attachment to his homeland and eagerness to defend party principles.

Those who desire the “fragrance of Moscow” in their own homelands must study Comrade Malenkov's life closely.

More than political parties that roam with capitalist programmes, progressive parties—the camps of builders of a new world fighting those programmes—can learn many truths from Malenkov's life.

Money is the fundamental strength of capitalist parties. The strength of progressive parties is their commitment to their principles.

Money and commitment now collide. Though money may appear victorious, parties that labour with commitment gain influence in the people's forum.

Money cannot approach the people; commitment mingles with them.

Political workers who serve with this commitment as their fundamental strength must reinforce the relationship it creates with the public and conduct themselves like Malenkov.

Commitment alone is not enough. It is insufficient merely to cry passionately, “Ah! Shall my homeland become enslaved? Shall I lie watching?” One must labour too.

Commitment and genuine labour—these two made Malenkov a jewel among human beings.

This is the counsel Moscow's new light offers political workers throughout the world.

We welcome Malenkov's leadership and salute him: may Malenkov, who attained world leadership through genuine service, live and flourish!

*Dravida Nadu*, 15 March 1953

## English Translation

*Ravana Kaviyam*: are you startled? Naturally so. Have not the people read the *Ramayana*, or stood beside readers and listened to it, for ages? The very sound of “Ravana Kaviyam” will therefore cause some alarm in their ears.

Yet this book is not unexpected. It may be called a product of the age, a sign of inquiry, or a spark of revolution. Those who recognised the new awakening in the people's minds knew that a book like this must appear. The revolutionary poet Bharathidasan announced it years ago:

> I look towards the South. What shall I say?  
> All my thoughts and shoulders swell with pride!

Like the revolutionary poet's new verse, *Ravana Kaviyam* is a portrait of the new Tamil Nadu. Its purpose is to remove the harm caused by earlier texts, examine them in accordance with present thought, and give them a new form. To those attached to the old form, it will appear a terrible storm. Its creator knew that; anyone who knows human nature will not be surprised.

There is no use regarding the production of *Ravana Kaviyam* in a country celebrating Rama Navami as a marvel. It should be read as an announcement that antiquity is dying. The conditions created by the Tsar's cruelty alone could produce a Lenin. Because Lenin appeared, the man whom the nation had worshipped as God's son came to be regarded as a demon sucking the people's blood. Without the Tsar, Lenin would neither have appeared nor been necessary.

Likewise, had people not continued promoting the *Ramayana*, painting its scenes and writing articles about it, this new epic would not have arisen. One is the consequence of the other, and more than a consequence: one is the other's refutation.

Rama was made a god, and to make him divine Ravana was made a demon. Because a temple required an icon, the poet placed the glorious Kodanda bow in the hand of Rama, who ought merely to have been described as a victorious king's son, and endowed the envoy's tail with the miraculous power to lengthen at will. To make bow and tail objects of worship, a king of strong shoulder and mind was made a barbarian. The poet gave Rama lotus-red eyes and therefore portrayed Ravana's eyes as emitting red flame. His purpose was to make Rama divine, and he invented the story accordingly.

*Ravana Kaviyam* arose to cast away the base qualities and cruel acts earlier poets loaded upon Ravana, offer many grounds for his integrity, and clarify faults visible in Rama's character and conduct. In an age when Rama temples stand everywhere, does such an attempt not require great courage? The author possesses it fully. Unlike earlier poets, he does not seek Aryan praise or garlands and honours from kings. He believes it is a Tamil's dignity to tell the people's assembly the truth present in his mind. He belongs to the Self-Respect movement. Thus, supported not by questions such as “Is it proper, fitting, acceptable?” but by the certainty that truth will prevail today or tomorrow, he has written this book because it had to be said.

Were Rama and Ravana real figures of historical times? No, they are inventions. The Self-Respect movement does not hesitate to say so. Merchants in classical Tamil call the *Ramayana* imaginary only when unable to answer our questions. At other times they think of, esteem and worship Rama as a real person. Their devotional mentality diminishes slightly only inside the research hall; everywhere else they remain servants of Rama.

Self-Respecters are not servants of Ravana. This epic was not composed so they might build him a temple, perform consecration and become priests. Ancient poets burdened Ravana with blame. This book was written after asking whether that was proper and finding, upon close examination, that the matter was entirely different.

It is not a reply from servants of Ravana to servants of Rama. It is medicine from self-respecting Tamils to cure the servants of Rama of delusion. It advises: “Tamil, do not live as a servant. The title ‘servant of Rama’ serves only to make you a servant of Aryans.”

The *Ramayana* was composed when two different cultures began meeting across this vast region. Those who understand culture, social psychology and national conditions have recognised this truth.

No one denies that Aryan culture was sprinkled into the beautiful life of the Dravidian people. We and our opponents do not differ on that point, because it has become impossible to conceal.

They too say Dravidian and Aryan cultures existed separately and then mingled. Smiling, they add that the mixture is now too complete to distinguish one from the other. Their desire is not merely that no distinction be made. Their own research clearly reveals that, in later times, Aryan elements were mixed into Dravidian culture with the utmost caution and skill precisely so they could not be separated: theft impossible to detect, adultery conducted unseen, a serpent biting without sound.

But this is the age of excavating buried things and discovering Mohenjo-daros, an age that has found easy means to know countless matters once thought beyond human intelligence. Therefore the claim that Aryan and Dravidian elements are inseparably mixed can now be overturned. That is the cause of the opponents' anger.

They worry: “For so long we laboured in every fashion to diminish and damage Dravidian art, to attach and weave Aryan elements into it, to paste and sprinkle them until they were mixed. Yet somehow these sinners have found the trail. They probe, sift and assay it, separating and measuring what is Aryan and what is Dravidian. Disaster has now overtaken all our labour!” Will the speed of time stop because they worry? Prohibition and organised opposition only increase it.

*Ravana Kaviyam* must be regarded as the fruit of this state of mind. Like the earlier *Ramayana*, it too is a war drum in a cultural struggle. Both are inventions. The former makes Rama divine; this does not make Ravana divine but makes him Tamil, that is, heroic.

The earlier poet had to enlist swords and heavenly beings. This book needs neither. From matters buried in the earlier work itself, its author draws Ravana's form. The old text describes a war with Sambara and frightens us by calling him an asura. In *Ravana Kaviyam*, Sambara is not a demon but a Pandya. On what basis? The earlier text calls Sambara “the one with the fish flag.” The fish flag belongs to the Pandya. Therefore, this book concludes that Sambara, whom the old author abused as an asura, must have been a Pandya king. In the same manner, the evidence for many events in this epic is sifted from the *Ramayana* itself.

Aryans and admirers of Aryanism who wish to escape *Ravana Kaviyam* have only one path: declare that the *Ramayana* itself is false and that they reject it. There is no other route.

Some may say, “Leave the story aside; what of epic taste? Does it not overflow in Kambar's *Ramayana*?” Let that be so. Why fear? This troublesome child does not allow them to say that Aryan Rama shines in beautiful poetry while his critics speak crude Tamil. Pulavar Kuzhanthai has said what needed saying without diminishing epic flavour.

The river flows as beautifully here as there. Nature is adorned with Tamil ornaments in *Ravana Kaviyam* as brilliantly as in Kambar. There, divine intervention at times injures Tamil sweetness; here even that defect is absent. The former was composed for scholars in difficult Tamil mixed with Aryan vocabulary. This was written in simple, sweet, independent Tamil for all Tamil people.

Consider a few examples.

### 1. Sunrise

Kambar depicts the sun as though it had hidden in fear of Ravana's cruelty and emerged only upon seeing Rama's protecting army:

> Thinking it hard to appear before the cruelty  
> of the merciless and mighty one, the sun suffered;  
> then, as the joyous lost their joy and protectors came in sight,  
> the hidden one appeared and made the dawn.

*Kamba Ramayanam*, “Arraying the Army,” verse 24.

*Ravana Kaviyam* instead pictures the young-rayed sun arriving as though counting that two days had passed since mighty warriors fought, won, fell upon the earth, and gave their lives:

> Dispelling the vast dark, the lord of tender rays arose,  
> as though he thought: “It is now two days  
> since strength met strength in battle, victory passed onward,  
> and great warriors rolled upon the earth and died.”

*Ravana Kaviyam*, “Second Battle,” verse 1.

### 2. Sunset

Kambar says the sun saw the many lightning-bright gems that his own son Sugriva had struck from ten-faced Ravana's victorious crown and disappeared behind a solitary mountain, as if hiding Ravana after that defeat.

*Kamba Ramayanam*, “Breaking the Crown,” verse 41.

*Ravana Kaviyam* asks whether night came because the sun could not bear to look upon the red battlefield spread like a sea, a forest of heaped corpses bathed in blood:

> Unable to behold the crimson field spread like a sea,  
> where heaps of corpses rose in a forest bathed in blood,  
> the sun withdrew. Was it then  
> that night arrived to hold its court?

*Ravana Kaviyam*, “First Battle,” verse 77.

Examples could multiply without end. Pulavar Kuzhanthai has fulfilled the formal requirements of a great epic. Poetic flavour, rhythm, simile and ornament are beautifully arranged. With love, chastity, modes of war, valour and other subjects described in classical interior and exterior poetics, *Ravana Kaviyam* can serve as a feast for those enchanted by Tamil sweetness.

It is an undeniable truth that the *Ramayana* served Aryan domination. Pandit Nehru himself mentioned this in letters to his daughter. That was the work's purpose.

Comrade Pulavar Kuzhanthai desires that Tamils and the Tamil people awaken, rise with courage, obtain liberation, and live as a heroic people possessing the right to autonomous independent rule. He has therefore employed his intelligence, research, experience, Tamil learning and poetic scholarship towards that aim.

In the epic he elaborates Tamil Nadu, Tamil, Tamil character, the Aryans' arrival, the Tamils' support for them, their hidden purpose, methods of espionage, harmful intention and evil action.

In brief, this book is a travel ticket for antiquity and an admission ticket for modernity; a powerful weapon driving away the slander that Self-Respecters are enemies of Tamil, ignorant of epic taste and devoid of artistic feeling; a foremost fragrant flower of the Tamil renaissance; a portrait combining long research, subtle scholarship and attachment to the people; a war drum for the Tamil's new life; a challenge to those who boast, “We inserted Aryanism into epic form, so it can never be destroyed”; a loving call asking Tamils to recognise truth; a foundation stone for Tamil rule; and a song of liberation.

I convey my gratitude to Pulavar Kuzhanthai for writing this rare work with great difficulty and the noble aim that Tamil self-respect should flourish. I assure him that our Tamil people will give it immense support. You who are Tamils, honour and support this lucid Tamil epic created for you, and fulfil that hope. Greetings.

Kanchipuram  
Your affectionate friend,  
**Annadurai**  
**15 September 1946**

## Translator's Notes

This article is Anna's research preface to Pulavar Kuzhanthai's *Ravana Kaviyam*. `Rama Navami` celebrates Rama's birth. `Kodanda` is Rama's bow. `Kamba Ramayanam` is Kambar's Tamil retelling of the *Ramayana*. The terms `akam` and `puram` refer broadly to the interior/love and exterior/public-war domains of classical Tamil poetics.

The Tamil verse excerpts are preserved verbatim in the source block. Their English renderings prioritise the explanatory sense supplied by the article and do not attempt to reproduce metre. The source glosses `thuppu` as strength, `paravai` as sea, and related poetic compounds.

The essay's claims about Aryan and Dravidian cultures, the historic purpose of the *Ramayana*, Sambara's Pandya identity, and Nehru's remarks are translated as the author's argument. They require independent source criticism before historical use. `Mohenjo-daros` reflects the source's plural rhetorical usage of Mohenjo-daro discoveries.

## English Translation

He was no terrorist. He was a singer! Yet people were prevented from hearing him sing. When he raised his sweet voice in song, it produced the feeling of frost touching a flower and the joy of sprouting wings and circling the arch of the sky. Even the cuckoo was defeated by the sweetness of his voice. Even stone melted before the flood of song. But wrongdoers whose hearts were harder than stone tried to erect a barbed-wire fence.

Struck by obstinate arrogance and barren prestige, they attempted to impose a prohibition upon the people. The jasmine may be beautiful, but it has blossomed in an opponent's garden; therefore it has no fragrance, and even if it does, you must not enjoy it. But will the heart obey? Like bees seeking honey, the minds of the American people settled upon that singer's sweet music.

If he was to sing, many thousands gathered. Men and women, young and old, ran asking, “Where? Where?” Neither ridicule, mockery nor voices of condemnation restrained the people's minds. On the contrary, they aroused curiosity about what could possibly be present in his songs. People began going in their lakhs. If he was singing, they moved like a flood that had broken its banks. Just as sandalwood releases more fragrance the more it is ground, the more he was obstructed, the more people gathered towards him.

Those who imposed the prohibition disliked it. They could not bear to see a musician shine without equal. The next stage began. Wherever he performed in America, people forced their way in and began wrecking the place. Stages were torn apart. Loudspeakers were destroyed. Bombs were thrown at the audience. A condition arose in which not a single concert could take place without the police!

There would be some meaning in obstruction if the singer did not know how to sing, murdered music, sang obscene songs and cultivated indecency. But magnanimity resounded in his singing, dignity shone in its sweetness, and he stood like a vessel filled to the brim. He knew not only the music of America but the subtleties of music flourishing in many parts of the world.

“What music do you want? French? I shall sing it. Polish music? Here it is. Norwegian songs? I know them. A Spanish dance theme? I know it well. A Russian theme? Listen, I shall sing. Do you want Hebrew? I know it well. The English national anthem? Do not imagine I am ignorant of it. I even know Indian music!” He knew music in many languages of the world.

Should not such an artist be cherished like the apple of one's eye? Is it not a matter of pride for a country to bestow praise and honours upon an incomparable artist and say, “Such a man lives among us”? One might object that although he knew all this music, opening his mouth sounded like the braying of a donkey; what use was multilingual knowledge if crudity and discord resounded in his voice, and would discerning people desire it? But not only did he know every musical method; beyond that, his voice itself was sweet. The sound of the veena, the sweetness of the flute, the rustle of the breeze and the taste of a stream of honey all shone in his voice. Even so, they opposed, attacked, obstructed and created disputes!

Do you know why? That singer, named Paul Robeson, was a Black man!

According to the dictionary of white racists living in America, he was an untouchable and a slave. They praise the red lotus though it grows from mud. They take a pearl and delight in wearing it though it is born in a shell. But they refused to value a great artist who arose from an oppressed community. They opposed him, tried to suppress him, and displayed violence and rage.

The world heard his song and marvelled. Every country longed to possess an artist like him. But racists sought to eliminate him and silence his voice. Through racist organisations, the Ku Klux Klan, they made strenuous efforts to prevent his voice from reaching anyone's ears. Every attempt was made to cover him with a curtain, and every campaign that could call him a villain was set in motion. Yet his music began sounding in many parts of the world, tearing through the darkness.

The peacock dances, the cuckoo rejoices, the coloured dove flies, and even the snake is enchanted. Yet human beings endowed with six senses alone began to seethe: “Should he receive so much distinction?”

Can rage and resentment suppress ability and truth? He sang. He sang across the barriers. He turned the outrages of those who obstructed him into songs. Through music he revealed the injustices inflicted upon him because he was Black. He became a man of feeling who made known the wrongs inflicted upon Black people by racist whites. He used his art as an instrument to express the longing for freedom surging in his heart.

Black people rejoiced. The racists were shaken. He was no longer merely an artist: by using art to explain the condition of his people, he became not only a singer but a symbol of liberation. To reveal his people's oppressed condition, the singer also became an actor. His music and performance did more than delight spectators; they spread ideas that exposed the suffering of Black labourers in the land inhabited by the followers of Jesus.

The sixtieth-birthday celebration of such a Paul Robeson was observed throughout the world on the ninth of this month.

It was celebrated with particular distinction in the Indian subcontinent. A committee was formed with government support to conduct it. We were astonished when we saw its membership: distinguished people including Bombay High Court Justice Chagla, Dr C. V. Raman, Dr C. P. Ramaswami Iyer and Dr Rajamannar all served upon it.

Prime Minister Nehru took a keen interest in the celebration. Moreover, in the proud message he issued, he observed: “By honouring him, we support the principle he holds. That principle is that inequality must not exist.” It is improper to refuse to value a human being on the basis of high and low distinctions, or to acquire the audacity to call even a ruby a stone.

“I care little what the country's rulers and white lords think about me and my views. For more than the past ten years they have tormented me in every possible way. They have vilified me, unleashed frenzied mobs and engaged in violence. They even deny my right to practise my profession as an art. They continue to prevent me from travelling abroad. Yet my voice will be heard everywhere!” the great artist declared with passion.

The American government refused him permission to travel to the Indian subcontinent and England. The reason was simply that he loved freedom!

However much they cover it with a curtain, unleash repression and reject him as an oppressed man, rulers of domination cannot prevent the fragrance arising there, whether it is the fragrance of art or intellect. No one can obstruct the fragrance of art infused with a longing for freedom. When feeling and art unite, they inevitably reach even the most inaccessible places. That is why Nehru too formed a committee here and honoured that king of art.

Everyone will praise Nehru's act of attempting to give respect and honour to a dove of liberation. We praise it too. But why does Nehru not feel the same enthusiasm and affection towards artists of liberation here? Distress arises when he does not even ask that they at least be spared torment like the sting of a scorpion, even if no committee is formed to honour them.

When many artists here dedicate themselves and their professional skill to their language and people, they are mocked as “mere performers” and “play-actors.” Through prohibitions and censorship, attempts are made to throttle their voices. When we imagine how many artists might flourish here, free of stigma and blame, if the generosity that praises Paul Robeson arose within our own country too, our hearts cannot help but grieve.

Nevertheless, Robeson's celebration gives us confidence that artists who serve the country and their people will one day be honoured by the world, even if today they receive only abuse. Though born among an oppressed people, Paul Robeson not only sought distinction for himself and his community through his art; he also shines as a star of hope for oppressed people throughout the world. At this time, we too offer him our greetings.

Long live his courage of heart! May his resolve shine as a lamp of light for artists who use their art for the welfare of oppressed people!

(News item, *Dravida Nadu*, 1-4-28)

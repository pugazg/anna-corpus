## English Translation

We exultantly proclaim that the festival of the cultivator is our new day. We rejoice in saying that this celebration exists to offer thanks to that great multitude. Yet we neither forget nor conceal the deterioration in the standard of living of those engaged in agriculture, nor the web of circumstances in which they remain trapped.

We speak of advancing agriculture. At the same time, we fearlessly explain the cruelty by which that occupation itself ruins the cultivators, and insist that this condition must change.

Our hearts ache when we see children who ought to be studying, running and playing instead carrying stones and grass, tending cattle, bearing loads, treading mud and gleaning paddy, their stomachs drawn in and their eyes dried out.

The degrading condition in which the cultivator labours without rest merely so that a few may enjoy an idle life of luxury must change. The cruelty by which cultivators are left uneducated and confined to hamlets so that new universities may be built for the rest of us must be abolished. The notion that they must remain worshippers of Maadan, Kaadan, Kaatteri, Malaiyandi, Idumban and Maakali so that we may establish scientific institutions and enjoy the pleasures of modern life must end. The arrogance of those who imagine that only while these voiceless beings remain in the villages can professors, great scholars, authors, intellectual workers, arguing lawyers, poets and painters, rulers, and mendicant-robed kings who manipulate rulers live splendidly in the cities must be suppressed. Whenever we praise the day when milk boils over, extol the plough, and celebrate Pongal as our new festival, we never fail to sing the battle-song demanding this change.

After a long sleep, the cultivator has awakened and begun to rise with vigour.

Rebellion beside the fields, violence on the bunds, blows on the threshing floor, and force in the village! Affection for the possessor of the treasury, but fear at the anger of the poor: the ruler's mind has been caught in this vice.

“O Time! Do you not see our tears? Will you not grant justice?” the cultivators asked in anguish. Time gave them revolutionary ideas.

Across the world, systems handed down from generation to generation have been abolished beyond recognition. Estates have been divided. The domination of landlords has been destroyed. The conviction has spread everywhere that cooperative farming can increase national wealth and raise the worker's standard of living, while uprooting the very class that lives by exploitation. Even those who continue to trust in Kasi and Gaya cannot escape the arrows shot by Time. Though unwilling at heart, rulers have been driven by fear of a great revolution into accepting at least nominal reforms of landed property.

The zamindari system was abolished. Drums were beaten. The good news was announced to the world: there would be no forest king holding court, no tyrant tying people up and beating them, no one violating a beautiful woman. Arrogance had been subdued; there could be no more grievance. Zamindari was gone, agriculture would flourish, and joy would spring from the cultivator's heart, they proclaimed.

Zamindari was abolished, but the zamindar became a mill owner, merchant prince, moneylender and magnate. The poor man's condition and the cultivator's standard of living continued as before, in the old world where salt cost a quarter anna and onions half an anna.

The landlord who tied Kaathaan to a tree and ordered him whipped with a tamarind switch may have disappeared; the hard-hearted man who lends money only to squeeze the debtor has not. To a great extent, the same lord merely assumed this new form.

The white man relinquished offices such as Viceroy, Governor and Collector, allowing people to savour the news that foreign rule had ended and self-government had arrived. Yet he could remain here as plantation owner, nominal director, industrial magnate, financier, official and man of influence. In the same way, zamindars abandoned their brocaded caps, beatings, booming voices and intimidating looks, but their wealth continued to serve as an instrument of domination. Entering new fields, they made the lives of the poor their plaything.

The agitation, disorder, turmoil and bloodshed that had not arisen even during the zamindars' tyranny appeared among those who still tilled the soil, suffered and remained poor after zamindari was abolished. The rulers were startled. They had been proud that abolition was a vast revolutionary measure ending an ancient cruelty, and had imagined that people would build temples to them and bow before them. Now they found themselves shaken by an uprising led by the plough. They came determined to suppress it, saw signs that this would not work, and became bewildered. Pretending to grant rights gladly, they enacted tenancy legislation. They assumed there was no longer anything to fear, and that the flavour of a law written on paper would suffice for simple people accustomed to oxen.

But reality refuses to comply. The agitation makes a mockery of restrictive rules. The law dallies with the wealthy; when we approach it, it steps away and ridicules us. Why should we invite needless trouble? We till and send the produce away; the owner eats and grows fat. We suffer so that lords may savour the fruit. We stand in the field and by the canal while the ostentatious wealthy drink strained fruit juice in silk and sing as they drink. The titleholders possess the land; we remain in the cattle shed while they stay in garden residences. We hold the plough; they tour in cars. We harvest; they take the fruit. Why should this continue? So long as it does, the cultivators will ceaselessly demand a fundamental change and agitate for the principle that the land belongs to the tiller.

“Hereafter you will receive more than half the yield. Is that not right? Sixty percent for you and only forty percent for the owner. Are you not satisfied?”

The rulers expect the cultivators to dance with delight and say, “This is enough, my lord! This is enough! We have performed penance all these years only to obtain this.” They are headed for disappointment.

A day will come, even after the demand “land to the tiller” has been implemented, when a great truth will strike the cultivator like a calamity: land alone cannot provide all the means and opportunities required for life.

Today both sides see and understand only the hunger for land. But, like the proverb about half a palmyra fruit being a joy, only after the cultivator has been given land and told that it will by itself fill his life with comforts will the truth press upon him: whether for an individual or for the whole country, believing that land alone can supply every necessity of life is folly and a grave danger.

When the population was limited and life's needs could be counted on one's fingers, it was indeed said that none equalled the person who lived by tilling, and that no occupation equalled agriculture.

Saints, ascetics, preachers and noble men appeared in ranks and offered beautiful instruction. “We eat but a measure and wear four cubits of cloth; we brought nothing at birth and take nothing at death; the contented mind is an alchemy that makes gold,” they said.

What has their teaching produced today? Fort-like temples and monasteries, golden roofs and tiles, and halls studded with nine kinds of gems! Desire was supposedly subdued. It was said that only enough food to unite body and life was required in the search for the Pure One: fruit, unripe fruit, fallen leaves, roots or tubers would sustain life, since human birth existed only to reach the feet of God; all here was illusion, everything visible a bubble on water or a rainbow in the long sky. If this truth was understood and property and claims over it were unwanted, why were laws made to protect such rights, and guards, courts and prisons created to enforce those laws? We do not hear the people within temples and religious institutions answer this. Cases over what is yours and what is mine reach the High Court. Courts inquire whether a woman who came to gather refuse was embraced, while villagers say that no answer is found there. If this is the state of the very seats of preaching, how can anyone claim that the people who heard the sermons subdued desire and discovered contentment to be the golden medicine?

Day by day, the necessities of life will grow. Intellectuals call this the very sign of civilisation.

Could anyone speak more warmly and sincerely than Gandhi about the excellence of the village and the merit of a simple life? Yet does Pandit Nehru go to Sabarmati, Babu Rajendra Prasad to Haripura, and Abul Kalam Azad to Tripura, live in huts, and guide the people from there? Can they do so? No.

In every country of the world, the plough and the ox were once regarded as dependable wealth. As populations increased, however, agriculture alone could no longer produce enough wealth and provide a comfortable life. Industry became necessary; machinery was needed to support industry; and scientific institutions were required to acquire the knowledge that would produce machinery.

Therefore, celebrating Pongal as the new day and the cultivator's festival does not mean that we sing special verses claiming a country can achieve every prosperity by depending on agriculture alone. The country must become a centre rich in many industries. Experts tell us that natural wealth lies buried, submerged, covered over and concealed by thorny scrub. Industrial planning is needed to make it all useful to the people. Experts say Neyveli lignite and Salem iron can provide resources capable of transforming the country's very appearance. The rulers say “yes” too, but after saying so they cannot improve our condition and bow their heads in shame. The reason is that they have no authority.

On a festival day we hold back the tears forming in our eyes and watch our children taste red sugarcane. We see the young wife who brought us that treasure, the beauty of the home shaped by her hands, the fire in the kitchen sending up smoke that darkens its whitewashed walls, and rice poured into cow's milk to cook Pongal. Children run and play amid cries of “Pongalo Pongal,” as the fragrance of new clothes and fresh flowers mingle. They are caught, kissed, embraced and delighted over. People joyfully ask which maiden is suitable for a young man, who taught a girl her music, and from whom she learnt her dance. Such scenes exist in some homes.

Our purpose can only be that homes like these should shine everywhere in our country. Is there any higher principle than the dignity and prosperity of family life?

The cultivator worked and was ruined; worked and surrendered the fruit; worked and stood in anguish on finding that the return did not even satisfy hunger. He continues to labour, yet we see no means by which his life may improve.

Our own happiness is not complete unless we see others happy. Gold bangles, silver anklets and flashing diamonds are the products of money; a smile may be called the product of character. A beloved woman, graceful as a creeper, serves food to her young husband on a silver plate. Infatuated with her, he looks at her before bringing a morsel to his mouth, takes her hand, and asks whether this is the feast he requested. Seeing a little child toddle toward them, she laughs and tells him to watch his step: “That mischievous child is your sister.” Having given him some imagined expectation, she points out the white Pongal, the relish that heightens its taste, a sweet, and jackfruit soaked in honey. He playfully asks the red-coral-lipped maiden whether she will give him honeyed fruit. An unmarried girl protests that she has never seen or heard of such a miraculous “coral fruit.” The sister-in-law smiles and tells her that if she escapes this year, a husband will come next year and ask for both coral fruit and pearl-like teeth; then she will understand. Calling her sister-in-law clever and a great enchantress, the girl runs away singing a teasing song, her slender waist swaying, long hair tumbling and new clothes gleaming.

Is this not a setting of happiness? Yes, indeed. Yet elsewhere a person has only hands, feet and eyes to show that he too possesses human form, while disease consumes every limb. At the sight of that aged, ruined man, the taste of fruit, the beauty of the woman and the pleasure she awakened all dry up. It is like a playful child throwing a stone into a crystal-clear stream just as a poet admires his reflection: the image is broken. In the same way, joy dissolves and unseen sorrow pierces the heart. Pleasure flees quickly at the sight of misery. What we taste gives no fulfilment unless others are happy too. If the whole country could shine anew, free from sorrow and rich in happiness, noble thoughts would arise and the nation would prosper.

The number of those who ask “Is it possible?” is declining.

The government says: “We framed one plan, are framing another, and will frame many more so that everyone may live happily and want, insufficiency and inability may be abolished.”

They no longer preach like mendicants that there must always be one person riding a palanquin and another carrying it, that a temple tower cannot become a rubbish heap nor a rubbish heap a tower, and that everything will happen in its destined manner and time by divine command. Remembering that they have somehow come to govern, they say that they devise plans so all may prosper. We are glad and grateful that they display plans and promise honeyed nectar; we cannot accuse them of lacking intention. Their hearts do not permit them to state openly what is nevertheless easy to understand.

If even the peacock can modestly admit that it lacks the plumage of the celestial bird, can ministers declare that their own position is without real authority?

If great joy is to become common to every household, governing power must be in our own hands. That means these rulers, now puppets on strings, must be reborn as people possessing the courage and capacity to govern.

“Salem's iron and Neyveli's lignite are our rare wealth lying unused. Once they are brought out and employed, our poverty and unemployment will disappear. Grace must be shown to us,” they pleaded repeatedly, with affection, tears, emphasis, and at times visible disgust, to the arrogant rulers and their officials. They told us that we spoke immaturely and acted uselessly, that they alone knew how to accomplish things, and then stood supplicating before Delhi. What benefit did they obtain? Look at the ministers and you will understand. Are they like children crying as a stronger person carries away their sweet, or like an elephant fallen into a pit? You may choose whichever comparison will anger them less.

At times they speak with wounded hearts; at other times they beat their chests. Before us, however, they shout as though wholly content. There is a story of a powerless husband with a violent wife. Thinking outsiders must not know that she beats him, he receives her blows while shouting, “Ah! Have you so much courage? Even after taking all these blows, will you not submit? You wretched woman! Just wait; I shall break your ribs before attending to anything else. Cry and roll on the ground!”

Honourable C. Subramaniam has conducted himself as if saying, “Whatever may happen, I shall never, even by mistake, behave in a way that makes people accept the arguments of this movement as correct.” Yet the newspapers report that on the sixth of this month even he went to Delhi and pressed the National Development Council:

“As far as the South is concerned, the Second Five-Year Plan is not satisfactory.

“It is meaningless to refuse to establish factories in the South on the ground that there are no natural resources. Could not at least small industries be created?

“The Plan does not inspire confidence among the people. In that situation, how can the people develop enthusiasm for the Five-Year Plan?”

Who else need proclaim this shame?

As the country celebrates its festival, what first troubles the heart is the sight of it standing stripped of splendour because it remains caught in the North's grip.

However many people receive an opportunity for happiness on the festival day, one resolve must arise in every heart: the purpose of our lives is to make the happiness we have received available to everyone, and we shall labour toward it. That resolve must be called the Pongal gift we offer the country.

O Time! We stand waiting for you to grant everyone the knowledge and strength needed for this task.

14 January 1956

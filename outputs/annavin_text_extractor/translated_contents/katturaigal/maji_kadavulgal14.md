## English Translation

[The surviving source begins as a continuation.]

At the place where he was buried, 365 blades of grass supposedly sprouted. Each became a miraculous herb curing disease in one of the human body's 365 nerves.

What a relationship between father and son in the world of gods!

Nuada regained his former splendour and strength, giving the old gods new confidence.

The new god, Bres, paid no heed to the change and did not alter his conduct. He even treated the god of poetry, the deity of the soul, with contempt. Enraged, the poet-god composed a song of abuse against Bres. Blisters immediately covered the new god's face and made him hideous. The gods assembled, declared that they would no longer permit such a monstrosity to rule, expelled Bres and crowned the old god Nuada.

Having lost his crown, Bres went to the realm of the sea and complained to his father. His father assembled an army and set out for war: heaven against the sea realm.

The great council of the gods met in heaven to devise a battle plan.

The god Lugh arrived to assist the heavenly host. At first they did not accept him, but he demonstrated his abilities. Once it was established that Lugh mastered every art and possessed every power, the heavenly beings permitted him to perform the work of a god for thirteen days.

During those days, the lord of the sea realm sent eighty-one messengers to collect tribute in heaven. Lugh killed all but nine.

The nine survivors ran back with the news. Elathan and his retinue were astonished and terrified, wondering who this new champion supporting heaven might be.

“That mighty warrior is none other than my grandson,” explained the god Balor. “I gave my daughter to Cian, son of heaven's physician-god. Lugh is Cian's son and therefore my grandson.”

“Grandson or not, he summons us to war; let us go,” roared the gods of the sea.

Both sides assembled forces for seven years. Then a terrible war erupted.

Balor was the god of the fiery eye. The eye in his forehead normally remained shut, but if its lid was raised, everyone struck by its gaze burned. Standing on the battlefield, Balor commanded them to open it so he could reduce the divine host to ashes. They lifted the lid. Lugh, however, hurled a magic stone into the fiery eye. It burst through and emerged behind Balor, and the army of sea gods was burned to ashes.

The survivors panicked. Their ranks fell into chaos and retreated, while Lugh's army pursued them. Bres, the cause of the great war, was captured. He begged Lugh for his life. Lugh stipulated that he must reveal a divine secret through which heaven might prosper.

Bres agreed. Lugh said he could live if he disclosed the proper days for ploughing, sowing and harvesting. Bres saved himself by revealing the great divine secret that Tuesday was suitable for all three.

Meanwhile the sea gods had stolen the physician-god Dagda's miraculous harp. Lugh and Dagda went to the sea realm to recover it and found it hanging upon a wall.

Here too weapons possess miraculous powers like their divine owners. Our Purana says Rama's arrow killed Ravana, pierced his entire heart, bathed in seven seas and returned to Rama's inexhaustible quiver. Devotees condemn anyone who asks whether such a lie is possible. In the same fashion, an ancient British fabricator said a god's weapon possessed supernatural power.

Knowing its master Dagda had arrived, the harp supposedly leapt from the wall and ran toward him, killing nine sea gods along the way. Dagda took it and played a sleep-inducing melody; the sea gods sank into slumber, and the heavenly party returned home.

That is the story of the struggle between the gods of heaven and those of the sea.

What does it lack—heroism, miracle, sorrow or wonder?

Could one not assign it a philosophical meaning and argue that this Puranic form represents the struggle between darkness and light? One could, but they did not. Knowledge prevailed. People recognized that all this was meaningless. They understood that inserting such stories into religion prevented anyone from perceiving the excellence of religion or the concept of God.

Thus gods who lost limbs, fought and died, old and new gods, male and female gods, arrow-shooting gods and gods playing magical harps were declared fantasies capable of confusing thought, stories invented by corrupt minds to extract money from humanity. Dagda, Nuada, Brigit, Bres, Aengus, Midir, Lugh and the others became former gods. The British people gained an honoured place in civilization.

Our people alone still offer excuses such as “sacred tale,” “ancestral inheritance” and “moral instruction.” They continue to declare ancient fantasies true: Rama's arrow, the spear and trident, severed heads, the burning of the three cities, the churning of nectar, drinking the Halahala poison, carrying earth for *pittu*, and demanding a child as meat. They twist their ideals and decay in false knowledge.

At first every people believed gods possessed cradle and bed, rise and fall, birth and death, war and more. Elsewhere true knowledge now governs; mythology was driven away centuries ago. Here, the person who asks a question is reviled as an atheist.

After seeing that beings once regarded in Britain as forms of the divine have become former gods, should clarity not arise in our minds? Religious advocates here are intellectually blind: they imagine no other people conceived fantasies as ornate as ours, and refuse to abandon them even after time has made them rot.

Some countries composed divine stories stranger than those made here, and common people revered them as meritorious texts bringing sacred grace. Yet clarity removed accumulated impurity from their minds, and they began to live.

Our Puranas stop with wars between gods and demons or among gods. Ireland went one step further, creating a myth of war between gods and human beings in which humans won, the gods lost their kingdom and fled to an unseen land.

Lugh and the other gods defeated the sea gods and spread their rule across Britain and Ireland. An ordinary devotee would say victory must always belong to gods, for who could possess power enough to defeat them? The Irish fantasy says the opposite.

Humans from Spain supposedly heard of Ireland's wealth and the gods who lived there victoriously, then resolved to conquer it. Warrior leaders marched with armies to overthrow the divine kingdom. War broke out between mortals and gods. The divine host was defeated, panicked and fled Ireland before the victorious humans.

What a divine tale: gods defeated by people, losing crown and territory, fleeing in terror to an invisible land.

Ireland had such a “great divine legend.” Do its people today treat that fantasy as a religion worthy of worship? No. They have acquired knowledge; how could they believe absurdities?

Here in our country, every year people fashion Duryodhana from earth and hold a festival in which a devotee dressed as Bhima cuts him with a wooden sword. They consider it a sacred act. Ireland and Britain recognized their own meaningless ancient stories as useless centuries ago.

Some eloquent speakers here still argue that divine stories may lack coherence and meaning but contain necessary morals. Connoisseurs melt and ask how they can abandon honeyed texts in which they find artistic flavour.

Britain, Ireland, France and Spain also had seekers of such flavour. Yet they could not stop the speed of knowledge or spoil development. Those lands too possessed savour-filled divine tales: a god who loved a maiden seen in a dream, pined until his body burned and lamented that his blazing entrails would not cool; divine romances resembling Murugan singing to Valli about the deer that came without grazing in an unfading forest.

There were gods whose bodies burned with separation, gods who sought new flowers and drank the honey of love, and gods who entered great wars for passion. All occupied people's minds until reason began to shine.

Actions found only among barbarians appeared in those divine stories. At the time, people did not ask whether gods would commit such degradation. The priest said even thinking it was a great sin. Could one impose upon gods rules about what was proper or control them like mere human births? “Yes,” the common people agreed.

The gods went beyond romantic play into sexual revelry. They abducted women, violated other men's wives and imprisoned them, yet neither lost divine office nor ceased receiving worship.

The god Aengus abducted Etain, wife of his brother Midir, and imprisoned her in a glass box. He carried this ornamental prison wherever he went so that Midir could not recover his wife. The captive beauty too seems to have held the broad principle, “If not that man, this one.”

Did the other gods punish Aengus for this injustice? No. Did war arise? No. Did the priest say a sinner like Aengus must not be worshipped? No. Aengus continued as one of the gods.

Only Midir grieved over losing his wife. Did he angrily decide to dispose of both the wicked woman and the villain and attend to other work? No. He desperately sought to regain his lost pleasure, but found no opportunity.

Aengus was the god of beauty, so female deities commonly desired him. One such goddess, infatuated with him, transformed Etain into a fly and obtained his love.

What could Etain do, married to one god, abducted by another and finally transformed into a fly? She flew about for seven years.

By then mortals had become kings of Ireland. During a royal feast, the fly fell into a drinking cup and entered the womb of a woman who drank from it. A beautiful girl was born.

Reborn on earth as a human child, Etain became astonishingly beautiful. Her fame spread throughout Ireland, and its king, overwhelmed by her beauty, married her.

Only then did Midir learn that his former wife had become Ireland's queen. His attachment remained. He descended to earth in the form of a handsome youth, told Etain of her former history and begged her to return to the divine world. She refused.

Midir's longing would not diminish. He resolved to recover his former wife by some stratagem.

In the form of a handsome and majestic youth, he entered the king's audience hall, where the king invited him to a game of dice. The loser had to grant whatever the winner requested. The king won; Midir lost. The king asked him to build a great road through Ireland, and Midir completed it.

The following year Midir played again and this time won. “Ask what you wish; I am bound to give it,” said the king. His heart was struck by thunder when Midir answered: “Under our agreement, you must give what I ask. King, give me your wife.”

The confused king told him to return the following year and take his beautiful wife. Midir came on the appointed day. But could the king willingly lose her? He packed guards throughout the palace to prevent Midir entering.

Midir was a magician. Disregarding the thousand and eight layers of security, he entered, called Etain, and the beautiful queen consented. They assumed the form of swans and flew away.

Midir rejoiced that he had rediscovered on earth, in another birth, the treasure lost among the gods.

The king, overcome by unbearable grief, vowed to retrieve his queen wherever she might be. He assembled an army and searched in all eight directions. Eventually he learned that Midir and Etain were in the underworld. He began digging the earth, declaring that he would enter that realm and recover her.

Midir panicked below. The wretch kept digging and seemed likely to discover their residence; then everyone would know about the underworld and its glory would fade. In alarm, Midir sent fifty beautiful women resembling Etain before the king.

The king remained adamant: “Did I cut a road from earth into the underworld for these flowering vines? I want my own beautiful queen.” At last Midir recognized that his only means of preserving his kingdom was to surrender Etain. Wearily he said, “Take her.” The Irish king took beautiful Etain away.

Such is the myth. According to its account, gods suffered these troubles and disgraces over beautiful women. Events contrary to humanity and every virtue—morality, justice and honesty—were divine tales.

When Irish and British people lacked clarity, they reverently regarded even sexual mischief as sacred play. Not one story could survive a request for reason or explanation. The mythmaker linked incoherent incidents and coated them with divine polish. Anyone asking why or how was called a sinner. People therefore listened patiently while priests proudly recited obscene stories.

It took a long time for knowledge to arise that attributing such degradation to God was not merely foolish but cruel. Yet once knowledge sprouted there, people allowed it to grow and flower. Here deceivers have used even brutal methods to prevent new flowers from blooming in thought. The realm of ideas contains countless scorched buds. Religious rulers have so skilfully guarded and fattened ignorance that the intellectual revolution which occurred in Britain, Ireland, Spain and elsewhere must enter here timidly, fearfully and looking to every side.

There are countless former gods there. Knowledge spread, and the forms of darkness disappeared by themselves.

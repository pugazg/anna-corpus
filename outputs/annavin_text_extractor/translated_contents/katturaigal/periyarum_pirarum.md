## English Translation

Throughout Tamil Nadu on the eighteenth of this month, Periyar's sixty-first birthday was celebrated with joy. Public meetings praised his service and spoke of his great qualities, while appeals were made to join his ranks. Speeches overflowed with growth, gatherings kindled enthusiasm, and every town carried the same message.

I do not wish to describe the decorations, portrait-unveiling ceremonies, processions carrying his picture in a palanquin, arches and festoons, songs in metre, or meals prepared for the poor. That is not my purpose. I speak not of the celebration's ornament but ask what idea the festival embodies, and offer my thought.

Those seeking standing and fame are taught: be a good man to everyone; never behave in a way that hurts anyone's mind; do not ask “What is that?” or “How is this?” about everything; follow whatever appears good to the majority; speak with a smiling face; even when you see wrongdoing, do not keep pointing it out, for opposition, blame and enmity will follow. Whatever happens, what is it to you? Do not set out to reform the town.

People have indeed become leaders and acquired name and fame by adopting this mentality, scattering money and putting on a little theatrical display. It is the art of conforming to the world and the technique most people use. A man may cry “Siva! Siva!” while privately desiring a woman, and none will rage; people will praise his devotion and emotion. Donate one hundred rupees to one relief fund and fifty to an association, and an army stands ready to sing hymns, even in this land starved of office. Smile and display charm to everyone, and crowds swoon over one's goodness, elegance and love. Many therefore perform for the purpose of satisfying others and obtain renown.

Periyar is called an expert in thrift. His is a simple life, one that does not seek even the comforts appropriate to his age. Giving money is not his habit. One might say he hates hardly anything as much. If he is made to pay, he becomes agitated because he believes he has been deceived and used. Thus money is not what binds Periyar to the Tamils.

The doctrine “follow where everyone goes” sounds to him like harsh metal. He asks why they went, where, and where another path would have led. He did not gain fame by repeating ideas already considered proper and good or following established methods. We see people seeking easy success graft themselves onto existing ideas and finish their business with little difficulty. Periyar did not reach his present position that way.

Let me explain. When everyone celebrates Purattasi Saturdays and sings Govinda bhajans, it is easy to join the cry, receive sacred food, shout louder than others for a larger share, dance as though possessed, and gain esteem. Mastering that trick is simple. But if one calls aside the man crying, “Govinda cures incurable karma and removes the suffering of all seven worlds,” and asks, “Noble man who shouts so loudly, has your tuberculosis disappeared?”, will he receive sacred food? The crowd will instead shout, “Seize him! Beat him!”

Fearing this, how many participate in fraud, cultivate foolishness and assist deceit? They fear the world will rage if they speak their minds. More than ninety in every hundred among the so-called educated live in that condition. Periyar dislikes such conduct. The medicine he gives is bitter.

In our country, whatever we had called good, he called harmful. Whatever path we had considered salvation, he showed to be false. While we suffered without knowing our enemies, he pointed into our own minds and said, “Here is your enemy.” He called the puranas that spread and flourished across the land false stories. Brahmins whom people trusted as guides to liberation, he called a group living by investing the people's superstition and deceiving them. The land said to yield gold and overflow with wealth, he described as a country cast into the pit of slavery by brokers of Aryan religion.

To those clamouring for self-rule, he began asking: “What is it? What will it look like? Whom will it benefit? How long will it last? Why has it not arrived yet?” To poets singing of the wonders seen in Gandhi's age, he asked: “Did you see anything other than khadi that turns cash to charcoal, a spinning wheel that makes hands wooden, barbarous doctrine, palm leaves and coarse jaggery?” Merely thinking of what this man from Erode said is frightening.

None of this was honey that would make people praise the speaker. Yet it was this preacher of bitter doctrine whom people honoured as Periyar and celebrated. Why? He gave no money, offered no old path, did not flatter the people as all-knowing, and did not beat accompaniment to the existing noise to assemble an army. He stood alone, found a separate path, and startled everyone. He found opponents everywhere. All asked, “What sort of speech is this?” He suddenly changed the people's minds. Many were bewildered; some were stunned.

“Atheist! Communalist! Brahmin-hater! Perverse disputant! Needless lover of disorder!” they abused him. When he began speaking, they called for curses and stones.

They cried that he destroyed religion and country. Hearing his revolutionary song, they argued: “Does he alone know everything, and are all others mad?” The orthodox wailed that caste and religious custom were gone and that shastras, epics and puranas were losing value. The government said: “This man is a revolutionary speaking many novelties. Seize him and put him in a cage.”

What words can describe the storm that rose to overthrow him when he began revolutionary propaganda, or the fearless heart with which he resisted it without falling? That, I say, caused his greatness and made him leader of the Tamils.

There are two kinds of people - I do not mean male and female. There are those who accept what exists and live accordingly, and those who change and elevate it. The former find a way to live; the latter prescribe how life ought to be. Those who participate in what exists are ordinary people. Those who alter, correct and reconstruct it are leaders, true leaders. One who turns a herd with a stick is no leader, nor one who enchants others by mastering their emotion with a flute.

Periyar belongs to the class that corrects, changes and renews what it sees. Therefore he became a true leader.

Every leader saw widows and felt sympathy. But they said, “What can be done? It is cruel, but it is their fate,” and went on. Periyar saw them and did not walk away invoking fate. He said, “What can fate do? We must acquire the intelligence to remove cruelty.” He campaigned, attacking the blind beliefs responsible for their suffering. Today widow remarriage has become entirely ordinary.

Inter-caste marriage likewise spread rapidly. Those who first found Periyar's teaching bitter and scowled at it have undergone enough change to regard these matters as normal.

Had he feared the initial opposition, trembled at blame, or been broken by the abuse of base minds, would society have changed today? Widows' tears would still flow like rivers.

Brahmins did not begin calling our people Sudras only yesterday. They called our grandfathers that, and they merely asked, “Why, master?” They called our fathers that, and they replied, “Your servant offers full prostration.” We were the ones who warned, “Do not call us Sudras; do not kindle our anger.” Only now do they address us as Mudaliarval, Naidugaru and Pillaival.

Could a greater change have occurred in social status? People who for generations silently accepted disgrace and lived in fear now walk with heads raised. Why? Only because of Periyar's relentless propaganda. In Tamil Nadu he became the commander guarding our people's self-respect. As a result of his revolutionary work, the agraharam now lies subdued.

He made the so-called earth-gods and divine Brahmins ordinary human beings, and transformed those burdened with the poisonous slave title “Sudras, children of concubines” into Tamils and Dravidians. I ask: who else, anywhere, accomplished such a change within twenty years?

For centuries our people believed that the existence of rich and poor was ordained by Brahma long ago, and that enduring it would bring happiness in the next world.

Today, when poverty is mentioned, everyone asks why. When seeing a poor man, they ask, “What wage? Why has he not awakened? Let him rise.” They say the poor's suffering must end and can be ended through a socialist plan.

Through whose effort did this idea enter the Dravidian land? How could those crying “fate, fate” have introduced it? It was born, grew and spread through Periyar's courageous declaration that poverty exists because of a conspiratorial group, not fate or any such nonsense.

Before that, all we knew were tales such as “Harishchandra guarded the cremation ground” and “the utterly poor Kuchela became Kubera through God's grace.”

The stones hurled at Periyar themselves spoke and spread the new world's thought through the country. This fearless man's tireless energy produced today's immense change.

One may say no one in our country equalled Periyar in exposing fraud and driving away impostors. Nor has anyone else lived to see and rejoice directly in the results of his own effort.

The same people who first abused him as atheist and perverse disputant now call him “the great leader who restored our honour.” Periyar witnessed that change with his own eyes.

He saw Sudras become Tamils, women seek equality, and the conviction that birth must confer neither superiority nor inferiority reach its height. He saw blind beliefs burn and fall, crooked schemes cast into pits, and the people themselves reject obscene religious folly. He saw the eastern sky brighten and saw renown.

His white beard dancing in the wind, victorious Periyar said, “Down with Congress rule,” and asked how many more days the ministers of surrender would remain. He saw them disappear.

He sought Hindu-Muslim unity and saw it grow. He laboured to remove the suffering of the untouchable and saw victory there too.

Today he says Dravida Nadu must belong to Dravidians. In this too he will certainly see victory.

The orthodox world once wished to pluck and discard him as a sprout. Today he has become a great movement. He is Periyar. This is the difference between him and others.

*Kudi Arasu*, 24 December 1939

## Translator's Notes

The opening title is damaged in OCR as `ஊியாரும் பிறரும்`; the article's closing sentence explicitly confirms `பெரியாரும் பிறரும்` (“Periyar and Others”). The sixty-first birthday reference points to E. V. Ramasamy's celebration in September 1939; the article itself is dated 24 December 1939.

`Periyar` means “the great one” or “respected elder.” `Purattasi Saturday` is associated with Vishnu worship. `Agraharam` denotes a traditional Brahmin settlement. `Sudra`, `Mudaliarval`, `Naidugaru` and `Pillaival` are retained or explained according to their caste/status use in the argument. `Khadar` is khadi, and the spinning wheel refers to Gandhian politics.

The article's historical, political and caste claims are translated as Anna's celebratory assessment of Periyar, not as independently verified conclusions. The OCR contains scattered damage in several idioms, but the paragraph-level meaning is recoverable; the Tamil source remains unchanged.

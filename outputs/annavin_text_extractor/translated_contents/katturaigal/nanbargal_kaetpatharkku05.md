## English Translation

## 18. Did other parties tremble at repression and abandon their principles in this manner? — continued

Those who demanded self-rule were permitted to participate in elections—not only here, but even in England.

Thus Congress's direct actions were suppressed, but the party itself was not rendered incapable of functioning. Did we not conduct the three-pronged agitation, erase Hindi names, undertake picketing and burn the Constitution? When Congress engaged in actions of that kind, or in still more intense ones, punishment was imposed, just as it was upon us.

But Congress never faced a cruel law like the one imposed upon us, stripping it of its rights.

The new influence it gained by suffering repression gave Congress an opportunity to work vigorously for its fundamental principle of self-rule. People showed affection for the Congress that had been attacked by repression and gave it their support.

They became angry with the British government that carried out the repression.

Since Congress's electoral rights were not taken away as ours are being taken away, Congressmen participated in elections and received abundant public support. Congress acquired the strength needed to overthrow the repressive British government.

The prohibitory law does not permit us to occupy that position.

The question before us is not whether we can withstand repression. Even if we withstand it, will we retain the right to speak about our fundamental principle, the right to operate a party for it and the right to contest elections, or will we not? That is the question confronting us.

Even the Communist Party was not prohibited from propagating common ownership. Its freedom of speech was not taken away, nor was it denied the right to contest elections on a programme of common ownership.

Repression came when it engaged in other unlawful activities.

When the Communist Party adopted violent methods, the party was banned, Communists were imprisoned, the party ceased to function and it did not contest elections.

But when the Communist Party announced that it had abandoned violent methods and said it would struggle nonviolently, through parliamentary means and in conformity with the laws of the country, to establish a government based on common ownership, the ban upon it was lifted. Propaganda for common ownership was permitted and the right to contest elections was granted.

Therefore, no explanation can be obtained by comparing what other parties did in circumstances of this kind.

If a comparison must be made, it can only be with authoritarian countries.

In China and Russia, which possess systems of government based on common ownership, no political parties other than the Communist Party exist; they have been prohibited.

Parties holding any principle other than communism cannot function, and their propaganda cannot proceed.

No party or individual other than the Communist Party has the right to contest elections; the law gives them no place.

The prohibitory law introduced here against us is of that kind.

A party may not exist unless it accepts a single India and the political structure of the Indian Union; it is prohibited. Neither an individual nor a party may speak against the doctrine of a single India; the law affords no right to do so.

An individual or party that does not accept the doctrine of a single India cannot participate in elections; it is prohibited.

Those who argue merely that defying the ban and accepting repression is the mark of courage should examine this carefully: in countries where every other party has been prohibited, have brave people willing to accept hardship and loss vanished without a trace? Why has no one there come forward to oppose such prohibitory laws, speak the principles they favour and operate a party for them?

When a law arrives that destroys the very foundation, defying it may provide the finest example of courage and sacrifice, but it will not show a way to continue systematic propagation of the fundamental principle. Thinkers in authoritarian countries therefore understood that they could not function after their basic rights were removed, and that even an attempt would yield no result; they became inactive. A countable few came forward to defend their rights. They were imprisoned, tortured and exiled. The world praised them as people worthy of honour and veneration. But that did not secure a right for parties other than the ruling party to function.

The list of martyrs grew longer; new names were added to the roll of heroes.

It is not those who roam about abusing us, but some among us, who possess the courage to enter such a roll of martyrs.

It cannot be said that every person in China, Russia and the many other states that have prohibited principles and parties accepts the ruling party's doctrine and method exactly as they stand. People with differing ideas and independent thinkers must exist there. Yet because their rights of speech and organisation have been legally barred, they cannot act.

Nor does anyone there rise to abuse them as cowards, people without principles or opportunists. In Egypt, other parties cannot function in defiance of Nasser's command.

When a fundamental right is taken away, the power to function is automatically lost. I have studied the histories of liberation in foreign countries with great care for this reason. In many places the road to liberation was filled with violence, so the difficulty we face did not arise there.

In a few places, when organisations were banned, they changed their names and continued to function. In Ireland, for example, when the Catholic Association, an organisation for liberation, was prohibited, it evaded the ban by changing its name to the Catholic Charitable Association and resumed its work.

Some of you may remember an instance in our own country. When the government harassed the newspaper *Kudi Arasu* by demanding security, Periyar changed its name to *Puratchi*. When that too was harassed, he changed it to *Pagutharivu*. Once conditions improved, it was again published under its old name, *Kudi Arasu*. No one called an organisation's operation under changed names cowardice or wavering in principle. It was welcomed as a change in the method of struggle and as a tactical plan. Yet even these examples do not give us complete guidance about what we should do.

To find a remedy for our present condition, we must examine what the Congress party that created it first tried to do.

Only by seeing the other methods Congress employed in attempting to destroy us before hurling the weapon of this prohibitory law can we understand all that a ruling party tried, and how it failed before bringing in this law. Only when we understand that will we feel the responsibility and concern necessary to decide our own course of action.

Our advocacy of separation did not begin today or yesterday.

It did not begin merely after the DMK was founded; we advocated it even when we were the Dravidar Kazhagam.

Why has it now become necessary to introduce a prohibitory law against separation, which we have advocated for more than twenty years? What created this urgency? We must investigate.

For a time they expected the Kazhagam never to grow into an organisation and to remain confined to four or five speakers who could be counted on one's fingers. It did not happen; the organisation grew.

They thought the Kazhagam would remain among educated people and city dwellers, that villagers would not welcome it and that no danger could arise while rural people withheld support. They were disappointed in that too; the organisation spread through the countryside.

They then expected that, although the organisation had somehow spread, a leadership quarrel would arise and break it apart. Instead, the Kazhagam became a family.

Thinking that dissension could be stirred even within a family and that the Kazhagam could be no exception, they sowed their seed. The conspiracy sprouted, but it was the conspiracy that fell; the Kazhagam was not destroyed.

They thought those at the forefront of the Kazhagam could be degraded and stained until their standing collapsed. They abused us without restraint—me in particular. You know there is no insult they have not hurled at me and no accusation they have not laid upon me. Yet the affection you show me did not diminish; it grew.

They mocked us, saying we might speak on platforms and receive applause, but asking whether people would actually vote for us. They expected us to collapse if we entered elections. We succeeded in that field as well.

They taunted us: “Even if they win elections, what does it matter? What can they achieve?” That did no harm; fifteen became fifty-one.

They conducted hostile propaganda. It failed.

They formed opposing alliances. They gained nothing.

They stirred up disorder. It produced no benefit.

They tried police harassment and attacks through law. In the fire, the Kazhagam became gold.

Only after all these methods failed did the Congress government bring in the prohibitory law.

So long as an organisation called the Kazhagam exists, Congress has no peace.

The Congress organisation cannot prevent the Kazhagam's growth.

It cannot destroy our principle through the force of propaganda.

Congress's aim has therefore become the destruction of the Kazhagam itself.

Had they merely wished to eliminate the principle of separation, they would have worked still more vigorously to overcome our propaganda.

But their real purpose is that the democratic organisation called the DMK must cease to exist, because its continued existence endangers Congress rule.

Just as Congress sought a different method and instrument at each stage in its effort to destroy us, we too must devise methods and instruments suited to each stage.

Congress possessed the principle of a single India with which to confront, attack and destroy our principle. Yet it did not merely use that doctrine against ours. Nor did it remain content to use its Congress organisation to attack and destroy our Kazhagam organisation. Instead of sending only the doctrine of a single India and the Congress organisation into the field, it has now deployed a new instrument there—the prohibitory law.

In the same way, we must change the method of allowing our Kazhagam organisation itself to collide with the prohibitory law. The Kazhagam has long stood as a great challenge to Congress; by keeping it from being destroyed, we must defeat Congress's plan. The remedy for the problem our members must confront can be found by examining the long relationship between our Kazhagam and Congress, not merely by looking at the methods adopted by other parties.

## 19. So you say the DMK must continue to function. Very well: is that your opinion or the Kazhagam's opinion?

To determine the Kazhagam's opinion, the General Council discussed the matter for three days and reached this decision. I am not accustomed to imposing my opinion upon others.

The Kazhagam's organisation possesses an excellence that no one anywhere has built in so short a period. I cannot understand how some find it in their hearts to say that this organisation may be allowed to break apart. For all these years we have guarded the Kazhagam as an eyelid guards the eye—from the attacks of Congress autocracy, from Periyar's formidable attacks and from conspiracy kindled within our own ranks. For my part, nowhere else have I found or felt greater joy and pride than in nurturing the Kazhagam.

There is joy in seeing the size and number of Kazhagam meetings increase.

There is fulfilment in seeing the quality of its speakers rise and the support people show them grow.

There is joy in seeing the Kazhagam's periodicals gain beauty and distinction.

There is pride in seeing its comrades occupy positions from panchayats to Parliament.

There is exhilaration in reading that a Kazhagam member became mayor, shook hands with Nasser, travelled to Moscow and was welcomed there.

The situation is enough to leave one wondering whether these are pleasant dreams or events occurring today.

My heart would not consent to the destruction of an organisation with such strength and splendour. I therefore reached the decision that the Kazhagam must be protected.

If Congressmen truly hold a deep conviction that separation must not occur, should they not rejoice that we have abandoned separation? Have you noticed that, on the contrary, they are distressed and angry?

They expected our destruction. Now they are disappointed and irritated.

Whenever a decision we take pleases our opponents, I become concerned that we may have chosen the very course they wanted.

Only when our decision irritates our opponents do I gain confidence that it is right. Seen in that way, the very irritation and abuse shown by opponents who seek to destroy us gives me joy and confidence that our decision is right and appropriate.

No decision I have taken on behalf of the Kazhagam has harmed it. I assure you that this change of method will not harm it either. More than that, I say with conviction that a radiant future is calling us. Let me remind you:

The DMK is the embodiment of thirteen years of labour.

The DMK is a camp built through the accumulated sacrifices of many martyrs.

The DMK is the battle camp that resists Congress autocracy.

The DMK is a party that has received the votes of three million people.

The DMK is the single largest opposition party in the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly.

The DMK is a democratic organisation in daily contact with the people.

The DMK has attained the stature of a forum that informed observers say can grow into the ruling party at the next election.

If the DMK is destroyed, democracy itself will receive a devastating blow.

Only if the DMK continues its work can we protect the language, attain prosperity and discover a new life.

Therefore, the country needs the DMK.

That is why I amend the Kazhagam's rules and constitution so that it may continue to function in a manner suited to the condition now created, and entrust the Kazhagam to you. You possess the power to create a radiant future. If you apply your intelligence to that task, you can ensure that our Kazhagam is not shattered by the abuse of opponents.

Our opponents may even boast that they will tear this explanation apart and rip it to pieces.

Tearing and ripping—especially with the mouth—is easy, very easy indeed.

Creating, building, safeguarding and fostering growth are difficult.

You have created and protected a Kazhagam formidable enough to bewilder its opponents. Your responsibility is immense and your work is noble. I have offered this explanation with the questions asked by friends in this situation in mind. Since you bear responsibility for protecting the Kazhagam, I trust this explanation is enough to make the situation clear to you.

## 20. Are you saying that the prohibitory law imposed upon the Kazhagam cannot be broken?

It is not merely that it cannot be broken. An organisation called the Kazhagam would be given no room to function precisely because it kept committing acts that defied the ban. Individuals may think of defying it, but those who intend to do so cannot operate together as an organisation.

If the organisation called the DMK resolves to defy the ban, the organisation will break apart. It would be like using seed grain for the cooking pot. Destroying the camp in order to attack the prohibitory law would yield no benefit and would not constitute a method of struggle.

Moreover, I firmly believe that by operating our organisation under its amended rules and constitution, we can secure for the people a very large measure of the benefits and welfare we expected an independent Dravida Nadu to provide, and enable them to live well. I call upon all of you to undertake that noble work.

## English Translation

All the jurors had arrived, and the magistrate too came in with an air of authority and took his seat. A vast crowd had gathered to witness the case, yet silence prevailed in the court. As soon as the order was given that the accused might be brought in, a large iron cage was carried forward and set down. Inside it was a wolf!

Yes, the creature placed on trial that day was the wolf.

First, counsel for the government read out the charge against it. Then another lawyer argued on behalf of the wolf. At last, after hearing the arguments on both sides, the magistrate pronounced the wolf guilty and ordered that it be hanged in public.

This strange trial took place in Europe in 1442. Many such trials were held in that period.

In 1386, a pig was tried on the charge that it had killed a child. The magistrate is said to have delivered judgment with great sorrow, ordering that its head be cut off in a public place. When it was brought to trial, they had apparently dressed it in human clothing.

For the same offence, a sow and her six piglets were brought before a court in the town of Sevigny in 1547. The lawyer representing the pigs displayed his full powers of advocacy. The result was punishment only for the mother; the piglets were saved.

Nevertheless, three weeks later, the six piglets were again brought before the court. Their owner had reportedly refused to guarantee that they would behave properly in the future. Believing that the mother’s nature could hardly be absent from her offspring, he had declined to give the undertaking.

At Burgundy in 1347, three pigs and their young were placed in the dock for killing a child. The owner of the pigs did not argue for the adults; he argued for the piglets, citing their age.

“The piglets stood idly watching the child being killed and did not come forward to save it. Nevertheless, taking their age into consideration, I acquit them,” ruled the magistrate.

In 1314, a bull was imprisoned for goring a man to death. It was confined in prison along with human beings. At the conclusion of the trial, it was hanged.

A stallion was hanged for killing a man, while a mare was publicly burned in Greece.

Another trial was held in France in 1512. The defendants were rats. A farmer prosecuted them for destroying and stealing ears of barley. Summonses were sent to the rats, but they showed no inclination to emerge from their burrows.

A celebrated lawyer named Bartholomew Chassenee argued on their behalf. His argument that there was no point in sending summonses only to the rats of one locality and that every rat in the town must be summoned was accepted. Summonses were consequently found at every rat hole in town.

Even then the rats did not obey the court’s order. Their lawyer therefore adopted another line of argument:

“The rats are afraid to leave their burrows because the plaintiff keeps a cat. If the plaintiff deposits a substantial bond with the court guaranteeing that his cat will cause the rats no harm, they are prepared to appear.”

The justice of this argument was accepted. Because the plaintiff was unwilling to furnish the bond, the case was dismissed.

In 1499, a bear was brought before a court. Its lawyer argued: “We have no authority to try the bear. The elders of the bear community themselves must serve as jurors!”

This argument caused great confusion, and the case dragged on for several weeks.

In trials for murder, robbery, and similar crimes, animals such as dogs and cats were accepted as witnesses. If an owner denied an accusation in their presence and they remained silent, he was deemed innocent. People believed that if he were guilty, God would not remain silent but would expose the offender through the animals’ behaviour.

It was an age without sophisticated forms of entertainment. What explanation can be given for such bizarre trials other than that they afforded good entertainment to uncivilized minds?

*Dravida Nadu Pongal Malar*, 1963.

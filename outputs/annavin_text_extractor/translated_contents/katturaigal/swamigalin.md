## English Translation

“No... no... I won’t come. I’m frightened, Amma. I won’t come there.”

“Why, my dear? What are you afraid of?”

“No... no... I won’t come.”

“Come, my darling. Just eat one mouthful and then you can go.”

“I won’t come. If I go there, Appa will beat me.”

“Why should Appa beat you? I’ll make sure he doesn’t. Come, my little sugar candy!”

“I won’t come. Look there, Amma, the cane!”

“The cane won’t do anything to you. I’ll look after you. Come.”

“No... no. I’ll come only if you pick up the cane and throw it outside.”

“I’ll pick it up and throw it away. Come. I’ve served rice in the silver bowl. Come and see. How much trouble I take for you! Come, my darling.”

“You must go and throw it far away first. Only then will I come.”

The mother removes the cane that has been frightening her son and returns.

“I’ve thrown it away. Come now and eat.”

“Say that you’ll never put the cane there again. Only then will I come.”

“I won’t put it there again. Just come and eat, my precious.”

“Truly, you won’t put it there?”

“I won’t. I won’t. I swear I won’t.”

“Appa beat me before, didn’t he? I won’t speak to him. You mustn’t speak to him anymore either. No...”

“I won’t speak to Appa. From now on I’ll speak only to you, play only with you, and eat only with you. Shall we beat Appa and drive him away?”

“Yes... From now on you’ll play only with me, won’t you?”

“Truly, I’ll play only with you. Shall I swear it? I swear I’ll play only with you. But first you must eat.”

“Then I’ll eat. You must serve it to me in the silver bowl.”

The scene sketched above is this: a child, frightened by the cane his father keeps to discipline him, stubbornly refuses to eat unless it is removed. The mother makes a show of taking the cane outside and throwing it away, and thereby gets the child to eat. We see many such incidents in everyday life.

The frightened child, the mother comforting him, and the father watching the spectacle from a distance are the figures in the scene.

The mother realizes that she has borne the child and is responsible for caring for it as her own, so she begins doing whatever it asks. That is why she makes a show of taking the cane outside and throwing it away. Believing the cane is gone and that there is no longer anything to fear, the child rejoices. The father watches with a smile, knowing clearly that if the cane really has fallen outside, it can be retrieved when needed, or another cane can be prepared.

The child never thinks that although one cane has gone, another may come. How could such a thought arise? It is only a tiny child whose understanding has not matured. That is why it delights in the disappearance of one cane.

The child is little, lacking full understanding and ignorant of the world. The father therefore does not become angry because the child asks him to throw away his own cane or rejoices when it is thrown away. He smiles at the child’s ignorance and at the comedy within it.

Do not imagine that scenes of this kind occur only in ordinary families. They occur in public life too, for ignorance-born childishness exists there as well.

Swami Sahajananda, enthroned as head of the Nandanar Mutt in Chidambaram, recently staged just such a scene in public life. All credit for it belongs to him!

On May 22, Swami Sahajananda reportedly went with his “chariots, elephants, cavalry, and infantry” to the Adi-Dravidar settlement in Seppakkam village. A black flag was flying there. The Swami saw it and was immediately terrified. He seems to have tried to burn it up by spitting the fire of rage from his eyes, but failed. At once he roared an oath: “I will not set foot in this settlement. Nor will I speak!” Taking on the form of Viswamitra, he declared, “You have all betrayed the Congress. Take my curse!” and left the settlement.

This appears to have thrown his hosts of devotees into confusion. Perhaps chanting inwardly, “Praise to Sahajananda, lord of the South; praise to the lord of every land,” they all assembled, uprooted the black-flag pole, carried it beyond the village, and performed an “August service.” Since the Swami’s third eye had not opened, they used a matchstick. Wordlessly proclaiming, “The fire set of old burned the three cities; the later fire burned in August 1942; the fire now set burns the black flag,” they set it alight. After the flag had turned wholly into sacred ash, they reportedly ran to the Swami, fell prostrate, rose, and swore, “None of us belongs to any party other than Congress.” The Swami’s heart then cooled like a block of ice; he entered the settlement in procession and graciously opened his sacred mouth!

See how *Dinasari*, one of the Congress newspapers, reported the spectacle staged by Swami Sahajananda. On page four, column two, of its May 26 issue, it stated:

“A public meeting was held in the settlement. Seeing a black flag flying there, Sri Swami Sahajananda said, ‘I will not set foot in this settlement, nor will I speak,’ and departed after telling them, ‘You have all betrayed the Congress.’ The residents immediately assembled, uprooted the black-flag pole, carried it outside the village, burned it, and came to the leaders and swore that none of them had joined any party except Congress. Everyone then spoke.”

Together with Swami Sahajananda, Comrade J. Sivashanmugam, Speaker of the Madras Legislative Assembly, was present and returned after planting a “flag of victory” at Seppakkam!

Every idea spread by the Dravidar Kazhagam in the social sphere strikes orthodox minds filled with rotten notions like a whip. When a Dravidar Kazhagam idea emerges and attacks, orthodox belief shakes and panics. Orthodoxy realizes that it cannot survive before the Kazhagam; it trembles, fears, and tries to flee. Like a child frightened by a cane and demanding that it be thrown away, Comrade Sahajananda, representing orthodoxy, was frightened by the black flag, emblem of the Dravidar Kazhagam’s ideal, and prompted its removal.

Just as the child stubbornly refuses food unless the cane is discarded, the “Swami” stubbornly declared that he would neither enter nor speak while the black flag remained.

Without considering that another cane could come when needed, the child rejoices and consents to eat once one cane has been thrown away. Likewise, without considering whether another black flag could or would appear after one was removed, the “Swami” rejoiced when it was taken down and burned, agreed to enter, and consented to speak.

The father who owns the cane sees the child’s conduct, pities its ignorance, and smiles at the spectacle, knowing he can obtain another cane when necessary. In the same way, members of the Dravidar Kazhagam, to whom the black flag belongs, see Sahajananda’s conduct, pity the “Swami’s” ignorance, and laugh, knowing clearly that another black flag can be planted in that settlement whenever necessary.

The mother says she will no longer join the father in order to placate the child, but that does not mean she will truly never speak to him again. Sahajananda may similarly believe the statement by some residents that they belong to no other party. But it is certain that when the settlement’s people understand the truth, they will inevitably come to the Dravidar Kazhagam camp. The very fact that a black-flag pole had been planted there shows that some had already enrolled themselves as soldiers of its camp.

To the pride of “Swami” Sahajananda and his disciple Sivashanmugam, the fire lit by their “hosts of devotees” could burn only the black flag. It could not burn the ideas that had entered the hearts of Dravidar Kazhagam comrades. Indeed, the light from that fire would make those ideas shine still more brightly, while exposing the deceitful and cowardly thoughts hidden in the hearts of the Sahajanandas and Sivashanmugams. In one respect, it has offered the country a fine opportunity to know them.

Sahajananda may have rejoiced that the black flag was burned. Congress newspapers may praise it as decisive action, and other “devotees” may delight in it. But by offering the incident to Congressmen, we remind them that Gandhi’s doctrine of nonviolence, which he taught them, was burned along with the flag.

When quarrels born merely of party jealousy occur among low-ranking workers who have no time to acquire thought and clarity, one may regard such acts as natural and find some room for pardon. But Sahajananda is praised by Congressmen themselves as one of their leaders in the South, is a legislator, is presented as a representative of the oppressed, and is the head of a religious institution. Sivashanmugam too is a representative of the oppressed, a lawyer, and Speaker of the Assembly. Yet they tried to burn, consented to the burning of, praised, and rejoiced over the flag of a political party. Is this political integrity?

If desired, one principle may be made to contend with another principle. That is the course of intelligence and courage. But unleashing official power, manpower, and money power against a principle and against the flag that symbolizes it, and then rejoicing proudly, is neither justice, integrity, nor bravery. The wise will call such action cowardice in matters of principle.

The black flag is a great emblem standing for the abolition of divisions created by birth and the creation of a society without such divisions. It is a staff upon which the people cast down and crushed at the bottom of society may lean to attain deliverance and advancement.

Even under the rule of white men intoxicated with imperial arrogance, the flag of a political party was not removed and burned in this manner. While the Union Jack was the flag of government, the flags of parties without state power continued to fly in different places according to the extent of their popular support. Under white rule, Congress flags continued to fly over municipal councils and district boards where Congress had increased its membership through majority influence. No one removed, burned, or disregarded them. Nor were the rulers so petty-minded as to insult a party’s flag. Anyone other than an insensible object knows that a flag is like life itself to members of the party it represents and will not injure it.

Because many of our country’s heads of monasteries do not possess even the intelligence of a tree, situations arise in which they drag at other women and are brought before courts for judgment!

This condition is not confined to heads of Saivite and Vaishnavite monasteries. It has become a common occurrence among certain religious heads in general. Newspapers recently reported that a Christian priest named Joseph had subjected a fifteen-year-old boy named William to his “misconduct,” for which a Dindigul judge sentenced him to eight months’ rigorous imprisonment. A case is also now proceeding in which a Dharmapuram Mutt ascetic is accused of molesting a woman named Vairathammal. Some of these hypocritical religious heads, who claim to have rejected family life, restrained sensual impulses, and undertaken to lead devotees to God’s world, roam about saying that while preparing a road to life in the next world, they also perform many pleasant services in this one!

Still others call themselves heads of religious institutions, enter politics, and engage in destructive work. Because they do not know even the alphabet of politics, they behave childishly, as described above. If Swami Sahajananda says, like the child who declares, “I shall eat only if the cane in Appa’s hand is thrown far away,” that he will enter the village only after the visible black flag is removed, can it be said that he possesses even an atom of political understanding?

A party’s flag is normally removed from a place only after the party beneath that flag has lawfully been defeated by its opponent, not one person remains even to utter its name, and everyone in the country has left it and joined the opposing party, leaving the flag with no further function. Even then, the natural course is to raise another flag in its place without committing the wicked act of burning the former one.

But while a flag, the party it symbolizes, and large numbers of its supporters all continue to exist, only those without ordinary sense would undertake the destructive task of removing that flag, encouraged by official power, assisted by arrogance, and incensed by ignorance. Anyone with even a little sharpness of intellect would not contemplate it even in thought.

Our beloved Swami Sahajananda belongs to a community treated as “untouchable.” According to the Gandhian lexicon, he received the label “Harijan.” If Swami Sahajananda, himself one of the indigenous Dravidian people, says that he will not even look at the black flag which signifies the degradation imposed upon Dravidian people and the demand that it be removed, the extent to which his mind’s eye has been obscured becomes plain.

Though Swami Sahajananda has joined Congress and hidden himself within khadi, he is still placed on the list of “untouchables.” Not only he; his whole community remains on that list under the label “Harijan.” Even after the government decided to abolish untouchability by law, terms such as “Harijan” and “untouchable” remain in practical use. The Dravidar Kazhagam maintains that when legislation was introduced to abolish untouchability, the government should also have prohibited anyone from using degrading expressions such as “Harijan” and “untouchable,” which perpetuate it. Untouchability has been abolished only on paper. In the country and in practice, it still lies comfortably upon mattress and pillow. The arrogance that no one can eradicate it continues its dance in every direction.

Newspapers recently reported that a wedding procession of members of an oppressed community at Kasukkur near Ponnur was attacked by dominant-caste people, as a result of which thirteen people, including the bride and groom, were injured and admitted to hospital. The couple attacked by the dominant-caste people were both reportedly qualified doctors. Yet because they bore the label “Harijan,” they were assaulted and humiliated. Does this incident not show that a law created on paper has not, and cannot by itself, become reality in the country?

We believe this news must have reached Swami Sahajananda too. Yet he is not known to have taken any action about it. He did do one thing: on the very day the wedding party mentioned above was attacked, he went to Seppakkam village and performed the sacred service of removing the black flag.

To put it more clearly, it was the very black flag that symbolizes the removal of the country’s degradations; the abolition of untouchability; the removal of the prefix “Adi” from “Adi-Dravidar” so that everyone may be united under the one name Dravidar; the curbing of dominant-caste arrogance; and the ending of the distinction between rich and poor. It was this flag that our friend Sahajananda, in anger, splendidly completed the rare service of offering to the fire-god.

Like sharpening a blade upon a stone, our friend Sahajananda attacks the very people who work for the advancement of his community. Though the speed of his attack may appear to test the patience of the Dravidar Kazhagam, we declare that the Kazhagam will never lose patience or abandon its noble task of ending the degradation of the country’s people and bringing everyone to equality. It will struggle and triumph until oppressed Sahajanandas become equal and joyful human beings.

People like Swami Sahajananda may regard conduct resembling an elephant scooping up earth with its own trunk and casting it upon its back as their “divine sport.” Recently at Tiruvannamalai, our friend Acharya said that the Self-Respect Movement too was one of God’s plays. In the same manner, if the Swami regarded removing and burning a party’s flag as one of the ruined episodes in his divine sport, let him perform it well and be prepared to accept the profit and loss it brings.

But these lovers of “divine sport” should understand at least this: the Dravidar Kazhagam continues its work precisely to prevent the emergence of a situation in which such accounts of profit and loss must be settled and needless conflicts arise among people.

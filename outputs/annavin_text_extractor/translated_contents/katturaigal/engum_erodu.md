## English Translation

The Goddess's nose-ring is worth three and a half lakh rupees.

The Lord's diamond crown, they say, is worth thirty lakhs!

Is Rangan's pearl robe of any ordinary value? Even a thief would offer fifty lakhs for it!

The emerald embedded in the garland upon Panduranga's chest is as broad as a palm. There is no other emerald like it in the world; no one has yet even assessed its price!

The sacred ornaments at Tirumala-Tirupati alone are said to be worth countless crores of rupees.

Is Thillai in any way deficient? Is what Annamalaiyar possesses at Tiruvannamalai only a little?

Millions of devotees speak in ecstasy, and economic experts with astonishment mixed with pity, in this manner about the immense value of the jewels in our country's temples.

Temple administrators spread a silk robe before the white governors, arrange these ornaments upon it, and display them with elation and pride.

After seeing this “wealth of Kubera,” the governors see the village dignitaries who tell them that Kuppan and Suppan, reduced to loincloth-clad paupers, live half-starved because their village has no river, or has a tank without a proper embankment. The dignitaries submit a petition “praying” that the “lord of charity” show mercy to the poor and the “compassionate government” provide “financial assistance” to relieve their suffering. The governors then climb into their motorcars.

They saw the nine precious gems, and they saw the poor becoming walking corpses! This has been the condition for a very long time.

For many generations, precious gems worth many crores, jewel settings, gold ornaments, and objects of gold and silver have slept in temples: a lion in gold, a bull in silver, a peacock set with emeralds, Garuda with hessonite eyes, a swan inlaid with vermilion-red rubies, a silver chariot with a golden canopy, and objects of that kind.

All this wealth remained imprisoned in the name of the Supreme even during many terrifying events here: the famine of the Dathu year, the Bengal famine, the Bihar earthquake, floods of the Ganges, breaches of the Kaveri, the cruelty of cholera, and food scarcity. Believing that heaven was where they would receive interest upon this money, devotees deposited immense wealth in this Bank of God, generation after generation.

No thought arose about the enormous economic loss and decline in industrial growth caused by immobilising so much wealth. People continued to render service, each according to their means. From time to time the Goddess's marriage pendant, the Lord's sacred thread, a horse's tail, an elephant's tusk, a peacock's plume, or an emerald bracelet would disappear. What if it did? Devotees would panic and detectives investigate. Before the outcome became known, a new devotee would hurry forward to replace the missing object. He might have become a man of great wealth through forged signatures. What of it? Can the object he gives as an offering narrate that history?

Thus immense wealth has remained for many generations without proper accounts or records. During that long period, Thanjavur Palace has fallen into decay, Gingee Fort has become a ruin, and Oomaiyan Fort has become a mound of earth. Countless changes have occurred: disorder, upheaval, changes of government, and minor invasions. In such conditions, who knows how many precious jewels offered to temples generations ago disappeared, or how many gems changed places? Such enormous wealth remains in temples without record or purpose.

The first people to bring this condition to public attention, and thereby even incur public anger, were the Self-Respecters.

They placed the country's poverty and the temples' wealth side by side. They argued that a government which appears to lament that it cannot find a way to uplift the poor behaves like someone crying that there is no ghee while butter lies at hand. Amid fierce opposition, it was the Self-Respecters who continued saying this. The country knows it; the government ruling the country knows it too.

Not only in this “sacred land,” but in every country on earth, when wealth first accumulated, devotees began by heaping great riches in places of worship.

Mahalingam, who keeps accounts in a wholesale shop, earns a little money, opens his own shop, and becomes a person of some standing. Immediately he replaces the ordinary rudraksha beads formerly around his neck with gold-covered beads, and his wooden container for sacred ash with one made of silver. Instead of going to Palani and feeding three mendicants, he plates the temple steps at Palani with gold. In this fashion he makes his devotion shine.

The nature of this individual becomes the nature of the country.

In early times, as the story of Kannappa Nayanar says, a devotee offered wild flowers and cooked meat. Later, he made gold and gems into articles of worship.

In many countries this habit of accumulating offerings spread, and temples became repositories of greater wealth than the houses of kings.

Only after intellectual clarity arose, and with it clarity about God, did many countries acquire the nobility to say: immobilising wealth in this way serves neither devotion, the proclamation of God's greatness, nor improvement in ordinary people's lives. Wealth that could uplift the poor and make life prosperous should not be wasted as crowns, medallions, armour, plates, sandals, palanquins, serpent figures and chariots. This wasteful practice must stop. These riches must be transformed into schools, hospitals, homes for the poor, and charitable institutions that cure the disease of deprivation.

This outlook spread rapidly through many countries.

The esteem once given to one who built a temple and performed its consecration faded, while the esteem of one who founded a school and served education began to rise.

A few people unable to abandon antiquity continued building useless bhajan halls and lamenting; those with the new outlook began founding universities.

Although so many changes were taking place throughout the world, only in our country did Lord Siva continue his procession upon a golden bull. Did he merely continue then? He continues even now!

The Self-Respecter, subjected to condemnation and ridicule, alone continued to envision this immense wealth being liberated and transformed into factories, educational institutions, hospitals, and homes caring for the destitute. Even today these remain imagined scenes. They will remain so for a little longer, but not for long. The day of awakening approaches swiftly; a thousand signs are visible.

A few days ago, during some function at Tiruvannamalai, a government officer asked, “Could not at least a portion of the money spent upon the temple be given to education?” That is all he could ask. Even for that, some will have begun to suspect him: “Has the fellow caught Erode fever?”

Limited though it was, does the incident not show that a senior officer recognises that the people of this country spend too much on temple affairs while showing no comparable enthusiasm for education, and that he comes forward with inner conviction to tell the public what he has realised? Does it not mean the cock is crowing? The Self-Respecters rejoice at such signs.

The Self-Respecters could speak only in general terms, saying, “So much money lies accumulated in the temples,” because complete statistics were not available, indeed did not exist.

We are delighted now to hear that our provincial government intends to prepare those statistics.

We learn this from a report published in *The Hindu* on September 26:

> All arrangements have been completed to collect and record full particulars and valuations of the jewels, precious gems, and valuable objects in the temples and religious institutions of Madras Province.
>
> These costly temple ornaments are renowned, but until now no attempt has been made either to determine their value or to arrange for their proper protection. A. Balakrishnan, former secretary of the Hindu Religious Administration Board, has been appointed to inspect and record the ornaments with the assistance of an expert in precious stones, an expert in gold jewellery, and an artist. Where old records exist, he will note whether the jewels remain in the recorded condition and will also offer recommendations for protecting these ornaments.

That is the information published in *The Hindu*.

This collection of statistics may even be preparation for an important undertaking.

We are not fools who imagine that, as soon as the statistics are collected, the present government will issue an order saying:

> Why should so many crores of rupees lie immobilised? Use these jewels given to the Lord for the country's public welfare. The serpent-adorned wearer of the tiger skin will not rejoice over these green and blue gems. He will rejoice upon seeing the happiness of life appear on his children's faces. Therefore transform this useless heap of ornaments into instruments that give the people a good life.

The present government will not acquire such courage. The moss of antiquated attachment in the rulers' minds will not permit the purity of heart required for it. Even so, they are taking an “account” and showing concern that the riches “must be protected.” Up to this point, we are very pleased, because we trust that once this account is produced, their minds will begin to work.

The friends preparing these statistics and the people who will see them should occasionally read and benefit from the little book *Temple Cats*. Its author formerly worked for the Hindu Religious Administration Board and now writes under the pen name Kovaikkizhar. He is not a Self-Respecter but a sincere Saivite.

It would be enough to publish a single volume combining the statistics prepared by our friend Balakrishnan, that book, information about the country's present needs such as roads, bridges, dams, hospitals and schools, and the ministers' occasional speeches explaining why the present government cannot meet those needs. After that, every town will be Erode!

These statistics will help people understand the condition and thereby begin thinking about sound ideas for national progress. This work of taking “accounts and records” will also make the “temple cats” realise that their rampage can no longer continue. Then reason will begin to work.

*(Published in* Dravida Nadu, *October 1948.)*

## Translator's Notes

`Erode` in the title and the phrase “Erode fever” signify the Self-Respect and rationalist outlook associated with E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar), whose political base was Erode. The conclusion predicts that documentary evidence about temple wealth will spread that outlook to every town.

`Ammai`, `Ayyan`, `Rangan`, `Panduranga`, `Annamalaiyar`, and the serpent-adorned wearer of tiger skin refer to temple deities. `Kubera` is the deity of wealth. `Kuppan and Suppan` are conventional names representing ordinary poor people. `Navaratna` denotes the traditional nine precious gems. `Dathu-year famine` refers to the severe Madras famine associated with the Tamil cyclic year Dathu. `Kainkaryam` means devotional service. `Rudraksha`, `vibhuti`, `kumbabhishekam`, and `bhajan` retain familiar religious terms.

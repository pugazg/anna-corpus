9. கொத்தனார் பிரின்சிபாலாகிறார்!

கொத்தனார் குமாரசாமி என்னைப் பார்த்துச் சிரித்தார். நானும்
இலேசாகச் சிரித்தேன்.

அவர் ஏன் என்னைப் பார்த்துச் சிரித்தார் என்று முதலில் எனக்குப்
புரியவில்லை. இருந்தும் இருவரும் சிரித்தோம்.

''கொத்தனாரே, ஏன் சிரிக்கிறீர்கள்?' என்றேன் நான். அவர்
சிரிப்பிற்குக் காரணம் பிறகு தெரியாமல் போகவில்லை தெரிந்தது.
தெரிந்தது மட்டுமல்ல எனக்கும் அந்த மகிழ்ச்சியில் பங்கு உண்டு.

கல்வியிலே நாட்டமுள்ள காளைகள் ஏராளம் தோன்றிவிட்டனர்.
ஆனால், அந்தப் பகுதிக்கு ஒரு கல்லூரி இல்லை. இந்தக் குறையைத் தீர்க்க
ஊரார் ஒன்று கூடினர். திட்டம் வகுத்தனர். கல்லூரி கட்டி முடித்துவிடுவது
என்று முடிவு செய்து வேலையும் தொடங்கப்பெற்றது.

கொத்தனார் குமாரசாமி, 'இந்த நல்ல காரியத்தில் நான் முன்னிருந்து
வேலை செய்கிறேன்' என்று கூறிக் கட்டடம் கட்டும் பொறுப்பைத் தானே
எடுத்துக்கொண்டார். ஊராரும், கல்லூரிக் கட்டட வேலையை அவர்
எடுத்துக் கொண்டதால், மற்ற வேலைகளைச் செய்யலாம் என்று அவரவர்
அவரவர்திறமைக்கேற்ற வேலைகளிலீடுபட்டனர்.

நான் எடுத்துக்கொண்ட வேலையோ, மிகவும் தொல்லை தருவது -
கல்லூரிக்கான எல்லாவிதப் பொருள்களையும் நிரப்புவது - விஞ்ஞானப்
பகுதிக்கு வேண்டிய ஆராய்ச்சிப் பகுதிகள், சரித்திரப் பிரிவிற்கான
படங்கள், பழங்காலச் சின்னங்கள், சிற்பங்கள், மொழிப் பகுதிக்கு
வேண்டிய ஆதாரப்பொருள்கள், நூல் நிலையத்திற்கான ஏடுகள் - இப்படி
மிகவும் தொல்லைதரும் சேகரிப்பு வேலையிலே நான் ஈடுபட்டி ருந்தேன்.

"கட்டி முடித்தவரை கண்டு களிப்படைந்தால் போதும் '' என்றார் கடு

என்னைப்போல் கஷ்டப்பட்டிருந்தால் தெரியும் உனக்கு! இத்த ஆறு

## English Translation

Mason Kumarasami looked at me and laughed. I too smiled faintly.

At first I did not understand why he laughed on seeing me. Even so, both of us laughed.

“Mason, why are you laughing?” I asked. I did eventually learn the reason for his laughter. Not only did I learn it, I too had a share in that happiness.

Many young bulls with an interest in education had appeared, but the region had no college. To remedy this deficiency, the townspeople assembled and drew up a plan. They decided to build and complete a college, and work began.

Mason Kumarasami said, “I shall take the lead in this good undertaking,” and personally assumed responsibility for constructing the building. Since he had taken charge of the college building, the townspeople were free to do the other work, and each undertook tasks suited to his own ability.

The job I assumed was extremely troublesome: furnishing the college with every kind of material. There were research instruments required by the science department; pictures, ancient relics and sculptures for the history department; source materials for the language department; and manuscripts for the library. I had immersed myself in this very difficult work of collection.

He laughed when he saw me, and there was mockery mingled in that laughter. That was why I pretended to know nothing and asked my question.

When my question reached his ears, lines of astonishment crossed his face and a light of wonder entered his eyes. He remained silent for a moment and then spoke.

“Do you not understand why I am laughing?” he countered, turning his gaze behind him.

I knew that he meant to draw my eyes towards the newly completed college building. So I spoke as though I had only then grasped the reason for his laughter.

“Is it the joy that the college build...ing has been completed?” I asked.

“Would there not be joy? Why do you say it so feebly?” His voice was harsh.

I managed that tone and continued.

“But the student hostel has not yet been built,” I said.

His scorpion-tail moustache began to twitch.

“It is enough to rejoice over what has been completed,” he snapped.

“I did not say I felt no joy,” I replied very calmly.

“How much I suffered to finish this building! You would understand if you had endured what I did. I am frightened merely to think of the hardships I faced over these six years,” he said. It was plain that he spoke with sorrow. I did not interrupt, and he continued.

“How hard I struggled to obtain the stones, iron beams, sacks of cement, windows, doors and all the other things needed to complete this college building! I went hungry for many days. I walked to towns where stone was available. Famished, I wandered from place to place.” His voice trembled. I made sympathetic “tsk, tsk” sounds.

“Was that all? How many people I begged to bring in other masons! The suffering I endured in hiring labourers and women workers and accelerating construction is something no one else in the world could possibly have endured!”

I laughed. His trembling lips silently announced that his anger had grown.

“Mason, please do not be angry. I am not ignorant of the hardships you have just described. The townspeople ought to mention them and praise you for overcoming them all. But you yourself are saying what they ought to say!”

I continued speaking. He tried to throw some question into the middle of my words, but could not. I went on.

“You said that no one else in the world could have suffered as you did. That is true.” Delight appeared on his face.

“At least you admit that! There is room for that much fairness in your heart,” he said proudly.

“There is a proper reason, Mason. No one in the world has endured these agonies to erect a building, because no one needed to! Just as you did, others built Pak University, established the Burmese institute and created Ceylon’s palace of thought. They began after you began and closed their work at the very time you finished. Yet they did not suffer as you did, and they completed buildings superior to yours.”

“There is poison in your speech,” he said.

“Not poison, Mason. Call it wisdom. You made difficult something that should have been finished easily. You turned a simple task into a cavern of misery. You must admit it.”

“You wretch!” he cried.

“Forgive me, Mason. You are a mason, and is not Ghulam Qadir a mason too? He too erected a building, just as you did. He did not smear himself with mud as you did or allow falling stones to injure him. Yet the building was created. Pak University has grown in a manner praised by the world.”

He reached the limit of his anger and shouted. I still laughed.

“That is how you will speak. But you have no qualification to criticise me!”

“You wrongly imagine that I am criticising you. I stated the truth, and I shall say more. You took six years to complete this building. Others have erected many beautiful buildings in six months.” His face grew redder and redder.

“You did not so much as move a hand to complete the building, but now you shout until your mouth splits! You did not lift even one brick, mix mortar, or carry a single pot of water to make the mud.”

“Therefore I have no qualification to criticise you?”

“Exactly.”

“I do not know what meaning you give the word ‘qualification.’ But you must understand something else. A college is not merely an empty building. Many things must still be assembled inside it. You must recognise this truth.”

“You have discovered a very great truth!”

“Mason, though I do not know how to hold a mason’s trowel, I know how to grasp ideas of this kind. Put away your anger and listen calmly for a moment.

“You say I did not lift stones or mix mortar for the building. True; I admit it. Because you assumed that responsibility, I stepped aside and engaged in other work. But it was not work outside the college. It was work vital to the college!

“How many kinds of companies have I visited for scientific training equipment! For historical research I travelled to every corner of the country. To collect manuscripts, how long I waited at the doorways of so many homes! Why do you refuse to understand that the task succeeds when people divide the work among themselves, rather than everyone doing the same thing at once?”

“All of that came only after the building.”

“Yes. We praise you for completing the building. We are happy and offer our congratulations.”

I expected him to end the conversation there. Not only did he refuse to stop, he spoke further, and what he said left me breathless.

“I built the building. You do not deny that.”

“I do not deny it, Mason. You built it and received praise and rewards for doing so.”

“Answer only what I ask. We built this building expressly for a college.”

“Yes.”

“Then does the builder not also possess the right to administer it?”

“Mason, you are making a mistake here; you are slipping and falling over your own words.”

“What?”

“You built the building. For whom? For the many young people suffering for want of a place. They staged plays to contribute funds and sang songs to raise money. They helped in every possible manner. They asked you to build, not even because you alone knew how, but because you were the first to volunteer. That was all the townspeople expected. But you say, ‘I built it, and therefore I shall rule it!’”

“Can I not rule what I built?”

“Whether you can or cannot is not even the issue. The issue before us is whether you know how. You know how to construct a building. You should stop with that.”

“Do not talk nonsense. I built the building; therefore I alone am the college principal! Not only that: those who worked under me are the professors, the labourers are all lecturers, and the bricks are the books!”

“You are speaking with your own independent thought, are you not?”

“Mind your words. I speak not merely with independent thought but with self-determination. I built it, so I must rule what I built.”

“Rule what you built, then, but it will cease to be a college!”

“Why can it not remain one?”

“Running a college is not the same as erecting an ordinary building.”

“Let us try running it!”

“Why try? Merely thinking about it tells us what will happen. Will children come there to study? Even if they do, they will wander about as brick-bearers, singing, ‘There is a stone in my hand, but no dog in the street.’”

“You are a madman!”

Mason Kumarasami said this and walked away. Only then did I understand that something was not quite right with him.

“The mason is going to become principal!” The words made me laugh despite myself. At first I thought he was joking. When I realised that he meant it seriously, my suspicion grew.

I asked around the town about him.

They said the joy of completing the building had changed him. He had laboured to construct it, they explained, and excessive happiness upon its completion had made him this way. What a pity!

With my own eyes I had seen Mason Kumarasami’s exertions while completing the building, and I had praised his labour.

But I never expected that, once it was complete, he would wander about raving in opposition to the very good purpose for which it had been built.

Mason Kumarasami ought to have rejoiced, saying, “We built and gave this structure; a great college functions within it, and every year thousands study and earn degrees.” Instead he wanders babbling, “I am the principal too,” and the townspeople look upon him with pity.

*(Dravida Nadu, 25 February 1951)*

## Translator's Notes

The source presents a political or institutional allegory entirely through the story of a mason claiming the right to govern a college because he built its structure. The translation retains the source’s names and institutional labels, including Pak University, the Burmese institute, Ceylon’s “palace of thought,” and Ghulam Qadir, without imposing an identification not explicitly stated in the article. *Karanai* is rendered contextually as a mason’s trowel. The colloquial song about carrying a stone while no dog is in the street is translated literally.

## English Translation

A magnificent festival, decorations, dazzling displays of pomp and a ceremonial parade: the Indian Republic witnessed all of them on Republic Day, 26 January.

Messages of greeting poured in from many corners of the world, accumulating in heaps. The greetings, pronouncements and speeches of praise sent by America, Russia, England, France, Pakistan and other countries may have given even the rulers a degree of indigestion.

Many messages of congratulation spoke of the nation's political ideals, their character, the emotion they had acquired and the advancement they would achieve.

Messages praised Pandit Nehru as the man coming to create a new India, as its founding source. They honoured his service and his heart and joyfully wished him long life.

Republic Day is an important date in the history of the Indian subcontinent. No one can deny it.

It is natural and unsurprising that on that day messages should arrive praising and congratulating both India and the Pandit who guides it.

At that magnificent celebration, rulers explained to the people in various places, through speeches and films, “We accomplished this and that; we provided these benefits to the people of this subcontinent.” There is no surprise in that either, nor anything that ought not to happen, except that while recounting their achievements they mixed in a little stock of their own invention.

Had they stopped with the discourse, “Look at India; see the growth it has achieved; recognise the heights it is going to reach,” no one would have had reason for surprise or pain.

Instead, on that very day they issued several appeals and, in a persuasive tone, a few announcements and commands. That is what caused not only us but all the people of the country both surprise and pain. It raised a question mark.

The same rulers who displayed films of Bhakra-Nangal and the Damodar scheme, inviting all the country's people to take part in the splendid celebration and see how India had grown, raised another cry: “Friends, tighten your belts; reduce expenditure; let thrift spread.” No one could understand it.

Decorations and parades on one side; an emotional appeal on another: “Help in the noble task of protecting the Five-Year Plan; reduce your own needs.” On one side military strength was exhibited. On another the people of India were told: “These are our difficulties; these are our economic problems.”

A magnificent celebration took place, and within it a sermon was delivered to those whose bellies burned with hunger.

Rulers caught and floundering amid many troubles and sorrows, desires and disappointments, hatreds and pains may need festivals of this sort. Such occasions may be indispensable for refreshing their minds.

On festival days like these, rulers are duty-bound to reflect upon their objective, the means of attaining it, the successes and failures encountered thus far upon that path, and the causes behind them.

If they recognise this duty and act accordingly, there is nothing wrong in conducting such festivals and celebrations. Everyone would wholeheartedly support them as necessary.

But if the rulers see the splendour, fireworks and illuminations and make the false calculation that the whole country is immersed in delight and its people lack nothing, harm will come not only to them but to the subcontinent entrusted to their hands.

However skilfully festivals and entertainments may be conducted, we cannot forget one great truth, however bitter it may be.

It is those occupying the seat of government who rejoice at the festivals and the splendour and delight poured into them, not ordinary people.

The people hold a different opinion of those festivals.

They regard them as performances staged by rulers who wallow in luxury to please their own hearts. They show neither eagerness nor enthusiasm for such celebrations, nor do these occasions fill their hearts with joy.

There are many who ask in anguish, “Is this yet another drama intended to deceive the people?” Many others speak in voices mixed with mockery and ridicule: “Are all these spectacles not displayed to cover and conceal the wretched conditions and shameful events that abound in government?”

Thousands say that the true festivals capable of demonstrating an improved standard of life and a life without want are the joy that surges in people's hearts, the delight shown in their eyes, the smile shining upon their lips and the music of happiness arising from their hearts.

Food to eat, a house in which to live, opportunities to work, a means of receiving wages appropriate to that labour: in brief, an improvement in their standard of living, the condition described by the words “there is no man called poor or slave within society.” These alone can bring happiness to people's hearts, not parades of the three armed services or periodic sermons from rulers proclaiming, “Ah, the country has grown so greatly!”

Even those standing intoxicated by the greetings piled up from many corners of the world cannot and must not forget the truth that none of the Republic Day splendour produced delight in the people's hearts.

On festival days such as Republic Day, praising the people or publishing a list of the small benefits rulers have provided them cannot create joy in their hearts. Nor does that amount to the government performing its duty.

Instead, rulers should think through and weigh all the tasks left undone but requiring completion, works begun but unfinished, and activities not performed efficiently. They should examine why there are obstructions in those activities and how they may be removed, and then enter carefully upon the work. Festivals such as Republic Day should be used as foundation ceremonies for such efforts.

It is not enough merely for rulers to possess concern for public welfare. They must make the people recognise that they display such concern. These are the works that will raise the country. The people must be trained to participate in the great responsibility of examining all these matters.

Only then will the activities undertaken by governments receive popular support. If such support is absent, the plans drawn by rulers will yield no benefit.

You must understand how far these rulers have won the people, and you must reform yourselves.

It is true that the people do not hold in their hands numerous publications explaining statistics and plans.

Yet they possess a worthy treasury: common sense and mature experience, which rulers have so far failed to use.

Because most of them are illiterate, one cannot conclude that they lack the ability to understand.

Rulers must not allow the wealth of knowledge filled within them to decay unused.

Those occupying positions of government and its many offices have much to explain to the people. Yet there is also a great deal they must learn from them.

Unless those in office explain the truth to the people, accept the advice the people give them, and create a bond between the two sides, no plan prepared by rulers will bear fruit.

Unless those in positions of government recognise all these truths on festival days such as Republic Day, the celebrations conducted on those days will be water poured upon barren ground and yield no benefit.

We are not alone in pointing out and condemning the inadequate connection between rulers and those they govern.

Here is the opinion of Gadgil, an economist who had served on the Planning Commission itself:

> “Rulers must understand the country's economic condition properly. Instead, they blindly believe that announcements of enormous plans and lengthy speeches about them will produce happiness. Delhi's imperial history, the displays conducted there, its imposing mansions, the magnificent lives of kings and the way they regarded society: all these are immense obstacles preventing today's rulers from properly understanding the country's condition.”

Our only wish is that our rulers should not turn Republic Day into yet another extravagant festival. They should use it as an opportunity to understand the state of the country.

When we say this, we know that the “minor deities” within the Congress tent will rage at us, and we know the angle from which they view these festivals.

They calculate that decorative celebrations, pomp and parades will produce delight in the people's hearts, and that this delight will help them at election time. That is all.

But for some reason our rulers refuse to consider that people who come to see these festivals compare the pomp there with the wretched conditions in their own homes. Nor do they consider what anguish may arise in people's hearts from that comparison, or what its outcome may be.

Many revolutions have arisen from the upheaval produced by precisely such comparisons.

Are those holding responsibility for government trying to create such a dangerous situation through the extravagant displays of festival days like Republic Day?

8 February 1959

## English Translation

The Soviet Union desires world peace. By peace it does not mean a doctrine of non-violence or a willingness to surrender. Peace is a natural characteristic arising from the Soviet system. There is no capitalist group in the Soviet country that profits from war, nor are there black-market vultures. Soviet Russia has no fanatics who pile up cannon and explosives and invite war. Its production plan is designed for use, not for profit.

Soviet leaders understand well that peaceful life cannot truly exist until a socialist order has been established throughout the world. Yet they believe a time of peace, rather than war, is better suited to the progress of the world's workers and the growth of socialism. Soviet history clearly records how hard Russia has worked for world peace. Only destitute people who have not read history cry that the Soviet Union has become an imperialist power bent on dragging the world into war.

Within half an hour of Soviet power being established in 1917, the Soviet government issued an appeal for peace. It sought peace with the countries engaged in war and declared that no territorial expansion or humiliating condition should be imposed through a settlement. The Soviet government demonstrated this in action. It tore up the Tsar's secret agreements to annex Constantinople to Russia and partition Turkey and Persia. It recalled Russian troops from the Persian frontier and rejected the Tsar's unjust agreements with Mongolia, China and Afghanistan.

Despite Lenin's strenuous efforts, no country except Germany came forward for peace. With no alternative, Russia concluded a separate peace with Germany. Lenin thereby gained time to restore a country weakened and shattered by revolution. But the great powers did not wish to give Russia peace.

Fourteen capitalist powers rose against the Soviet state, led by the “compassionate” British government. British imperialism spent one crore pounds—thirteen crore rupees—to crush the revolution. The governments of France, America, Japan and others cooperated with Britain's effort.

Workers throughout the world came forward to aid the Soviet system. Dock workers refused to load ships with weapons and explosives intended for use against Russia. Workers in the imperial countries raised the slogan “Hands off Russia!” The conspiracies against the Soviet Union failed, and little by little the powers were compelled to recognise the Soviet federation.

Once it gained respite, the Soviet government began implementing socialist economic plans in Russia. In foreign affairs, Russia made peace treaties with Iran, Afghanistan and Persia in 1921, with republican Germany in 1922, and with China in 1924. The other powers refused to respect Russia. At the Genoa Conference of 1922 they introduced resolutions against it. Chief among their demands were that the Soviet Union repay Tsarist debts, submit to the powers' economic control and immediately abandon its socialist programmes.

Who would summon a doctor who offers treatment only after life has departed? The Soviet power rejected the resolutions. Hostility towards it increased. In 1923, the English lord Curzon prepared a plan against the Soviet Union in Britain. Pressure upon the Soviet state intensified, and the Soviet representative Vorovsky was murdered.

The intentions of the great powers were perfectly clear to the Soviet Union. It did not come forward to begin a war over minor disputes. By 1925 Russia had established long-term agreements with every country. Japan alone did not accept the Russian appeal. Anti-Soviet propaganda again intensified in 1927, and Russia endured it.

Russia was the first to accept and put into practice the Kellogg peace proposals formulated in 1929.

The other powers merely spoke about peace while continuing war production. Russia alone opposed this. Soviet representatives fought vigorously at the disarmament conference. The powers would not agree to complete disarmament. Russia then introduced a resolution to reduce military preparation at least to a limited degree; that too was rejected.

By 1934 the imperial powers had reduced the disarmament conference to a leisure-time occupation. Every great power was busily preparing instruments of war, and fascism had established its camp in Germany. Russia tried to convene a world peace conference to preserve peace. With that hope, it joined the League of Nations in 1934. The fascist governments had already left the League; the powers remaining in it passed their time without concern. Only the smaller countries understood the danger and trusted the League. Russia entered believing it might serve as a weak defence.

In 1935 Soviet Russia concluded agreements with France and Czechoslovakia. The fascist countries and the British government condemned the Soviet peace treaties. British imperial diplomats went further and concluded a naval agreement with Nazi Germany in violation of international law. Believing the German fascists to be powerful enemies of the Soviet republic, the British government gave them full assistance. The imperialist camps became close friends.

Japan attacked China. Italy invaded Abyssinia. A fascist movement grew in Spain. Under the pretext of “non-intervention,” the imperial powers stood aside. Russia alone denounced these daylight robberies. It continuously supported China against Japan, made the Abyssinian question important despite Italy's opposition, and helped the Spanish people resist fascist massacre.

Meanwhile, confronted by Soviet strength, Germany began to yield and was prepared to reach an agreement. This was no secret. Until two days before the Russo-German agreement, Russia invited Britain to conclude an agreement. With a great invasion hanging before it, Russia had no alternative but to make an agreement with Germany. The respite gained by postponing war upon Soviet soil was essential.

Although Britain had declared war upon Germany, its hostility towards the Soviet Union did not diminish. To British lords, Hitler's entry into war was not so great a crime; his peace with a socialist government was the unforgivable offence. They expected the fortunes of war to change. They supplied weapons to fascist Finland against the Soviet Union. Even when fascism had enslaved all Europe, Churchill's associates did not relax their Soviet hostility.

The British government again refused an agreement with the Soviet Union, while at the same time making a trade agreement to supply Japan with oil. The Soviet republic alone possessed both the strength and ideological power to oppose fascism, yet Britain's ruling class placed no trust in it.

The Polish government, having rejected Soviet assistance, fled before Hitler's armies. To prevent Germany gaining control, the Soviet Union protected eastern Poland and the Baltic countries. Finland prepared itself against the Soviet state. The British and French governments sent auxiliary forces to Finland's fascist leader and Hitler's agent, Mannerheim.

The agreement Russia made with Finland at the war's end was historically significant. It returned the occupied territory to Finland. Russia demanded neither compulsory payment nor land as war reparations. When Finland later joined Germany and attacked Russia, even English and French politicians said Russia had shown excessive generosity in its agreement with Finland.

After France fell, Russia became still more vigilant and strengthened the countries along its frontiers. On 22 June 1941 Hitler's battle lines sprang upon Russia—and leapt into the grave.

All the military strength fascism had accumulated across Europe was hurled against Russia. It was a terrible war. Four years later fascism was destroyed and the Red Flag flew over Berlin.

Russia once more laid the foundation for firm peace. It did not wish to destroy Germany. At the victory celebration, the warrior Stalin gave this assurance: “Some people may appear and disappear from Germany's political stage. But the Germans remain. The German nation will live!” These were words that gave hope to a weakened country—an unwavering commitment to peace. That is the Soviet character. With Soviet assistance, the Eastern European countries and Germany are advancing towards peaceful life.

The Soviet course disgusts the Anglo-American imperialists. Russia condemned reactionary politicians in disputes involving Greece, Indonesia, Spain and other countries. The Soviet country has nothing to gain from war. For Churchill's group, talk of war is a miraculous elixir. Our own illustrious political leaders surpass even the Anglo-American warmongers. The stench of fascism is vile. Alas, it is growing.

(18 May 1947)

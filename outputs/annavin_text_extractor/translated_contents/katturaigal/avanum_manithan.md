## English Translation

It is true that someone died of starvation—but not thirty-three people; one man, and he was only an ordinary beggar!

This is the information supplied by the Honourable Munshi, the message delivered to the people by a minister who came promising to give them life.

Our tears rise, our throats close, and our hearts swell and drain away when we hear the sorrowful news that a man who lived among us has died for want of food.

They read aloud, “Beat the drum and proclaim that the condition called want of food shall be no more.” They sang, “If a single man has no food, we shall destroy the world.” They spoke as though they understood Valluvar’s words: “If one must live by begging, may the creator of this world wander and perish.” Yet they tell people to die of starvation and hunger.

Before mounting the seat of government, Congressmen posed a thousand riddles. But what they have now brought with them is death by starvation. Shame, shame! Pain and torment—this is a needless ordeal.

The death is true. He does not deny that someone died. But the number who died was not thirty-three; it was one, and he was an ordinary beggar! So says the Honourable Minister, without the slightest tremor in his heart or faltering of his tongue. What indifference! What arrogance!

A beggar died of starvation. Are we therefore not to care? Why this conceited language? In a union of thirty crores of people, did he not have membership too? If everyone is a sovereign of this country, is he alone an exception? He was one of the atoms that together constitute Indian society—an important atom that ought to have been cared for. Allowing him to become a beggar was itself an unforgivable crime. Allowing him to starve and then speaking without even a feeling of pity is cruelty and a grave wrong.

He was a beggar, but he was human. He had life, legs to walk the earth, hands to work, a mouth to speak, a tongue to taste, ears to hear, and eyes to see. His dancing, singing, and seeking strangers’ doorways were all for the sake of that half-empty stomach. His song, “My precious golden one,” proves that he too had a stomach. With no way to fill it in this Ram Rajya, he died for want of sustenance. The government ought to bow its head, yet it speaks arrogantly. The Honourable Minister calmly says that the dead man was a beggar.

Learning of reports of starvation deaths in Bihar province, the minister flew over the five affected districts. He returned and graciously declared:

“The prevailing panic is the reason people say starvation deaths are increasing. People who died long ago, and those who died of old age or diseases such as malaria and cholera, are being described as having died for want of food.”

So speaks the minister. Up to that point, very well. But what he said next provokes anger and brings tears surging from compassionate hearts. He seeks reassurance by admitting that one man did starve but saying that the man was a beggar. That torments our hearts and arrests our thoughts. Why did this minister forget that he too was human?

Because the stock of rice in Tiruchirappalli district has declined, the ration of seven ounces of rice in the preliminary rationing areas will soon be reduced to six ounces.

What “sweet” news for the people of Tiruchirappalli! A joyful message from our national government—agony! Not Tiruchirappalli alone; the condition is the same in every direction across India.

The British said sixteen. Prakasam Garu said twelve. Omandurar said ten. Now those in power sing eight, seven, six. Whatever else they may fail to do, they cheat the stomach. How can the people endure it? How many days can they bear this cruelty to the stomach? The rulers must understand. Yet with conditions as they are, a minister of the central government declares that only one man died, forgetting that he too was human.

“The expected assistance has not come from the central government. Procurement too has deteriorated. Help must be given to manage the food situation.”

Thus Madras State Food Minister Roche Victoria sings in the plaintive raga Mukhari. Yes, this is nothing they did not know, nor is it the first time the central government has stretched out an empty hand. Unless these ministers begin an Adana of action, the country’s Mohanam will not endure. Yet he stops after lamenting in a sorrowful voice that help has not come from the centre.

If they do not help, are people simply to lie starving and die? Even if they do, the central government will stoutly say that the dead man was no great lord, only an unknown beggar. And these ministers will have no choice but to go on singing Mukhari.

The people ask only for food. A government that cannot provide even that is a caricature in the new world, the embodiment of disgrace. The people do not ask for ostentatious living. They ask to eat without starving. They do not aspire to live in splendour; they say it is enough if they can eat at least half a stomach and keep their lives from departing. Yet the sorrowful news that they are dying of starvation bores into our ears and strikes the heart.

Those in power must reflect upon this dreadful condition. Instead, the Food Minister must not speak as Munshi does and thrust a spear into an open wound. People are dying for want of food. What would true rulers of the people do on hearing this? They would writhe like worms cast into fire and run about working to set the food situation right. They would not speak haughtily and double the existing sorrow.

It is true that someone died of starvation. Whether the dead man was a beggar or a famous lord, what must the minister do? He must find a way to prevent such heart-rending deaths.

If he says, “The dead man was a beggar, so there is no need for concern,” he must remember that the man too was one of the children who crawled in Mother India’s lap. Like the minister, he too was a human being—a complete human being, a human being with feelings. What will happen if this number grows? The Honourable Minister must reflect—the same Honourable Munshi who only yesterday declared that he would not permit starvation deaths. Yet it is he who speaks this way.

*Dravida Nadu*, 8 August 1950.

## Translator's Notes

The article quotes Bharati and the *Tirukkural* while attacking the minister’s attempt to minimize a starvation death because the victim was a beggar. Its Mukhari–Adana–Mohanam passage plays simultaneously on Carnatic raga names and moods or action; the names are retained and briefly signalled in the translation.

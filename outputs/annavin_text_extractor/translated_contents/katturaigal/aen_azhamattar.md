ஏன் அழமாட்டார்?

சின்னாட்களுக்கு மூன், திருச்சிக்கருகில் உள்ள லால்குடி
என்னும் ஊரில் சைவப் பிரசங்கம் ஒன்று நடை பெற்றதாம்.
அப்போது, ஒரு 'சைவ மெய்யன்பர்' பெரிய புராணம்
என்னும்பொருள் பற்றிப் பேசும்போது, அழுதழுது கண்ணீர்
வடித்தாராம். பெரிய புராணத்தில், குறிப்பிட்ட சில நிகழ்ச்சிகளை
எடுத்து விளக்கிப் பேசும்போது அவரையுமறி யாமல் அவர்
கண்கள் நீரைக் கக்குமாம்; தேம்பித் தேம்பி அழுவாராம்; ஒரு
விநாடி அப்படியே திகைத்து நிற்பாராம்; மறுபடியும் தொடர்ந்து
பேசுவாராம்; மீண்டும் அழுகை வந்துவிடுமாம்; கண்ணீர்
உகுப்பாராம்; இந்த முறையில் அவருடைய பிரசங்கம்
நடைபெற்றதாகப் பத்திரிகையில் ஒரு செய்தி வந்துள்ளது.

உண்மையாகவே, பெரிய புராணத்தைப் படிக்கும்போது,
அதிலுள்ள பல நிகழ்ச்சிகள், படிப்பவர் உள்ளத்தைத் திடுக்கிடச்
செய்து, 'ஆ! இப்படியா?! என்று கண்ணீர் விட்டு அழும்
நிலையையே உண்டாக்கியிருக்கும் என்பதில் ஐயம் இல்லை.
எடுத்துக்காட்டாகச் சில நிகழ்ச்சிகளை மட்டும் இங்கு
குறிப்பிடுகிறோம்.

கலியர் என்பவர் சென்னைக்கடுத்த திருவொற்றியூரில்
இருந்த ஒரு சிவனடியார்; அறுபத்து மூவரில் ஒருவர். சிவன்
கோயிலுக்கு விளக்கேற்றுவது இவர் தம் பணி. கையில் இருந்த
பொருள் எல்லாம் சிவனுக்கு விளக்கேற்றுவதிலே செலவு
செய்யப்பட்டு விட்டது. மேற்கொண்டு விளக்கேற்றப் பணம்
இல்லை. உடனே அவருக்கு ஒரு யோசனை தோன்றிற்று.
வீட்டுக்குள் இருந்த தம்முடைய மனைவியைக் கூப்பிட்டார்.
கடைத் தெருவுக்கு அழைத்துச் சென்றார் - “இவள் என் மனைவி;
இவளை விற்கப் போகிறேன்; வாங்குவாருண்டா?'' என்று பேசி
விலை கூறினார். வாங்குவாரில்லை. அதோடு விட்டாரா கலியர்!
தெருத் தெருவாய்ச் சென்று விலை கூறினார்; அப்போதும் அவளை
வாங்குவாரில்லை.

கட்டிய மனைவியைக் கடைத்தெருவில் கொண்டுபோய்
விலை கூறினான் ஒரு தமிழன் என்ற செய்தியைப் புராணத்தில்
படித்தால் இப்படியும் ஒருவன் மானங்கெட்டு நடந்திருக்கலாமா
என்று எண்ணி யாரால்தான் அழாமல் இருக்க முடியும்!

கட்டிய மனைவியைக் கடைத்தெருவில் விலை கூறிய

போகவே, எண்ணெய் வாங்கி விளக்கேற்ற முடியவில்லை.
உடனே அவருக்கு இன்னொரு யோசனை தோன்றிற்று. அதாவது
எண்ணெய்க்குப் பதில் தம்முடைய உடம்பிலுள்ள குருதியைக்
கொண்டு விளக்கேற்றுவது என்ற முடிவுக்கு வந்து, தம்முடைய
கழுத்தை வெட்டினாராம். இப்படி ஒருவன் - இரத்தத்திற்கும்
எண்ணெய்க்கும் உள்ள வேறுபாட்டைக் கூட அறிந்துகொள்ள
முடியாமல், இரத்தத்தை எண்ணெய் என்று எண்ணி, அது
கொண்டு விளக்கேற்றலாம் எனக் கருதித் தன்னுடைய
கழுத்தையே ஒரு தமிழன் வெட்டினான் என்ற நிகழ்ச்சியைக்
கூறும்போது, இப்படியும் ஒரு 'மூதறிஞன்' தமிழினத்தில்
இருந்ததாகப் புராணத்தில் எழுதப்பட்டிருக்கிறதே என்றெண்ணி
அந்த அன்பர் அழுதிருப்பார்.

இனி, கோட்புலி என்ற ஒரு சிவனடியார், வேளாள
வகுப்பைச் சார்ந்தவர், சோழ மன்னன் ஒருவனுக்குப் படைத்
தலைவனாய் இருந்தார். இவர், தமக்குக் கிடைக்கும் ஊதியம்
முழுவதையும் சிவன் கோயில்களில் நடைபெறும் வழி
பாட்டுக்காக நெல் வாங்கிக் கொடுப்பதில் செலவு செய்பவர்.

அரசன் ஆணைப்படி, கோட்புலி என்பார் ஒரு போர்
முனைக்குப் போக நேர்ந்தது. உடனே, கோட்புலி, கோயிலுக்கு
வேண்டிய நெற்குவையை ஓரிடத்தில் சேர்த்து வைத்துத்
தம்முடைய சுற்றத்தார் அனைவரையும் அழைத்து, 'இந் நெற்குவை
முழுதும் திருக்கோயில் வழிபாட்டுக் குரியது; எக்காரணம்
கொண்டும் இதனை நீங்கள் எடுத்தலாகாது”” என்று கூறிப்
போர்முனைக்குச் சென்று விட்டார். சென்றவர் திரும்பச் சில
காலமாயின. இதற்கிடையில் கொடிய பஞ்சம் ஏற்பட்டது.
கோட்புலியின் உறவினரும் பிறரும் உணவின்றி வருந்தினர்.
கோயிற் பூசைக்காகக் கோட்புலியால் சேமித்து வைக்கப்பட்ட
நெல்லையாவது எடுத்துண்டு பசிப்பிணியைப் போக்கலாம்
என்றெண்ணிய கோட்புலியின் உறவினர் அவ்வாறு செய்தனர்.

## English Translation

Tamil title: ஏன் அழமாட்டார்?

A few days ago, a Saivite discourse was reportedly held in Lalgudi near Tiruchirappalli. While speaking on the *Periyapuranam*, a "true Saivite devotee" repeatedly wept and shed tears. When he selected and explained certain incidents from the *Periyapuranam*, his eyes apparently poured forth tears without his knowing it. He sobbed, stood stunned for a moment, resumed his speech, and then began weeping again. A newspaper report says that his discourse proceeded in this manner.

There is indeed no doubt that, while reading the *Periyapuranam*, many of its incidents would startle a reader's heart and make one cry, "Ah! Was it like this?" and shed tears. We cite only a few examples here.

Kaliyar was a devotee of Siva who lived at Tiruvotriyur near Madras, and was one of the sixty-three Nayanars. His service was to light lamps in Siva's temple. Everything he possessed was spent on lighting those lamps, until he had no money left for more.

An idea immediately occurred to him. He summoned his wife from inside the house and took her to the marketplace. "She is my wife. I am going to sell her. Is there anyone who will buy her?" he announced, naming a price. No one bought her. Did Kaliyar stop there? He took her from street to street and named a price, but still no one would buy her.

When one reads in a purana that a Tamil took his wedded wife to the marketplace and offered her for sale, who could refrain from weeping at the thought that a man could have behaved with such a loss of honour?

Kaliyar did not stop with offering his wife for sale in the market. Because she could not be sold, he still had no money to buy oil and light the lamp. Another idea immediately occurred to him: he decided to light the lamp with the blood in his own body instead of oil, and reportedly cut his throat.

When describing how a Tamil man could not even understand the difference between blood and oil, mistook blood for oil, imagined it could light a lamp, and cut his own throat, the devotee must have wept at the thought that the purana claimed so "great a sage" had existed among the Tamil people.

Next comes Kotpuli, a devotee of Siva from the Vellalar community who served as a military commander under a Chola king. He spent his entire salary buying paddy for worship in Siva temples.

By order of the king, Kotpuli had to leave for a battlefront. He gathered in one place the heap of paddy required for the temple, summoned all his relatives and told them, "This entire heap belongs to worship in the holy temple. Under no circumstances may you take any of it." He then departed for war.

Some time passed before he returned. Meanwhile, a terrible famine arose. Kotpuli's relatives and others suffered without food. Thinking they could at least eat the paddy he had stored for temple worship and cure the disease of hunger, his relatives did so.

Kotpuli returned from the battlefront to the beat of victory drums. He learnt of the "improper" act committed by his household and relatives. "How dare they eat paddy stored for Siva!" he raged. He decided that those wicked people who consumed Siva's property must be killed and eliminated.

He killed the mother and father who had given birth to him, the children he had fathered, his wife, his relatives, and everyone else who had shared that paddy. At last, only a nursing infant remained alive. Kotpuli moved to kill it too.

A man standing nearby pleaded, "This infant did not eat Siva's property. Moreover, it is the only surviving child of the family. Do not kill it."

Kotpuli replied, "Even if the child did not eat Siva's property, it drank the breast milk of a woman who did." He picked up the infant, threw it high like a ball, held his sword beneath it as it fell, and cut it into two pieces.

When recounting this incident from the *Periyapuranam*, the devotee who spoke at Lalgudi must have wept and said:

"Devotees! Behold the rare deed performed to protect Saivism by the murderous and merciless Kotpuli! O prosperous followers of Siva's path, ponder deeply the beauty of the sublime service rendered by the devotees of Siva, that supreme being proclaimed the lord of boundless bliss!"

Famine had arisen. People weakened by cruel hunger had no other way to relieve it, so they took the paddy and cured their hunger. This alone caused Kotpuli to murder them. Those who ate the paddy called Siva's property were themselves devotees of Siva; they did not belong to Buddhism, Jainism or Vaishnavism, faiths outside Saivism.

Even so, is it intelligent for great Saivites who support Tirumular's doctrine, which valued the walking temple above the temple of images, to endorse Sekkizhar's claim that Kotpuli's relatives, themselves walking temples, committed a crime by consuming property belonging to Siva, the temple of images?

The forms of gods made of stone, bronze, wood and other materials in temples do not consume, and cannot consume, any kind of food. Every variety of food offered to them ultimately falls into the stomachs of walking beings like ourselves. Knowing this, Sekkizhar nevertheless composed a purana that made a Tamil hero of a cruel sinner who massacred Kotpuli's relatives because unbearable hunger drove them to eat paddy called Siva's property, and who tossed a nursing infant like a ball and killed it as it screamed.

The devotee must have wept, thinking, "Alas for Sekkizhar's small-mindedness, and alas that even today Saivite scholars exist who believe this invented puranic tale to be true and support it!"

Next, Kazharsingar, a king of the Pallava line, was also one of the sixty-three Nayanars. One day he went with his crowned queen to worship the Supreme One at the Tiruvarur temple. On the temple street, the queen picked up a flower lying on the ground and smelt it.

Seruthunai, who saw this and was himself one of the sixty-three Nayanars, ran over and cut off the queen's nose. She screamed and fell. Her husband, King Kazharsingar, then arrived and learnt what had happened. He said it was insufficient merely to cut off the nose that had smelt a flower belonging to Siva; the hand that picked it up must also be severed. He therefore cut off his wife's hand.

Who could read this incident without weeping? If news said that a person had stood before a large audience and explained it without his eyes filling or shedding tears, that alone would be astonishing. Otherwise, it is entirely natural to say that one recounting it wept with moist eyes, an anguished heart and a shuddering body.

An unknown man seized and dragged another man's wife by her hair and mindlessly cut off her nose because she had picked up and smelt a flower lying fallen on the temple street. Not satisfied that only her nose had been cut, her husband severed her hand.

Anyone recounting this would surely think:

"A woman picked up and smelt a flower that lay below going to waste, and a man cut off her nose for it. He boldly cut the nose of the wife of a man he did not even know. Could such a person possess honour, morality, compassion or intelligence?

"And if a passer-by cut off a man's wife's nose because she picked up and smelt a fallen flower, and her husband, saying that was not enough, also severed her hand and made her prey for the earth, what are we to think? A king cut off a woman's hand for the offence of picking up a flower lying underfoot. When recounting this incident, can anyone refrain from weeping as they reflect, 'How very excellent was this king's magnificent system of government, and how very excellent was the tender mercy of Siva, who rejoiced in this deed and granted salvation to both men!'"

The *Periyapuranam* contains countless other incidents over which one may think again and again, shed tears, wail and cry: the story of the son who killed his father; the fool who cut his daughter's hair in the wedding pavilion; the brute who rubbed his own hand upon a stone as though it were sandalwood; the villain who dashed his head against a stone; the man who shamelessly sold his wife's marriage pendant; the madman who cut out another person's tongue; the witless man who mistook a stone for fragrant jasmine; the "great sage" who sent his wedded wife away behind a man who desired her; the blind fool who gouged out his eye and pressed it upon a stone; and innumerable others.

One might think and yearn and weep over the existence of all these stories in a purana deliberately degrading the Tamil people, composed by a Tamil himself.

The moment we learnt that the person who repeatedly wept and shed tears while delivering the *Periyapuranam* discourse at Lalgudi was our friend K. M. Balasubramaniam, B.A., B.L., tears came even to us: "Ah! Has even his intelligence become caught in this puranic whirlpool and been made to weep?"

13 April 1947.

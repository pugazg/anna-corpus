## English Translation

The *Periyapuranam* contains an episode sung by Sekkizhar in which the Jains, seeing Sambandar arrive to cure the Pandyan king's burning fever, grew angry and barked out things drawn from their books. Sambandar is said to have looked at them very calmly and told them to speak of matters in a proper order. The Jains then allegedly raged like the submarine fire and babbled whatever came into their mouths. Perceiving Queen Mangayarkkarasiyar's fear at their conduct, Sambandar reportedly turned to her and said, “I shall not fear these low men.”

I have often heard certain “true Saivite devotees” say: “Did not the tender-hearted Sekkizhar compose the *Periyapuranam* in sweet, classical Tamil? Some people find fault with this purana, which is written in beautiful Tamil, supported by immeasurable intelligence in describing devotees' love, and made an abode of purity. What are we to call their ignorance?”

At such times I too once thought that those who called a faultless thing faulty lacked judgement. But ordinary human reason urged me to ask: when people identify a thing commonly called faultless as containing fault and criticise it, is it proper to leap upon them without considering why they did so or what fault they found?

Reason exists generally in everyone born human, yet most do not consent to follow it. Many among us regard it as wrong to analyse something, distinguish its truth and falsehood, gain clarity and stand by that clarity.

In matters concerning God, religion and puranas, people are compelled to regard using reason to identify merits and defects, and then acting according to their understanding, as a “great sin.”

The ancestors who created these institutions declared that doubting God, religion or puranas and trying to remove those doubts were improper. They wrote such warnings in advance because they feared their ignorance would be exposed and their livelihood destroyed.

Their doctrine could therefore survive until people developed the habit of using reason. There was once an age when people did not even know they possessed reason and could not learn it because they lived as uncivilised barbarians surrounded by ignorance.

People no longer live in that condition. This is an age in which it has been decided that people possess the right to examine everything carefully, whether God, religion, purana or anything else, and follow it only after determining whether it benefits themselves and others.

Yet some still insist that people must remain forever in that darkness and ignorance, because otherwise their livelihood cannot proceed smoothly. They fence off reason, protect it from use in God's name and lock it away by displaying the puranas.

Nevertheless, at least a few people today possess the quality of using reason, smashing these barriers to dust with the key called rights. That is why we can examine such puranic episodes and explain their truth and falsehood to the public.

Anyone who looks impartially, without hatred or favour, at the character of the Jain people described by Sekkizhar will never accept his account as true.

Sekkizhar freely flings abusive expressions: the Jains “barked,” “babbled,” “raged like the northern fire” and “grew furious” when they saw Sambandar. But do these words fit the Jains? Did they conduct themselves so? Was that their nature?

Examination shows that Sekkizhar deliberately used these expressions to make others hate the Jains.

As evidence, I present only a few of the many good teachings laid down by Jain elders concerning proper human conduct. After reading them, consider whether Sekkizhar's claim can be true:

“Knowing that even a fly may climb and sit upon one's head,
it is good for the discerning not to possess burning anger.”

Its meaning is that good people who understand and reflect upon even a fly climbing and sitting on a person's head should remain free of anger that flares and falls upon others.

Who stated this ethical rule? Was it Saivites of Sekkizhar's tradition? No. The Jain elders whom Sekkizhar accused of “barking” gave this rule of conduct to the world.

Sekkizhar unashamedly said that the Jain elders who gave us such excellent ethical advancement and guided us toward a good path “barked.” Did he not even understand whether barking is a human quality or a dog's?

Sekkizhar is no longer here. But his admirers remain. I ask at least them to recognise that dogs bark, not human beings, and that abusing Jain elders who rendered indispensable service in the field of knowledge with such degrading words was wrong, a great and unforgivable crime.

Even if we suppose for argument that Jains themselves used such low language, should Sekkizhar have repeated it with his own “exalted” tongue? Ethical teaching says that when low people speak degrading words, the noble do not repeat them:

“When base people utter base words,
will the noble repeat them with their own mouths?”

That is, if low people use words unworthy of speech, would noble people repeat those same low words?

Sekkizhar composed the *Periyapuranam* with the aim of portraying Saivism as an exalted religion and Saivites as noble people, while treating Jainism and Jains as low. He considered himself and his religion superior and worked to make others do the same.

Yet in the *Tondar Puranam*, intended to explain the devotion of the sacred company, Sekkizhar uses against the Jains he considered inferior words such as “barked,” “babbled” and “raged in fury,” degrading even the richness of Tamil.

Anyone who reads the *Periyapuranam* impartially will then clearly understand who is truly low and whose religion is inferior.

Perhaps Sekkizhar, through ignorance or in order to turn King Anapaya Chola from attachment to Jainism toward Saivism, attributed nonexistent faults to Jains and spoke slightingly of them and their religion. The *Periyapuranam* is said to have been composed precisely to make Anapaya Chola a Saivite.

Even granting this, did Sambandar, who is described as wearing peace itself as his ornament, refrain from speaking degradingly of Jains, even if he did not praise them? No.

In abusing Jains, Sambandar went a step beyond Sekkizhar. He inserted into his *Tevaram*, without omitting one, every insulting word available: deceivers, thugs, club-men, fools, low men and outcastes.

Even an uneducated, uncivilised and undisciplined person might fear to use such burning words in an anger too great to bear. Yet Sambandar, said to possess everything from learning to divine grace and to have neither equal nor superior, used them freely and without restraint.

I ask readers to consider whether any consistency can exist between what the puranas say of such people and their conduct.

Anyone who doubts our claim that everything Sekkizhar and Sambandar say about Jains is false rather than true should place side by side two Saivite works, the *Periyapuranam* and *Tevaram*, and two Jain works, the *Civaka Cintamani* and *Naladiyar*.

Read separately and understand the events and structures described in them, then compare them impartially. It will become easy to see who truly deserves the words barkers, babblers, deceivers, club-men, fools, low men and outcastes.

Jain elders gave people, without expecting recompense, the virtue, discipline, love and knowledge indispensable to them. Sambandar and Sekkizhar may in their own age have gained influence by placing nonexistent charges upon those Jains and degrading them and their religion. But today's rational world will never, for any reason, accept such unfitting lies.

8 December 1946

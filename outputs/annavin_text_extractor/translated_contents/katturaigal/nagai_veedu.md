## English Translation

Kattur is a small village close to Tiruvarur. It could have been named Sirur, “little village,” but for some reason unknown to me, our elders called it Kattur.

You see me seated in the home of our friend Ramaiah, while younger brother Karunanidhi, his face in bloom, looks at me and speaks. You may say it is a neatly composed photograph and remark that we are seated upon a spread mattress. But this is written to tell you the meaning of my smile and of the light visible in my brother's eyes, so that you may know and rejoice.

The conference succeeded in a way that delighted everyone who saw it. For two days it functioned splendidly as a fortress of heroes, a forum of learning, a hall narrating history and an upper chamber of joyful conversation. We are meeting after it.

“Anna!” my brother called.

His eyes seemed to ask, “Did you see the craftsmanship? Did you find any flaw?” Am I one who is easily caught in a net? I summoned a slightly stern voice and asked, “Why? What?” But that wretched smile emerged with enough power to cut through my design. The photograph shows it; look and you will understand.

“Did we not give more money than you asked?” was my brother's question.

“Is this all that can be given?” was the arrow loosed by my smile.

“You are happy, are you not?” asks my brother.

“Should you not seek and provide still more?” I ask.

Do not infer from my question that their achievement brought me no great joy. The desire that more should be obtained, and confidence that they are capable of finding and providing it, make me ask in that fashion.

Whenever any of us sees another, our eyes perform a dance of delight and our faces bloom because joy surges within. The country once asked: their ability is so small and the task they have undertaken so great, how can they succeed? We entered the work at that very time, and have now emerged as a new formation.

The rare ideal that took possession of us gave us strength, encouragement, feeling, the capacity to work and the power to conquer hostility. It has made us new human beings.

When digging a well in sand, one must labour painfully beneath the cruelty of the burning sun. Afterwards cool water is found, sweet as tender coconut water. In the same way, before the eyes of those who asked what we could accomplish, we now see many pleasant things and have attained many distinctions.

Because we have been able to see this, confidence arises that we can see still greater things. A determination to see them is born. I begin examining what capacity each person possesses and in what form. When I undertake that examination, some think I look weary, complain or become angry. Very few fail to understand that all of it is the result of spotless affection. Because my brother understands, you see the light of delight in his eyes and discern meaning in my smile.

The day when such scenes come to exist in every home will be the new day of Pongal. I said “come to exist,” not “already exist,” because that day has not yet arrived. The reason it has not arrived is that the victories we have won are insufficient; many more remain to be gained.

The thought “Is it possible?” must not arise. Do not imagine I say this merely to create confidence. There are not many others to do this work. What can be done? We must do it ourselves, and as we do it, the necessary strength must grow.

When a tiger springs at us, will we stop to think that we have no spear? Our arm itself is a spear. If a mountainous wave rises and tears away the sail, what will the helmsman do? Will he leap after the departing sail, or continue steering the vessel without it?

Likewise, is it good to sit under a banyan tree wondering whether we possess enough ability? No, no! Let us begin working with what we have, trusting that all we lack will come and join us. That is the distinctive character of our work. It has already given us victories that delight observers. We know it, and the country knows it.

*Murasoli* comes to your home in colour. Do you know its former form? In those days it circulated among the students of Tiruvarur as a small handbill, so fragile that it seemed it would crumble when one finished reading and its letters scatter when unfolded.

Today it offers stories and poetry with flavour, illustrations of many kinds, sheen and polished beauty. It appears in a form that astonishes even those who once thought this field belonged exclusively to them, and enters every home as a welcome guest.

Friends may say, “It is a journal, Anna, merely a journal. Turning it into a honeycomb is easy. But is the task of making the country ours, making our country good, and enabling that goodness to guide the world just as easy?” I am not unaware of the difficulty of the work. Yet I see many signs that effort can succeed, and I want you to see them too.

If we draw out all the talent accumulated and lying dormant in the heart of the precious younger brother Tiruvarur gave us, we can witness many more victories.

“Why such an enormous crowd, Anna?” my brother asked me. I asked in return, “The crowds have not diminished; they keep growing. But what will the position be when the time comes to obtain votes?” The answer lies in my brother's smile. Discover it!

That is a question not only for the brother from Tiruvarur but for everyone to answer.

The form of our organisation's next stage will depend upon the quality of that answer.

The ideas brought to you by *Murasoli*, which will come into your hands and keep you company on the new day of Pongal, can give your mind new happiness and capacity for action.

Today our movement contains many good people skilled at explanation, adding flavour to speech, clarifying policy and answering rebuttals. The benefit the country receives from their rare ability is great. Their service has secured a favourable place for the work we have undertaken today.

We touched the nerve and a note has sounded. Courage has arisen to call, “Let us compose the melody; come!” In a way that demonstrates our capacity to create that melody, *Murasoli* must become sweet music in every home, unite all good people and show the path by which our objective can triumph.

Do not think I refer only to the journal called *Murasoli*. I refer also to the younger brother who made it beautiful and asks me with his eyes, “Did you see the craftsmanship?”

That is what I answer with my gaze. My brother understands and rejoices.

It is the same whenever any of us sees another. The strength arising from that joyful state gives the country new splendour; happiness overflows.

It would be wrong to keep you waiting when you are eager to drink a mixture of sugarcane juice and the love offered by your lightning-waisted beloved. Could I forget when I was in your position? Go, brother, go! Enjoy the happiness of the new Pongal day. May today's enthusiasm give you the capacity to perform the good work that will create a condition in which the whole country can be described as eternally festive.

*Murasoli Pongal Annual*, 14 January 1959

## English Translation

If someone said, “Sankaracharya carried the palanquin of Prativadi Bhayankaram Annangaracharya, and the head of Tiruvavaduthurai Mutt carried the palanquin of the head of Tiruppanandal Mutt,” would anyone believe it? Could it be believed? Could such a thing happen?

Who said that one monastic head lifted and carried another's palanquin? How did he dare say it? Where is the wretch who uttered that burning statement? Has humanity left him alive? Have the times deteriorated so far? What audacity, hardness, arrogance and conceit! Is that not what everyone would say? We agree. Who could deny it?

If one said that the two holy men travelled together in one palanquin rather than separately, and ordinary bearers carried it, that might have happened. No one need call it wrong. But if anyone said one holy man carried another's palanquin, the world would not spare him in the slightest. No one washes milk with milk in such an irrational fashion. Likewise one monastic head will not carry another's palanquin, nor be granted the right to do so. It would diminish both sides. For two men of equal status, both “he carried” and “he made him carry” produce degradation and disgrace. Such an event in religion would be called especially cruel.

Yet such an event did occur in religion.

“When?”

“Approximately 1,300 years ago.”

“Where?”

“At Tiruppoonthuruthi.”

“Who did it?”

“How can it be said?”

“Then is this an incident you invented?”

“No. It is in a purana.”

“Which purana?”

“The *Periyapuranam*.”

“In whose history?”

“Sambandar's.”

“Does Sambandar's history say one religious teacher carried another's palanquin?”

“Yes. It says Tirunavukkarasar carried Sambandar's palanquin.”

“Who was Sambandar?”

“A teacher of Saivism.”

“And Tirunavukkarasar?”

“He too was a teacher of Saivism.”

“If both were Saivite teachers, why did one carry the other's palanquin?”

“That is what is unknown.”

Should what is unknown simply be left alone? The proper course is to clarify what is unknown, unintelligible and obscure. Here is the explanation.

Saivism has four teachers: Sambandar, Appar, Sundarar and Manikkavasagar. Of these, Sambandar, Sundarar and Manikkavasagar were Brahmins. Tirunavukkarasar, called Appar, was a Tamil. Sambandar's age was sixteen, Sundarar's eighteen, Manikkavasagar's thirty-two and Appar's eighty-one. All four served as teachers and developed Saivism. The first three did as much as they could within their shorter lives; Appar lived eighty-one years and fostered the faith.

The *Periyapuranam* says that Appar, the eighty-one-year-old teacher, carried the palanquin of the sixteen-year-old Sambandar. Why did one teacher carry another? It cannot be argued that the bearer was vastly inferior as a teacher and therefore properly carried the superior. No one would value the teaching career of sixteen-year-old Sambandar above that of Appar, who had sixty-five more years. Even the ages of Sambandar, Sundarar and Manikkavasagar together fall fifteen years short of Appar's.

Under these circumstances, if anyone were to carry another, the younger Sambandar, Sundarar and Manikkavasagar should have carried the palanquin of Appar, mature in age, knowledge and experience. Yet “that Father” gave Appar no palanquin. Even if he had, we would not accept the younger men carrying it as proper. Among four people of equal standing, one should not be elevated and the others lowered. Anyone who wishes to abolish the cruel servile practice of humans carrying humans will never welcome one person sitting in a palanquin while several others bear it.

But in Saivism, said to embrace every life equally with love, the impartial Lord who shares his form with the bamboo-shouldered Goddess established this “excellent” practice of one person sitting while many carry him. The purana says Sambandar's palanquin was sent by the Supreme himself, strongly underlining the point.

If a palanquin were one honour granted to teachers who instructed the people in religious principles, we might welcome it. But was the same distinction given to every teacher? No. Nor were equivalent honours distributed as palanquin, horse or elephant. Had the purana merely said Sambandar obtained a palanquin through Siva's grace, there would be no need for this inquiry. We could read it and finish there. But Sekkizhar did not stop there.

The great three-eyed God could not bear seeing Sambandar's father carry him upon his shoulders, so immediately gave Sambandar a palanquin and commanded him to use it. How could Sekkizhar be content with that? He did not compose a purana merely to stop there. Its purpose was different, and Sekkizhar knew that purpose would not be achieved at that point. So he completed it “properly.” How?

Sambandar came to Tiruppoonthuruthi wishing to see Tirunavukkarasar. Learning this, Appar set out to meet him, entered the crowd unnoticed, and:

> Joined those who bore the rhythmically jewelled palanquin,  
> and came bearing it, his mind rejoicing.

That is, Tirunavukkarasar entered the crowd and joyfully became one among those carrying the pearl palanquin.

The ordinary and proper course was for Appar to receive Sambandar, who had come to see him, and for the two to exchange greetings. Why instead does the purana say Appar carried Sambandar's palanquin? When the Lord granted the palanquin, did he arrange that anyone wishing to see Sambandar must first carry it? No such evidence appears explicitly.

Nor can it be argued that Appar wished to see Sambandar and first carried the palanquin as a mark of respect. The purana says Sambandar came to see Appar. Was there then a custom that, to meet a visitor in a palanquin, one first carried it; to meet one on horseback, one dismounted him, seated him upon one's back and carried him; or to meet one in a cart, one unharnessed the bulls and pulled the cart oneself? No evidence exists for such customs. Yet Sekkizhar says Appar carried Sambandar's palanquin without explaining why.

Kalyanasundaranar, in his research commentary on the *Periyapuranam*, writes:

> By carrying the palanquin, Appar revealed his love.

Does this explanation fit? How can carrying another's palanquin serve as a measure of love? Any intelligent person will agree that it reveals a servile mentality, not affection. Love is shown by hurrying forward to embrace someone warmly; by lifting, kissing and smelling the crown of a small child's head; or, where physical touch is inappropriate, by welcoming a woman with affectionate words. To claim that carrying a person's palanquin displays love only exposes the ignorance of those fallen into the abyss of servility and unable to understand love's nature.

Sambandar, who came to see Appar, was young. According to the purana, he may then have been about ten, while Appar was about seventy. To say a seventy-year-old elder demonstrated affection for a ten-year-old by carrying the palanquin in which the child rode is contrary to nature and forcibly contrived.

In other words, the episode was recorded and confirmed so that the assertion that Tirunavukkarasar carried Sambandar's palanquin would become established later. Otherwise, would anyone write that a mature religious teacher carried the palanquin of another teacher younger in both age and knowledge? I ask you to consider it.

Although Tirunavukkarasar was a religious teacher like Sambandar, Siva granted him none of Sambandar's facilities or distinctions. From Sambandar's birth until death, the almighty Siva bestowed every honour upon him: milk of wisdom, golden cymbals, pearl palanquin, pearl umbrella, gold, and finally liberation. To Appar, an equivalent teacher, he gave only a weeding tool and made him clear grass around temples. He also made him carry Sambandar's palanquin and, in the end, did not even grant him the “liberation” said to have been given to Sambandar under the religion's doctrine.

This has been written so that people may consider whether such a partial God, and a religion taking that God as its head, can truly belong to the Tamil people.

**6 April 1947**

## Translator's Notes

`Appar` and `Tirunavukkarasar` name the same Saivite saint. Sambandar, Appar, Sundarar and Manikkavasagar are conventionally counted among the four principal Saivite teachers. Sekkizhar authored the *Periyapuranam*. `Uzhavaram` is the hoe or weeding implement associated with Appar's temple service.

The source's age statements are internally inconsistent: one OCR passage appears to assign `78` to Sundarar and later totals and comparisons do not align perfectly, while subsequent paragraphs describe Sambandar as about ten and Appar about seventy at the meeting. The translation follows the clearly intended opening list as sixteen, eighteen, thirty-two and eighty-one, while preserving later age estimates as stated. These figures should be checked against the scan or cited edition before historical use.

The argument frames Brahmin/Tamil identity, servitude and divine partiality in the source's polemical terms. Its interpretation of the hagiographic episode is translated faithfully and not presented as a neutral consensus account of Saivite tradition.

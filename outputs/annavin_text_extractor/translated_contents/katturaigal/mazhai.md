## English Translation

## Praise

Make the fields flourish! Make life flourish! New life and new beauty for parched places!

Rain gives fresh radiance to the earth, which had seemed to say: “What can I do? I nourished you as much as I could. What remains with me to give you now? I have dried up and you are collapsing. At the sight of your condition, my heart lies cracked into great slabs. There is no use trusting me—I lie barren!”

Now the withered plants and vines can be watered. Everything that had fallen will stand upright. All that had grown weak will dance in delight. Everything about to perish sends out shoots. Greenery, abundance and beauty everywhere! All that had languished has gained a new state trembling with loveliness; rain has brought everything that was fading toward death into overflowing joy.

Where had so much beauty hidden until today? Who could see this spectacle and not rejoice? Yet only yesterday all this lay exhausted.

Is rain a great medicine? A feast bestowed by the sky? Rain, the munificent giver of life!

Call the painter and say: “Here is your opportunity to display all your skill! Colours of every kind, forms without number. You may have held many ideas about beauty; here you will see beauty's varieties and forms.”

See the green that awakens desire. Amid it, see the red like the face of a spear-eyed maiden standing bashfully—a tiny flower smiling.

See the beauty of the grove where birds once tormented for want of shelter now circle everywhere and raise many songs. Know that it lay a desert when it saw no rain and gained its grace through rain.

Abundance overflows under the name of a canal. Look at the little birds drinking in its excellence, like one touching honey poured into a bowl, placing it in the mouth and delighting.

What more do you need for your thought to take form? Blue above, with a white cloud swimming there; green below; within it many colours and forms—all flowers!

Hear them sing in joy. Not birds alone but plants and trees possess, in their own measure, the ability to express culture. Their sound is their manner of singing praise to the great rain that gave them life.

Calves come bounding as the water bodies proclaim that mother's nectar is available here too.

Are the leaping *kendai* fish saying, “Eat and travel on; we shall roll in the lap and drink sweetness”? We do not hear the stork say, “Is not the waterfall my mother, who raises you for my sake?” Yet it takes its food and flies away calling, “My mother! My mother!”

Those who lift irrigation water no longer suffer pain in the waist. There is no room for the lament, “What shall we do? There is no crop.” The great rain fell and sorrow changed.

Like a victorious king advancing as drums of triumph sound and banners of honour dance, the great rain set forth with thunder and poured prosperity. Transformation blossomed.

If God asked, “What is your desire? Tell me what you need,” one might say: “I need a house, marriage, flowers, happiness, gold, wealth, work and beauty. I want the joy of seeing a peacock-plumed woman draw our child close, place it in my lap, and lead the calf into the shed. I want to feed visitors and gain the happiness of living. I want opportunities for many colourful arts to grow and be enjoyed. I want unfading beauty, youth that does not become a dry leaf, an unwounded mind and efforts that never burn away. I want to see everyone lead a noble life.

“I want to behold and delight in the dancing peacock, the singing black cuckoo, the parrots that ask, ‘You have only music—do you possess my beauty?’, coconut and palmyra, honey-dripping fruits, and the prosperity and beauty everywhere, and to sing of their excellence.

“I want to see victorious a way of life in which no trace remains of the evils that produce turmoil—famine, fever, deceit and theft—and everyone can say, ‘We live; they live,’ and enjoy inward peace.”

After saying many things, one need not drag out the speech: “I forgot some more; now hear these too.” A single answer contains the thousand wishes arising in the heart: what we need is rain. What else? See the great rain that grants whatever one may desire. That alone is enough. No one will deny it.

A government, war drums that defend it, troops who beat them and the prosperous towns in which they live all require many things. Yet nothing can be obtained without the aid of one thing: the great rain, nature's treasury.

People who have not seen rain are like infants who have not seen their mother's face.

There is a lake, but no prosperity. There is a town, but no food. There is a government in name, but no benefit. There is a text, but no reader. There is music, but no listener.

Where does such a condition exist? In a place where the great rain, the motive force of everything, fails. Can writing show it? Can even the painter's eye make it visible?

Likewise, the many magnificent pictures nature has bestowed require great rain to receive living breath and preserve earthly life. Without it the field, life and mind dry; therefore every virtue dries.

Great rain is thus our mother. Is that all? It protects from disappearance the virtues elders once assembled in councils and bestowed.

Knowing this, Ilango placed the auspicious invocation in his beautiful Tamil epic: “Let us praise the great rain! Let us praise the great rain!”

You will say, “Yes, true—there is no denying it.” Yet, friends, does everyone gain only good when that same rain falls? Listen to the curses.

## Abuse

Ruinous rain that destroys one's trade! Violent rain that blocks the road and causes distress! Rain that spoils colours and forms and brings trouble!

When I hurried out to sell my goods, you rushed in and ruined my work, rain! You soaked my clothes and damaged house and cattle. The stream burst because of you. Is that the only harm you caused?

I hurried to hear a beautiful young woman skilled in the seven notes. Thinking you would stop with the wind and not grow heavy, I continued walking. But you swelled, laughed with a rattling roar, emitted eye-stealing light and poured without restraint. You imprisoned me again in my house when I had set out for the hall of art. Remembering it turns my heart into a furnace. What do you care? You spit without pause.

We may welcome a guest who comes and goes. It is not good manners to say, “I have come; however much you suffer, I shall not leave quickly,” and remain.

People say your speed and volume are falling and signs of clearing have appeared. Mocking them, you pour down in triumph. Those with homes remain inside, escape your assault, eat happily and converse. What can people who possess human form only to labour for a living do by staying home? How will they eat? What you pour is not rice and curry!

The landlord has summoned a man and is easily enraged. There is much work, the road is long, and travel is impossible because of you. Did you not bring this harm?

Someone applied plaster and slept thinking it would dry by tomorrow. What harm had he done that you destroyed it? Was that not enough? Everything built dissolved and fell. “Did I dig this pond? Is this the fate of my house?” Bewildered, I had to shelter in a public hall. Why this cruelty, demon rain?

The Iluppur market meets today. I gave what I had for these goods, hoping to sell them for a little income. You made travel impossible. My goods are here; where are the buyers? Will you tell me to go tomorrow? Let us agree I shall live—but will my goods remain unspoiled? If they spoil, who will want them? Will not the price fall? You turned my hope into dust, churned the earth and confused my mind. Do those who call rain good have no hearts?

“Signs of rain are appearing, Madhava! Do not sell the stock. Let it remain in the warehouse. If great rain falls for many days, new stock will stop arriving. Those in need will buy at our price. If they ask for a basketful, say there is not even a winnowing-trayful. Let four days pass and our gain will increase. Fortunately the rain came and our trade prospered!”

Did you come to bless such a merchant—pushing the have-not into distress and making the wicked rejoice? Is this a conspiracy? Is “rain” its name?

An old man, already struggling to sustain his body, went his way without knowing you had turned a canal into a deep pit. He walked, fell and floated away. A boy wandered, searching for Grandfather, and those he met told him. What could he do? He poured tears. Grandfather floated away because of the “tears” you poured. What can the child's tears do to you? Yet they burn hotter than fire.

The government has a thousand duties. It sends people around the world to study pageantry at great expense. Like a rich man seeking oil to hide grey hair, it needs money for acts that conceal defects: drums and celebration, a sky-high flagstaff, a shining flag and troops arrayed to salute it. Festivals proclaim, “We shall build a fort; behold the spectacle.” After all that, little money remains. Still, to silence opponents, it built a few things here and there. You smashed even those and erased every trace. Is government not enough to make us suffer? Do we need you too?

Did you hear labourers and the poor crying that they were dying in huts, decide their loud cry would remain as long as they lived and disturb the government, and therefore come as a flood to stop their speech by stopping their breath? Are you another instrument of destruction sent by government? Is calling you “rain” not an error?

Weavers displayed and sold the beauty they wove, received payment and lived to labour again. Your downpour destroyed their work. With folded hands they begged: “Hear our grievance. We have starved for two days. Compassionate people, give what you can.” What did you do? You roared, “Behold my power! I shall swell every river, submerge every town and pour down fear.” That the strong must not torment the weak is morality's alphabet. Is rain too ignorant to know it?

They built eight storeys for others, yet had neither houses nor streets. Beside a muddy river they built little huts and survived as humans in name, only to labour. The river raged as though saying: “You have seen hunger, evil and rulers who threaten prison, yet you endure. Behold this advancing destruction. Can you withstand it?” The flood reduced every hut to refuse and swept it away, becoming fire in poor hearts—and only its name is rain!

People say restraint is required, knowing measure is wisdom and acting according to place is a virtue. You rejected all these, entered everywhere and unleashed ruin. Is that good?

Though poets knew you as a shameless wicked mind committing cruelties, they praised you as milk and honey from the sky. Ah, I understand! Knowing no way to stop you destroying the town, they thought flattery might cool your head and halt your work. Following the method of taming the insolent, those elders praised you in sugarcane-sweet Tamil.

## Explanation

“They called me in distress, and so I came. I left a pure place far away, with beautiful realms where I might have remained. I descended, did not shrink from hard labour, and gave so abundance might flow and lands flourish, defeating all benefactors in generosity. A few incidents of destruction occurred—so what?

“Why forget that all this abundance came through me and attack me? Fire destroys; water prevents destruction, apart from seven or eight cases in a thousand. Must I explain what I can do when the cruel wind joins me?

“Though sweetness is my nature, is blaming honey because a bitter *etti* fruit fell into it clear thinking? I pour for many days; destruction occurs on a few. Many things are born and some perish. Does some labour go to waste? Is birth evil? Is labour useless?

“Is every thought in your mind good? Do no evil thoughts arise? If they do, will anyone say the mind should not exist? Everything and every act combines good and evil. Evil must be removed, but do you not possess the wisdom that removes thorns to take the flower and peels skin to eat fruit? Treat me likewise.

“A thousand means exist to prevent destruction when I rush with force. Who established them? Huts were submerged—I admit the cruelty. But what did you do to leave open the flood's path, provide homes in suitable places and bring happiness to the poor? Where is that government?

“You say I create opportunities for hoarders. Even granting it, why seek me out for attack when the cruel stand before you? Restraining them is government's duty. Why did it do nothing? Did I prevent it?

“You lament, ‘There is no rain!’ When I arrive, you rejoice, ‘Rain!’ A merchant deceives you with ‘Because of rain!’ You lack strength to challenge him, ‘What of the rain?’ You do not know whose side government is on. Many troubles arise from that. Why hurl harsh words at me? Can I stop pouring upon hearing them?

“A pregnant cloud trembles, unable to bear its burden. Only when I emerge does it find relief. I come and pour. Sometimes you praise me; sometimes revile me. I cannot become honey when praised or hide again in the cloud when abused.

“I descend, but is there a proper path for me? No. Are there places to stay? None. I seek the broad path stretching far—you call it a river. Had you maintained it, neither I nor the town would suffer. Earth mounds obstruct it.

“You could check my speed, store me and benefit, but do not. A lake's bank gives no protection. A pond cannot endure my force and bursts. What am I to do?

“People once built lakes and ponds within and around a town for me. Now lakes are filled and made settlements; riverbanks become masses of huts. The sea alone still gives refuge.

“One by one you seized every place that belonged to me. I have nowhere to remain orderly, so I move wherever there is room and you see destruction. Yet I am blamed.

“Am I without feeling? I dismiss that as deficient thinking. I roam happily remembering the songs in which renowned poets praised me. I consider those who revile me without recognising their own failings fools, though I remain humanity's friend. My place in the verse of poets you honour is enough.

“Valluvar spoke of ‘The Excellence of Rain.’ After invoking God and saying A is the first of letters, he understood that rain was needed for the world. Knowing even worshippers prosper only if the sky does not fail, he gave me a noble place.

“Yet even he asked people to worship God's feet and not me, who pours prosperity. Is that a defect? No—can the *Kural* contain a defect? God grants happiness only to those who reach his feet, making it an exchange. With me, no praise or prostration is required; every prosperity may be gained without giving me anything. That is why Valluvar prescribed no worship for me:

“‘What recompense can the world render
to the rain-cloud that expects none?’

“Thus spoke the great one.

“If stained minds abuse one so exalted as I, they reveal their low condition by reviling even rain. How will you know whether this is what I think whenever you hear thunder's laughter?

“Forgiving your many faults, we shall pour again. May you live! We have bestowed manifold abundance.”

*Dravida Nadu*, Pongal Annual, 1967

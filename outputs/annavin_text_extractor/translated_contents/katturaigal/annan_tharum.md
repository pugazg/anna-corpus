## English Translation

This year's Pongal New Year must be observed while the country is involved in a difficult and troubled situation.

Rather than say "celebrated," it would be more fitting to say "observed."

A festival should bring joy.

Today, the heat of the fire of war kindled by a foreigner remains. We are therefore not in a position to celebrate the festival in happiness.

Even so, from that day until today, Pongal has been a golden festival that brings us heartfelt joy.

Therefore, on this day too we set out to observe the sacred festival. Though our customary enthusiasm is diminished, an indefinable happiness nevertheless arises today.

Unlike other festivals, this one did not arise from thoughts about the impermanence of life—"Do not trust this illusory life; you will reach the feet of God." It teaches a good path: regard life as a great responsibility and a pure duty; experience its pleasure while also finding benefit in it; discover a way to share with others the benefit one has found; and conduct oneself accordingly.

That is why, at this festival, we find the heart ready to make room for fitting ideas.

We offer greetings, receive greetings, and rejoice.

We salute everyone who has made it possible for the country to flourish in prosperity.

We think and declare that a suitable form of government is needed for the country's welfare.

A form of government is not a fixed structure that no one may think of changing. It is an arrangement founded upon ideas. When ideas change, the form of government changes—and must change—to suit the new condition. Understanding this, we continue our work.

Our distinct dignity is worthy of acceptance. We wish to take it forth and offer it to the world. We voice our rights so that a government providing the opportunity for this may be formed and those who rule may understand.

There are a thousand and eight obstacles to this sacred work. Yet did not the farmer find cultivated land only after clearing the wilderness?

Not every cloud he saw poured down as rain. Disappointments and irritations came one after another. Though many said the field would dry up, by proceeding patiently and with an understanding of responsibility, he saw the grain bow heavy and found victory in labour.

Today we celebrate his victory, sing his praise, and offer our affection. Nor is that all. We learn that countless obstacles will arise and intervene in any work one intends to complete. Methods must be shaped to suit circumstances while ensuring that the original purpose is not broken. One must understand and employ every aid—time and instruments—and obtain victory.

Whatever the field, this is the lesson: the treasury of wisdom given by the man who protects the cultivated land. On this day, let us gain confidence that, carrying it with us, we too shall labour and attain victory.

We shall destroy the enemy that has risen to disrupt borders, sovereignty, thought, and the arrangement called democracy, and to introduce frenzied domination here. It is a pest upon the crop called culture.

We shall drive away the enemy, obtain victory free from blame, establish wholesome peace, and undertake the work of protecting our government.

After that condition arrives, we shall labour well to discover what methods are needed to achieve justice and prosperity.

Driving a crooked furrow is ruinous; damaging the bund is harmful—not only to farming, but also to the cultivated field of governing a town.

Let the four quarters praise us, saying: "These are good people and good farmers. They sow worthy ideas, channel their labour like water, remove harm, and cultivate virtue so that the harvest may bestow sacred abundance." Let us work now in a manner worthy of such praise.

Long live democracy! May government by principle grow!  
May the enemy that has arrived be defeated! May the wealth of rights increase!

Long live, younger brother! May you live well forever.

Long live all in your household! May virtue flourish here!  
May good government come here! Long live the glory of our Tamil!

Your elder brother,  
Annadurai

(*Murasoli* Pongal Annual, date printed with a damaged month as 14-)-1968)

## Translator's Notes

The message sustains an agricultural metaphor linking Pongal, patient cultivation, democratic government, and political work. One syntactically damaged line about borders, sovereignty, thought, and democracy is translated conservatively from its list structure. The month in the final date is not silently supplied.

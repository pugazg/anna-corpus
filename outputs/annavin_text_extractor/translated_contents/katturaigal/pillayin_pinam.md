## English Translation

The child's corpse lies in the street!
The mother sits beside it, begging!

Do not close your eyes!
This dreadful sight is not in some other land.

> "Mountains rich in eaglewood and teak that never perish,
> seas where lovely pearls lie heaped,
>
> clouds and red paddy resounding over fertile fields,
> a land fragrant with jasmine forests."

You speak of that land with such pride. Yet this is what we now see in its streets.

Skin and bone: this is the fate of a tender three-year-old who ought to have leapt and played and laughed, delighting his mother and father. This is the fate of one of this country's future citizens. Mothers will anguish at the sight; elders will tremble. As for us, our whole body turns to fire, every breath boils, and the heart itself sheds tears.

A life born to live lies upon the earth, reduced to skin and bone.

There sits the mother who once gathered him into her arms, kissed him and fondled him, crooning, "My darling! My treasure!" Now, having lost her precious child, she is the very image of grief, all tears and misery. Look at that mother weeping!

She is none other than a descendant of those whom we praise in heroic verse as "the mothers who gave birth to valour in the land of classical Tamil." She belongs to the lineage of Tamil women whom we proclaim to have sung joyfully of the moon and sea with their beloveds, as smiles made flesh.

Hair that has seen no oil, tattered clothing, a body covered in dust: alas, it is unbearable! She is a woman of golden Dravida, a sacred daughter of a wealthy country. Her country is vast; it has people and is not without wealth. Mansions abound and temples stand everywhere. There are "great men" who wander about speaking of the magnificence of their towers. Yet she has nothing. All she can call her own is a cracked pot, a broken earthen vessel and a rag.

> "In the breeze that floats through the sky,
> upon jewelled mansions and halls..."

So, we are told, lived our women: ancient glory says so. This mother knows no breeze. Her mansion and hall are merely that patch of bare earth. And she too is a woman of Tamil Nadu!

The Tamil land in which she was born has not been shattered by storm or earthquake and turned into a desert of white sand. It has not become a wasteland without paddy or sugarcane. Yet her "property" is an earthen pot and a cracked pan, while the cherished son of her heart is "skin and bone."

This ghastly spectacle that wounds the eye was seen two months ago on a Salem street, in front of Victoria Market. Nor was it confined to Salem: such things happen every day throughout the land.

A three-year-old child lay dead. A twelve-year-old boy watched tears stream from his mother's eyes and saw his little brother lying dead, all bone and skin. His own stomach burned with hunger, and he did not know whom to tell. He crouched there, nearly naked. The mother's heart boiled at what she had borne, yet she lacked even the strength to cry "Alas!" She sat in the street shedding tears.

She was not someone who had always begged in order to live. Once she had been in a position to give alms to many wanderers. Yet she herself was forced to beg. Crying, "Mother! Lady! A little gruel for a half-filled stomach," she wandered through the streets.

She had lived as the lamp of her household. She was Pappammal, thirty-five years old, the beloved wife of Rengasami Mudaliar of Tharamangalam near Salem. She and her husband had lived like body and life joined together. For many years the two lives had made one body and conducted their household. Two children were the echoes of their happy life.

A storm struck the life of Rengasami Mudaliar, who had supported his family through his daily labour. He was not wealthy; his household moved forward only when he worked each day. His labour and strength were his sole "capital," and suddenly a mighty thunderbolt struck him.

There was no work. He was a weaver. When he received yarn, he made it into cloth, converted the cloth into money, and kept his family going. There was no yarn, and therefore no work and no income.

He searched, wandered and struggled. Hunger, not for one or two days, but for many.

The children's cries and his wife's sorrow tortured him.

Unable to endure the sight of his family, broken-hearted, he went away somewhere. What became of him is not known.

Rendered helpless by the loss of a husband who disappeared because he could not bear their misery, Pappammal went to her elder brother's house. He too was a weaver. There too was a shortage of yarn, no work, and hunger.

Unable to endure the cruelty, the mother became a beggar on the streets of Salem.

This new occupation, the fierce sun and the wandering made both children ill.

What recourse has a poor person? She walked to the government hospital. She was destitute; those who came and went there recoiled merely on seeing her. They drove her out of the hospital.

The very image of suffering, she did not cry out a price for her child. Even had she named a price, who would buy a child reduced by hunger and illness to a skeleton? And so she stood at house after house pleading, "Gruel, mother, gruel!" Tamil Nadu is evidently in such a condition that gruel may be had in abundance! Yet from every home comes only the cry, "Rice, rice!" Hunger therefore grew, while the pangs of hunger remained unappeased.

The end came: the child became a corpse. The three-year-old treasure fondly named Palani was dead. A future citizen of independent India had died. A new life of the Tamil people had been extinguished.

This event, capable of crushing the heart to powder, is taking place under self-rule.

The ordeals through which weavers' families now suffer are savage.

Because there is no yarn, they are subjected to the disgrace of becoming beggars without any means of survival.

If one hears the reports arriving from Salem, Kanchi, Sankarankoil and many other places, even stone would melt and the hardest heart would cry aloud.

The Congress government has made no effort to end this disgrace.

Nor has this government made proper arrangements to provide the necessary yarn to people who ask, "I am willing to work; give me the means."

As a result, many more "Pappammals" appear every day.

In a land where gold grows, deprived even of food to eat, they watch their children die of hunger and, writhing in anguish, become destitute wanderers.

Without attempting to end this cruelty, the ministers go from town to town on pleasure tours, wielding batons and firing guns!

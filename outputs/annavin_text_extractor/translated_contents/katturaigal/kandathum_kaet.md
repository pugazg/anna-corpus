## English Translation

Somewhere, in some town, the Chief Minister apparently opened his august mouth and compared the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam to a beggar! He is said to have declared that people gather around us just as they gather around a beggar who sings and dances, and that they leave as soon as they have finished listening to our speech. Even a beggar can gather a crowd; these grandees who rule the land cannot manage even that. But let that pass.

Should MLA Govindasami, who read this news in the paper, have told C. N. A. about it only then? It was the final day of debate on the Budget in the Legislative Assembly. This time our MLAs had taken the government's case apart, point by point. Even in the reporters' gallery where I sat, people were discussing it.

“There was no monsoon,” the Governor had said in his address. “The monsoon was normal,” the Finance Minister had said in his Budget statement. Karunanidhi raised the question whether this contradiction was proper. P. U. Shanmugam asked how the government's statistics could contradict one another. In this manner, each of them had assailed the government's conduct from several directions.

At the end of the debate, the DMK's leader in the Assembly was to speak. The Finance Minister, Subramaniam, would then reply to everything.

Because it was the final day of debate, I went there with a reporter's pass. People said our leader had been allotted about an hour. Perhaps because they wanted more members to speak, he was given only forty-five minutes. How could that be enough, I wondered, for C. N. A. had many points on which he intended to speak.

He explained our position on the Budget and the various ways in which the country's sources of revenue ought to be developed. The government should not think only of passing time by imposing taxes; it too should earn revenue, he said. The government could take over and operate bus services, run cinema theatres and establish meat farms, among other enterprises.

He had spoken about meat farms even the previous year. The ministers who immediately set off on a triumphal tour proclaimed: “Ask Annadurai for a plan and he tells us to open a butcher's shop! Is that a plan?” Yet this very government has established fish farms and has spent money to develop them. Because we had proposed it, wherever they went these ministers asked, “Is that a plan?” Even the Finance Minister, attending a public meeting in Kanchipuram, mocked it: “Annadurai is asking me to open a butcher's shop!”

Referring to this, C. N. A. said: “After going through everyone, would I look at you and ask you to open a butcher's shop? There might be some meaning if I said it after looking at the Chief Minister!”

Laughter rose in the Assembly. In physical build, the Chief Minister was a better fit than the Finance Minister.

“If one or two goats are slaughtered, it is a butcher's shop. If one or two thousand are slaughtered, it is a meat farm. Many countries accumulate wealth by running such meat farms,” C. N. A. explained.

By contrasting the Finance Minister's slender figure with that of the Chief Minister, he made the point in a humorous tone. What is called satire in English appeared in many places: humour and the same wholesome sting remained throughout the speech.

Had the Chief Minister not called us beggars? C. N. A. referred to it repeatedly in the course of his speech. At one point he said, “The Chief Minister is said to have called us beggars. He is no lord himself.” The whole Assembly laughed.

At another point, with a double meaning, he said, “The Chief Minister knows very well how to take something this way and give it that way.” Kamaraj too knew this art, did he not? Those present were Congress MLAs, and so they too joined in the laughter.

Who are the beggars? In truth, it is this government that repeatedly stretches its hand towards Delhi. C. N. A. explained this as well and offered proposals for creating conditions in which we could manage our own economy instead of standing and begging in this fashion.

“Denmark earns a good profit from meat farms. Denmark is a much smaller country than ours. It can do so. Why cannot we?” he asked. He continued: “Can it be said that the people here are less capable than the ministers there?”

Kamaraj, seated opposite him, immediately said, “No!” The way that “No” sounded was as though he were asking: “We do not possess the ability that Denmark has. Is that enough?” He must have meant it as mockery. Yet his voice also sounded as though the Chief Minister were saying: “We simply do not have their competence! What can you do about it?”

Truly, it stung me. It made me think: is it you, so indifferent, who holds the reins of democracy here?

That was not the only occasion. Before completing his speech, C. N. A. said: “All the opposition parties in the country united in asking that the state be named Tamil Nadu. For that cause, a great man in the Chief Minister's own town undertook a fast. Whenever the Chief Minister returned from that direction, he would ask, ‘Has he not died yet? Has he not died yet?’”

C. N. A. had not finished. He meant to add, “That was what the Chief Minister asked; he did not ask the man to end his fast.” But Kamaraj could not contain his impatience. He burst out: “He is dead!”

What he said sounded like the delivery of bereavement news to C. N. A., as though the latter did not know the man had died. It also sounded as if he were displaying the indifference of one who asks: “What if that noble elder lost his life? Is that such a great matter?”

Why does the Chief Minister reveal himself in this manner, I wondered. Is it irritation, or does he want to demonstrate the force of his voice to us? Whatever it may be, his statement is something democracy cannot tolerate.

Though the Chief Minister made one or two such remarks from time to time, he remained and listened until C. N. A. finished speaking. Afterwards, a member of his own party also spoke, but the Chief Minister was not in the House. He was absent even while the Finance Minister replied.

I had attended the previous year too when the Finance Minister answered the debate. Good heavens! He brought forth and piled up the history of our organisation, the views we had expressed at the time and our books, and attacked us relentlessly.

“Do you know who the DMK are? They were once members of the Justice Party! They were in the Dravidar Kazhagam, and before that they were in the Self-Respect movement! They fraternised with the British!” He spoke in this fashion.

I had wondered: we have undergone so many changes and have today become the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. What kind of reply is this? Does he perhaps strongly believe that we remain exactly what we were then? Does he refuse to recognise our changes? Or does he think those changes should never have occurred?

The Finance Minister who spoke so harshly that day did more than refrain from “scorching” us this year: he repeatedly mixed pliancy and flexibility into his reply. He twisted and turned like an accused man arguing from the dock. Sentence after sentence, he continually offered garlands of affection. I was greatly surprised.

The Chief Minister boiled because he could not bear the “heat” of the opposition parties. On feeling the heat, the Finance Minister became melting wax.

He admitted that mistakes do occur in statistics. “What we say is not scripture! People say even that scripture proves false. Therefore, I do not come here to say that everything the government does will be right. There will certainly be defects. I do not deny it,” he said.

The Finance Minister acknowledged that defects would exist. Was it not surprising that he made such an admission? When the opposition parties point out mistakes made by Congress ministers, the ministers never accept them. He also said that statistics could change with changing times. Not only statistics, but what he himself had once said might change as the days passed; new thoughts arose every day, he explained.

Thus a song of conciliation and considerable humility could be discerned in his speech. In one way, this gave me pleasure.

Subramaniam answered our charge that abandoning handloom and wasting money on the khadi scheme was futile. At that point he said plainly: “Even I have no faith that khadi must continue to exist.”

Congress members insist that khadi must necessarily exist, do they not? That could change. Handloom could fill its place. He said he did not believe khadi had to remain permanently. Here too his reason was that ideas might change with the passage of time.

In this fashion, “our voice” appeared at many points in the Finance Minister's reply.

At one place he spoke about “the North”: “Bengal is not a northern state. The people of the northern states speak of Bengal together with Tamil Nadu,” he said.

When referring to Subramaniam's preparation of the Budget over eight consecutive years as Finance Minister, C. N. A. said: “Instead of preparing an ordinary Budget like this, may he receive the opportunity to present a greater Budget.” His meaning was: you have been Finance Minister here long enough; may you become a minister in Delhi.

Subramaniam immediately replied, “If you do not like me, do not ask me to go north for that reason.”

## English Translation

A storm to teachers who wished to dominate; a gentle breeze to students. His conversation, even upon first acquaintance, planted in one's mind the thought that it would be impossible ever to forget him.

His gaze seemed to say: "I am a symbol of revolution. They have brought me, who ought to be somewhere else, and cast me into this wilderness. I remain impatient, wondering when I will escape this cage."

He was dark-skinned and wore untidy but Western-style clothes.

His whole heart was filled with concern for the advancement of non-Brahmins. His laughter was like thunder. When he began to condemn something, it was pungent. When he entered into fun, he became like a little child.

For twenty years he remained such a sight and companion to countless young people. The news that teacher Varadarajan had died must have come as a shock to everyone fortunate enough to know him.

My eyes had been accustomed to teachers carrying canes. The sight of teacher Varadarajan walking one day along the upstairs veranda of the old Pachaiyappa's College, his hand patting me on the back, was something new.

When I entered college, it was an age in which one could not even imagine students receiving a teacher's "comradeship."

At the time, Varadarajan was both a teacher and a student: a teacher at Pachaiyappa's College and a student at Madras Law College.

That was not his only dual role. He was also leader of Madras city's non-Brahmin youth and head of Sir A. Ramaswami Mudaliar's election department.

Nor was that all. To the types who shouted from street corners, threw stones at election meetings, or struck from darkness and ran away, he was a warning of danger.

Bicycles would set out by the hundred, two hundred, or five hundred. Neatly dressed students rode them while raising the slogan, "Vote for Justice." Who were they? All Varadarajan's men? No, his friends!

While attending to all these affairs, he effortlessly governed even those college classes that some professors could not control.

When his class was in session, every student's face blossomed. It was not because they witnessed his rare literary learning or heard a subtle explanation of the lesson, but because they listened to his explanation of affairs in the wider world.

Bearing so many responsibilities while still young bent the course of his life. By the time he recognized it, the bend could no longer be straightened.

In those days, many said that Varadarajan would enter the Corporation, go to the Legislative Assembly, and perhaps even become a minister. But until the end he remained confined to that college.

So far as his own welfare was concerned, his life became like an article ruined through disuse. The non-Brahmin movement, however, benefited greatly from him.

Even while sitting in class, he would cast his net, identifying those who could be useful to the movement. Within ten or twenty days, the student would have joined Varadarajan's group.

There was a street filled with an undesirable atmosphere. Upon it stood a horse stable. Upstairs, amid scattered books, some eaten by insects; a bed that looked fit to be picked up and thrown into the rubbish; piles of newspapers; and leftover food swarming with flies, sat that dark hero surrounded by students. I was among those in the front rank.

The plans formed there were grand indeed. Tea came at every turn, and cigarette smoke increased the room's stench. Many important political affairs concerning the city were decided in that tiny room. Later, some of them even moved through the Corporation and Legislative Assembly.

Varadarajan was one of the two mentors in my public life. The other was Thiruvengadasami. Alas, neither is with us today. Both died before attaining the positions they ought to have reached.

During his final years, teacher Varadarajan acquired a measure of disillusionment. The reason was the selfishness of some eminent leaders of the non-Brahmin movement.

Ordinary Ramaswami used to say that he could not feel happy unless he saw "friend Varadarajan" at least once a day. After becoming Sir Ramaswami, however, he would say, "There is no time today; ask him to come tomorrow." If this reached Varadarajan's ears, would it not cause pain and disillusionment?

At certain times, he freed himself from the grip of that disillusionment and participated in some of our movement's meetings in Madras. But those of us who had known him in the earlier days understood very well that this was not the old Varadarajan.

Until the end, he remained like a man exhausted by endlessly searching for something he had lost.

His private life was exceedingly devoid of pleasure.

"What time is it now, sir? It is nearly three, sir! That fool still hasn't come, sir! What am I to do without food, sir?" he would cry.

When the meal finally arrived, he would stare at the food on the plates and let out a tremendous shout, frightening his younger brother who had brought it.

"Would a dog eat this rice, sir? Would a dog eat it? They only snatch the money, sir! Hey! I will kill you first and only then eat!"

Shouting all the while, he would eat like Dr Johnson. He would urge others to eat, call out for tea, and drink that too. Sweat would pour from him. There would be no upper cloth with which to wipe it, and any cloth available would be covered with soot stains.

Such was his joyless private life.

A few years ago, he tried to make his surroundings slightly more comfortable and moved into a small garden house adjoining the college.

"Sir! Let me accumulate ten or twenty thousand rupees. Then I shall throw away this job, and you will see what I do," he would exclaim.

The anger produced by that thought would later fade away like smoke.

We are pleased to learn that, as death approached, he said the small sum he possessed should be spent upon education.

An excellent friend, a shattered life. Through his noble efforts, many people like me entered public life. His service was of immense use to the non-Brahmin movement, but his own life was blighted.

At the age of forty-eight, teacher Varadarajan died on the fourteenth of this month. A hero who laboured without receiving the fruits and gave life to many has departed. The crop he cultivated flourishes.

Many people will never know the rare service he performed in those days. Friends like me, grieving at his departure, could speak for months about his many activities.

But his family! Their pain can be reduced only by many of us sharing it with them.

*Dravida Nadu*, 28 September 1947.

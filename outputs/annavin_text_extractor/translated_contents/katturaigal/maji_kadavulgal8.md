Hadding again had to fight a wolf. He killed it and ate its entrails, whereupon he gained strength no one could restrain. Realizing resistance was futile, the heavenly beings sought peace. Ull, who occupied Odin’s throne, sent an envoy to Thor. A settlement followed and Odin became God once more.

On another occasion too Odin had to abandon his “divine employment” and flee. Hother set out to defeat the gods and seize heaven through his prowess. Odin, Thor, Balder, and many other gods fought together without success. Odin again lost office and became a wanderer; Balder died as well.

In grief Odin consulted a soothsayer: God himself went to ask for an oracle. The seer said a son would be born to avenge his humiliation, and that he must marry Rinda, the fortunate woman capable of bearing him. It resembles the disembodied voice declaring, “We shall be born in Gokulam to kill Kamsa.”

Rinda was the daughter of the king of the Ruthenians. Desiring her, Odin assumed human form, entered the king’s service, and performed many pleasing deeds. Choosing a happy moment, he requested her hand. The king welcomed a great warrior as son-in-law but required his daughter’s consent.

Rinda rejected him: “He may be a hero, but he is old and I am young. Is he to be my husband?” Odin needed her not for love but because only a son born through her could restore his honour. He tried every variety of courtship without success. Once, when he attempted to kiss her, she struck him hard across the cheek.

However much humiliation came, the Lord had to endure it. After repeated attempts, he caused a demon to possess her, disguised himself as the magician who could expel it, bewitched her mind, and married her. Rinda bore a son named Boi. He defeated the arrogant enemy who had driven out his father and restored Odin to the divine throne.

Such is Odin’s mythology: stories with neither coherence nor meaning nor a particle of genuine divinity. Yet Teutons treated their reverent recitation as religious duty. Their Kambans composed epics, kings built temples, holy men appeared, and floods of devotion spread across many regions.

It took ages for courage and clarity to ask: “How obscene and full of conspiracy and deceit are these stories! Fools, do you seek to glorify God through them? Are you true theists? Is this religion?” Once clarity arose, people did ask. The ignorant harmed them, but they did not fear to establish truth. Considering their lives less important than freeing people from those who ruined the world, they worked and prevailed. Odin fled the temple and became a former god.

Priests who lied, “I saw a cosmic crow,” and fools who babbled, “Wolves came with tongues hanging in my dream,” did not easily attain clarity. Opposing religious frenzy was dangerous, yet thoughtful people resisted, triumphed, and expelled these fantasies.

A god with a quarrelling family; a god with enemies who defeated and drove him away: vast numbers once believed these things. Today even playful children there mock them. Here, however, whoever refuses belief in Siva killing Daksha is considered sinful. Ordinary people treat tales of gods fighting, turmoil in heaven, and one deity defeating another as sacred, and thereby ruin the course of reason.

Our people do not know that Odin, who once lived in such splendour, became a former god. Those who know are afraid to say so.

Thor was Odin’s son. Odin had many divine sons, but Thor was foremost and most important, possessing power beyond his father’s. He commanded greater devotion and affection among earthly people. This mighty god had a chariot drawn by two goats in which he travelled throughout the cosmos.

## The God Thor

Our mythologists name Vishnu’s discus Sudarsana and his conch Panchajanya. Likewise, Teutonic mythologists named gods, vehicles, and implements. People worshipped the latter just as they did divine names.

Theism had not reached the view that one primal God capable of creation, destruction, protection, and punishment was sufficient. People sought many gods, praised each differently, and invented relationships and divisions of power among them. Mythological lies grew into a mountain, all of which Teutons believed a theist was obliged to accept.

Before philosophies arose describing God as beyond mind, speech, and body, unknowable to those who seek and unspoken by those who know, God was not one but many and possessed many forms. The gods did not merely govern together; they feuded and fought. Although these stories opposed reason, priests threatened that believers attained salvation while unbelievers would be struck by lightning. Fear of which god might become angry at what moment tormented ordinary people. Thinking safety depended on pleasing all, they spent much of their time doing so, assigning each deity its own festivals and seasons of worship.

They did not reason that Odin, incomparable primal cause of the cosmos, was enough. They sighed: Odin’s grace and worship were necessary, but... Then mythologists created another god before their eyes. Thor, the divine son, appeared to receive worship and offerings, displaying the fruit of superstition.

Thor, son of Odin, was the strongest and most influential. Teutonic mythologists made him lord of thunder, hero of the hammer, and omnipotent. His physique proclaimed authority, and he bore a hammer capable of crushing opposition. It was named Mjolnir. As Murugan has his spear, Siva his trident, and Vishnu his discus, Thor had a sacredly named hammer. Early Teutons worshipped Mjolnir as people here worship spear, trident, discus, and conch.

Thor rode throughout the universe in a chariot drawn by two goats. When mythologists praised Odin’s horse as travelling at the speed of wind and thought, devotees celebrated it as divine; with equal devotion they honoured Thor’s goats. Garuda and the peacock are likewise honoured here. They celebrated there; they celebrate here. It is past tense there and present tense in this country.

Even now people here say that in an age of righteousness cow and tiger drink at one ford, and if the tiger shows hostility Vishnu’s Sudarsana flies forth to sever its head. In the same way, unenlightened Teutons once said Thor’s hammer immediately set out to crush any injustice anywhere.

Mythologists invented many tales of its power, poets composed hymns, devotees sang in ecstasy, and priests grew fat. Ask their descendants today about Thor’s sacred sports and they clap with laughter; unlike our people, they do not embrace a Purana of Divine Sports.

Today people say God is the power that created sun, moon, stars, sea, and earth, possessing strength and glory unlike anything else, and call this clarified theism. Earlier there was no such clarity. Gods suffered hardship, danger, enmity, affection, anxiety, defeat, and grief like others. Sometimes they were expelled from heaven and later fought to recover their kingdom.

Mythologists did not worry that readers might ask what distinguished such a god from a forest king or whether these tales disgraced divinity. Their confidence came from being able to incite ordinary people against anyone who investigated, doubted, mocked, or condemned their stories, label him atheist, and destroy him. They destroyed many. Only through the few who survived torture and murder did the world eventually see truth’s light.

Thor’s influence once made rulers tremble. Fabulous stories displayed his power. Common people heard them and became devotees, seeing in their mind’s eye heaven, Thor enthroned there, his goats and hammer. They judged him stronger than Odin, sought his grace, and performed many rites.

The mythologist proclaimed, “Devotees, hear the valiant acts of Thor, Odin’s peerless jewel!” Teutons listened open-mouthed as though the speaker had personally accompanied Thor. Even today our ordinary people hear stories of lion-faced and buffalo demons and the Lord setting out to destroy them with reverence. Not only commoners but parliamentary experts shamelessly spread such bare falsehoods. The commoner literally believes Mahishasura had a buffalo’s face; the parliamentarian supplies a philosophical meaning, saying it denotes a face revealing strength and cruelty. That alone is the difference.

Whatever philosophy is supplied, spreading irrational stories that cultivate no excellence and injure the idea of God does harm, not enlightenment. Yet these learned men do not consider it. They celebrate Gokulashtami, Ramanavami, Sivaratri, Deepavali, Karthigai, Avittam, and other festivals built upon such tales and push ordinary people deeper into ruin.

Teutons who held festivals based on Thor’s exploits gained clarity, found truth’s light, and rose in the world because their thinkers laboured toward it. They declared it foolish to imagine God with wife and children, war, hostility, love, and amusement. Attacks by fanatics did not silence them. They endured distress, abuse, and indescribable cruelty, yet forgot their own lives and vowed to dispel popular delusion. They succeeded, and Thor became a former god.

Driving out a king is perilous: he has armies, forts, killers, wealth, and crowds who regard serving him as sacred. Opposing all these, overthrowing him, and establishing democracy is called revolution. Many have died, much blood has flowed, and countless people have suffered torture, prison, and exile.

If deposing one king is so difficult, consider the dangers faced by armies of knowledge in making Odin and Thor, worshipped as gods generation after generation, into former gods. Then the courage and selflessness of rationalists become clear. Overthrowing a tyrant is rightly celebrated as revolution, but making gods former was greater still. In many respects, revolutions for the rule of reason were more heroic than those for democracy. Their result was Thor’s retirement from divinity.

While Thor was a god, tales were so abundant and marvellous that one might say even our mythologist was defeated. A band of Jotuns constantly troubled the gods, like the demons in our myths. Wars repeatedly broke out, and Thor subdued them through the glory of his hammer. Just as the Kanda Purana glorifies Murugan’s spear, a mythology of Jotun destruction arose to glorify Thor’s hammer.

Once the sea-god visited Odin’s palace for a feast. Odin, Thor, and the entire family entertained him warmly because he was exceedingly rich. He had a jewelled palace beneath the deep, where gold was burned as cooking fuel. This sea-god Aegir had a goddess named Ran and nine daughters. Sailors escaped danger only by offering piles of gold, so immense treasure accumulated with him. Even among gods, the rich received special respect.

Odin therefore gave Aegir a magnificent banquet with divine drink and music, like one Indian ruler entertaining another with song and dance. Aegir in turn invited Odin to his realm. Odin went with his family, and the entertainment was magnificent, for Aegir burned gold as firewood. Yet one defect appeared. Aegir wished to serve a sweet drink like Odin’s but lacked a cauldron large enough to brew sufficient quantity for all the gods.

One naturally asks what madness this is: a glorious god who commands the deep and burns gold in his stove has no vessel for payasam? Since the subject is not one of our gods, even strict observers dare mock it. Teutons then did not; they considered questions sinful. We ask whether they were truly so foolish. Yes, they celebrated that ignorance as theism. They no longer live in that state, but respectable people who mock their past do nothing about most ordinary people here still revering similarly irrational tales as sacred texts.

We naturally ridicule stories of a goat-riding Lord, a hammer-bearing Supreme Being, and a God searching for a pot. Yet when a hymn here describes the earring-wearing Lord riding a bull, crowned by the pure white moon and smeared with cremation-ground ash, anger erupts if anyone asks whether God should be portrayed so. Is that right? Think.

Seeing Aegir distressed for want of a cauldron, Thor promised to bring a great one. A god named Tyr had told him where the largest vessel in the nine worlds could be found. Thor departed with Tyr to fetch it.

Beyond the cosmos lived a dog-faced giant, Tyr’s foster father, who possessed the vessel. Thor harnessed his two goats, Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjostr, to the chariot. They travelled with the speed of wind and thought, rested at the home of a friend named Orvandil, and continued on foot to the giant Hymir.

At the palace a terrifying many-headed old giantess welcomed them because she was Tyr’s grandmother. She belonged to the demonic race while her grandson belonged to the divine race. Call it farce and religious advocates answer: “It is divine history; doubt is sin.” The grandmother was hideous, but her daughter was supremely beautiful and helped Tyr and, through him, Thor.

Hymir returned that evening with enormous fish. His furious glance at the visitors shattered many objects. The beautiful woman calmed him and humbly explained that Tyr and his friend were guests.

They sat to eat. Hymir roasted three bulls. Thor swiftly consumed two, astonishing and angering his host. Hymir had vowed to kill anyone who could not eat as much as he; expecting ordinary-looking Thor to fail, he had prepared three bulls. But Thor swallowed two, leaving no excuse to kill him. That was Hymir’s sorrow.

A priest of that age would have sung before devotees just as a Kali-temple priest now chants extravagant measurements and devotees assent. At Thor’s festival he might have sung:

The devotees would have entered ecstasy. Unenlightened people once celebrated these as divine sports. Here even today people delight for merit in the tale of Gundotharan eating a mountain of rice and drinking the whole Vaigai.

Startled by Thor’s feat, Hymir went fishing next day and Thor joined him. The giant warned: “Boy, do not come.

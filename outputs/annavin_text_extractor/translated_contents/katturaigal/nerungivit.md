## English Translation

“We will not let it go!”

“What if they file a case? Justice is on our side, so we shall win.”

“Never mind. We lost in the High Court, but we shall see in the Supreme Court.”

“We will not lose heart because the new Constitution provides no room for it.”

“The opposition parties think this is their opportunity to overthrow us. Are we alone unconcerned about the progress of non-Brahmin people?”

“Whose government is it? Ours, is it not? We shall go to the high command and ask them to amend the new Constitution itself. Will they refuse even this? If they do, are we trees? Our ministers will not hesitate even to leave office. They will resign.”

Comrades, you heard Congress leaders roar like this from public platforms. You saw Congress papers with non-Brahmin editors write with sparks flying.

The roars raised by Congress ministers in the legislature over communal representation did not vanish into the wind. Even today they remain in our hearts. What courage, what speed!

Madhava Menon spoke like a cyclonic storm. Was he alone? Our ministers sounded the conch over the Communal G.O., making us think of storms and volcanoes. Wherever Kamaraj went, he roared that Congress would not retreat on the issue. How loudly his ceremonial attendants clamoured! How fiercely his party newspapers, *Dinasari* and *Gandibam*, wrote! Their pens emitted sparks as though restoring life to severed nerves and joining broken bones; their speeches moved with volcanic force.

The cries for equal justice raised by South Indian Congress leaders and ministers gave strength to our bewildered hearts. Fire appeared in tear-filled eyes and a furnace in anxious hearts.

“The Congress legislative party will not abandon it,” they said. We believed the dead would rise and social justice survive.

Newspapers reported, “If our resolution is defeated, our ministry will not hesitate to resign.” New light arose in our darkened eyes.

The Madras Legislative Council passed a resolution demanding amendment of the Constitution so the Communal G.O. could continue. It did not stop there; the resolution was immediately sent to Delhi. Hope blossomed in our hearts.

“We are going to the high command about the Communal G.O. We shall meet its leader, Pandit Nehru,” said our provincial premier, Kumaraswami Raja, and departed for Delhi. Ministers Gopala Reddi, Sitarama Reddi and Bhaktavatsalam went with him. Enthusiasm rose in our thoughts. Not one but four were going, so we expected them to explain the matter in Delhi and return victorious. We waited eagerly for news.

Those who went to Delhi have returned. The four ministers set out to catch a tiger and came back without even a mouse, their minds seized by fear.

Returning minister Gopala Reddi announced without hesitation:

> The Constitution will not be amended to preserve the Communal G.O.

They returned without accomplishing the purpose for which they went.

Do not be stunned. Yes, comrades, do not ask, “Did they not sound the conch with sparks flying?” The minister delivered his answer calmly, almost as though saying, “I know anger rises like fire in your eyes, your hearts beat and your thoughts become volcanoes. What can be done?”

The reply may strike your mind like a hammer. But he is a minister, so he gave it leisurely, without the slightest agitation.

You will ask: “What horror! Where is their courage? Have their slogans become paralysed and their roars turned into wild noise? We trusted our eyes would receive new light, but he returns saying nothing can be done.”

You will rage: “What kind of country is this? Why have a ministry, government or administration here even in name?”

You will ask: “The Legislative Council passed a resolution. Did the central government not respect even that?”

Another minister, Sitarama Reddi, has answered you too. A reporter reportedly asked him: “The Madras Legislative Council resolved that all communities must receive equal opportunity in government recruitment and admission to professional colleges, and sent the resolution to the central government. What became of it?”

On seeing the question, Sitarama Reddi laughed. The Honourable Minister laughed! Do not imagine a breeze blew only within the mind of a man who returned empty-handed from Delhi. The reporter did not explain whether foolishness dripped from that laugh or sorrow appeared in it, but recorded the answer. Look at it. On seeing it, one feels compelled to ask whether it is an answer at all.

The Madras minister reportedly said, smiling:

> The Madras resolution remains in Madras.

What a painful reply! The Honourable Minister who carried a kavadi to Delhi and returned says the Madras resolution remains in Madras. The man who left declaring that the Constitution would somehow be amended says it. This is an occasion to cover one's face with black cloth like Muthanna. Yet the minister laughed, then delivered this “sacred news.”

As though one were insufficient, four went to Delhi. They now return saying the Communal G.O. will not be protected, without agitation or distress.

It is shameful. They went as envoys to remove an injury affecting the life of their province's people and failed. A legislature said to echo public thought sent a resolution, yet the deities of Delhi did not turn their sacred gaze towards it. They dismissed it and sent the delegation back with: “It will not help; it cannot be done; not now.”

Bhaktavatsalam brought another message: the Madras government will soon consider new rules or orders to replace the Communal G.O. Consider it! Were all the earlier deliberations, the Council resolution and the journey of four ministers to Delhi conducted without thought? Or did all their thinking become silk-cotton caught in Delhi's seasonal wind? What fresh consideration is needed?

They cannot preserve the Communal G.O., so now they say they will seek new rules and issue a new order. Are their tongues revolving tongues? A new thought each minute, a change each hour, an action each day. The pain becomes unbearable when we remember that Dravidam is trapped in the hands of such weak, irresolute men.

It seems the Madras premier has not stopped the correspondence he began earlier. Home Minister Rajaji gives us that information. What did they bring even after going in person? A groan of “cannot,” a dissatisfied murmur that amendment is forbidden, and the plaintive tune “we shall devise other rules.”

Their roar has become a cat's squeak. Their volcanic speech is the bubbling of a hot-water pot. Their storm-paced assurances have fallen into dust.

Of our ministers who spoke bravely and returned empty-handed, one laughs, another stares, a third seeks a new rule, and the premier reads letters and thinks.

We expected this spectacle, for Madras resolutions always remain in Madras and have never once received Delhi's attention. Four years have taught us that well.

Even so, we respected the concern and enthusiasm shown by southern Congressmen over the Communal G.O., a question of life and death for a vast community.

They too are citizens of Dravidian society, we thought. Let us wait and see, for they roar so bravely.

They hold responsibility for government and belong to the same party as the central rulers, so we thought they possessed both access and a path.

To this extent we placed faith in their actions, because our concern is that communal justice must somehow be protected.

We did not regard the issue as an opportunity to develop our organisation. At a time when the claim “Dravida Nadu for Dravidians!” echoes many times more loudly than before, we need not wait eagerly for one dispute or another. Moreover, our foundational principle had already taken root inside the Congress camp. We regarded whatever benefit their attempt produced as the fruit of our labour.

Let them at least accomplish this work themselves, we thought. Let the “enemies” learn that the war drum for non-Brahmin rights sounds within the nationalist tent itself. Let this become an opportunity for that.

When they themselves roared and undertook the required measures, it would have been improper for us to interfere. They could afterward easily cast the blame upon us, as is their habit. So, with an unstained mind, we resolved to give public support to their effort. But now, after trying every avenue available to them, they have failed.

They have returned with the lesson taught by northern imperialism.

Whether they feel pain or will continue behaving like frivolous children does not concern us. We gave them enough opportunity to protect Dravidian society from a darkened life.

Their slogans and efforts have been wasted. We hoped Delhi might consent at least to an attempt conducted through them. Instead the familiar spectacle has occurred again.

We have therefore been brought to a position in which we must act decisively.

The right to equal justice has been broken and thrown aside by the enemy's cunning manoeuvres. Recovering it will not be easy, but neither is it something we can abandon.

Many spoke about this at the Salem district DMK conference. The conference passed a resolution on the Communal G.O. and recommended action to the DMK executive committee.

Therefore, comrades, do not fear. We waited. Delhi drove them away; Madras trembles.

A great storm has arisen. Our demand for rights has been ignored. We gave the nationalist camp its opportunity, and Delhi sent even them back with blackened faces. The responsibility for labouring for our community's rights has now fallen upon us.

Dravidam must no longer waste time trusting empty speech.

The Dravidian lions cannot refrain from surging up and saying, “We have endured enough; let us march to the battlefield.” The people understood long ago that social justice must not fall, for if it falls, the community and its life fall with it. Until now they maintained hope. After seeing that hope destroyed, they will not stand with folded hands and covered mouths. We shall see flame in their eyes. No longer will only tears flow.

Sparks will scatter through every street. Hearts that groan will become hearts that shake the world.

Until now the people respected the rulers' elaborate talk. They can do so no longer. Those who waited, saying, “We shall remain disciplined and patient; the time will change,” now see that time has not changed but become grotesque. They cannot and must not remain turtles and mute men.

Though saying, “It is hard, hard, hard to remain idle,” they remained idle because these “men of freedom” were ministers and had declared that everything could therefore be achieved. But the handsome heroes have returned with soot upon their faces.

Now the war drum will sound. That day approaches, comes ever closer, and has already drawn near!

*Dravida Nadu*, 13 May 1951

## Translator's Notes

The `Communal G.O.` was the Madras Presidency system allocating educational and public-service opportunities among communities. Court decisions against it led to political demands for constitutional protection and formed part of the context for India's First Constitutional Amendment in 1951. `Communal` here means community-based representation, not communal violence.

The named Madras politicians are P. S. Kumaraswami Raja, K. Madhava Menon, K. Kamaraj, B. Gopala Reddi, K. Chandramouli/Sitarama Reddi as rendered in the source, M. Bhaktavatsalam and C. Rajagopalachari. The precise identity behind the OCR form `Sitarama Reddi` and the allusion to `Muthanna` require verification against the printed edition.

`Kavadi` is a burden ceremonially carried in devotion and is used here sarcastically for the delegation's journey. Political claims and quoted ministerial remarks are translated from the article's account and have not been independently checked against legislative or newspaper records.

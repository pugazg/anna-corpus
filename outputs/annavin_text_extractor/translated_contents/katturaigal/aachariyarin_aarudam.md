முடியாத வெறுப்பு உண்டாகியிருக்கிறதென்றும், அதனாலேயே
சமஸ்கிருதத்தை விரும்பிக் கற்றவர்கள் கூட இப்பொழுது அதனை
ஏறெடுத்தும் பார்ப்பதில்லை என்பதைக் கல்வி௮மைச்சர் உணர்வதோடு,
அதிகார பலத்தை நம்பிச் சமஸ்கிருதத்தைப் பரப்பும் பணியில் இறங்கினால்,
அது பயனற்ற காரியமாகவேமூடியும் என்பதையும் அறியவேண்டும்.

சமஸ்கிருதத்தைப் பின்னடையச் செய்வதற்கு யார் காரணம் என்று
தெரியவில்லை என்பதாகக் கல்வி அமைச்சர் கவலைப்படுகிறார். இதற்குத்
தனிப்பட்ட எவரும் காரணமல்ல - காலமாறுதலும் மக்களுக்குத் தங்கள்
சொந்த மொழிகளில் ஏற்பட்ட பற்றுதலுமே இதற்குக் காரணமாகும். இந்தக்
காரணத்தை அமைச்சர் பதவியால் ஏற்படும் அதிகார பலத்தால் மாற்றி விட
முடியாது. எனவேதான் மாதவமேனனாரின் மருத்துவமும் ஆச்சாரியாரின்
ஆருூடமும் பலிக்காது என்று கூறுகிறோம்.

(திராவிட. நாடு - 26.3.50)

## English Translation

Tamil title: ஆச்சாரியாரின் ஆரூடம் பலிக்காது

Our friend Achariyar predicts, "Sanskrit will enjoy great esteem in the future." At the same time, he appears afraid that his prediction may perhaps prove false. That must be why he urges everyone to learn Sanskrit. He seems to think that he can at least create esteem for it by making everyone study it.

He has also grown anxious about what will happen if the people do not believe his "marvellous" prophecy. He therefore immediately makes a sweeping claim:

"In former times, all the affairs of our country were conducted in Sanskrit."

Achariyar even gives details of the affairs conducted in Sanskrit in those days. He says dharmashastra, reform propaganda, government administration and trade were all conducted in Sanskrit. He does not stop with claiming that dharmashastra, government and trade functioned through Sanskrit; without any hesitation, he says that reform propaganda too was carried on in that language.

Reform! Achariyar does not explain the form in which Sanskrit carried it out. Nevertheless, we cite here just a few of the reforms performed by Sanskrit.

"Just as Agni is the supreme deity in both Vedic and worldly rites, so a Brahmin, whether wise or foolish, is himself the supreme deity."

One of Sanskrit's reforms was that a Brahmin must be regarded and worshipped as a god whether he was intelligent or an utter fool. No distinction should be made between a wise man and a fool. What an excellent reform! How could any language other than Sanskrit accomplish such a "distinguished" reform? What other language would even attempt the reform of requiring people to regard and worship a witless dullard as God because he is a Brahmin, a god upon earth?

"Even when a Brahmin commits wicked deeds, he is worthy of worship on every auspicious and inauspicious occasion, because he is the supreme deity."

This too is one of Sanskrit's reforms. Even when a Brahmin performs forbidden acts, he reportedly remains worthy of everyone else's worship. The reason is simply that he is the supreme god. How magnificent is the Sanskrit that carried out the reform of turning the Brahmin into a god!

Behold the glory of a reform that deifies one section of humanity and requires every other person to worship it. In what language other than Sanskrit could such a precious reform be found?

Can the mother tongue that says love is God, knowledge is God and truth is God equal the Sanskrit language that says the Brahmin is God? Tamil, which presents love and knowledge as divine, must not be called a language of reform. Sanskrit, which presented the Brahmin as a god, alone must be called the language of reform. After all, Achariyar says reform propaganda in those days was conducted only in Sanskrit.

In those times, Tirumular said in Tamil that love was God, and Tayumanavar said knowledge was God. But before them, Manu had already declared in Sanskrit that the Brahmin was God. Our friend Achariyar therefore appears to regard Manu's declaration as the true reform propaganda.

People of understanding say reform means rejecting an earlier interpretation and explaining that its meaning is not this but that. Achariyar refuses to follow that path. He says an interpretation given in ancient times should be treated as the reform of what someone said in a later age.

Thus, those who made love and knowledge divine are not to be regarded as reformers; the one who made the Brahmin divine is the reformer. Achariyar seeks to reform the very word "reform." Achariyar is, after all, a descendant of Manu. Would not his reform naturally follow Manu?

Even so, our request is that people like Achariyar understand that the sophistry of defining fire as cold will not prevail today, and that they should not suffer by mixing knowledge with ignorance according to the person and place before them.

Because Manu carried his "reform propaganda" to the point of making the Brahmin a god, most people today not only refuse to accept the Brahmin as divine but have awakened enough to say the country has no need of such a god.

Yet not even a person of the poorest intelligence refuses to accept love and knowledge as divine. The reason is that treating love and knowledge as God creates no trouble or distress for anyone. But treating the Brahmin as God inevitably creates enormous, unbearable hardships, sorrows and cruelties for every non-Brahmin.

We do not supply the reason. Manu, the reformer according to Achariyar's dictionary, supplies it himself. Here are a few examples:

1. A Brahmin may compel a Shudra to work whether or not the Shudra was purchased as a slave, because God created the Shudra for the sole purpose of serving the Brahmin.

2. A Brahmin may seize a Shudra's property with complete peace of mind and without the slightest thought of sin, because the Shudra owns nothing and his master may take his possessions.

3. If a once-born Shudra insults a twice-born Brahmin, his tongue must be cut out.

4. If a Shudra speaks contemptuously of the name or caste of Brahmins, a red-hot iron ten fingers long must be thrust into his mouth.

5. If a Shudra arrogantly instructs the teachers about their duties, it is the king's duty to have boiling oil poured into his mouth and ears.

6. If a Shudra raises his hand or a stick to strike a high-born person, his hand must be cut off; if he kicks in anger, his leg must be cut off.

7. If a low-born person intends to sit as an equal beside a high-born person, he must be branded upon the hip with a heated iron and expelled from the country, or his buttocks must be cut away.

8. If a Shudra arrogantly spits upon a Brahmin, the king must have both his lips cut off.

These are only a few of the "reforms" stated in Sanskrit. Such Sanskrit reforms cannot be carried out today because the law does not permit them. That is why Achariyar desires Sanskrit to gain great esteem in the future and makes his prediction. Perhaps he imagines that, if Sanskrit acquires esteem according to his prophecy, the Sanskrit reforms listed above may return to the country.

The honourable Achariyar dreams on, forgetting the proverb, "Churned butter will not return to buttermilk."

He also laments:

"A language that is not used in a country's affairs fades. Moreover, the use of Sanskrit words in the country has declined. That is why Sanskrit reached the point of extinction."

He tries to breathe new life into it and cause it to be reborn.

We wish to ask Achariyar, who says that a language fades when it is not used in national affairs, one question. If Tamil is not made the language of government in Tamil Nadu and a foreign language is installed instead, will Tamil not fade? If Hindi becomes the language of government, will Tamil not fade? And if Sanskrit too becomes a language everyone must learn, what will become of Tamil?

Even if Achariyar does not answer, should he not use his profound experience, give his intelligence a little work, and think about these questions?

Achariyar fumes that the use of Sanskrit words has declined in the country. Why this anguish and fury? Does he call it wrong for a country to protect its own language with the greatest care and prevent foreign words from mixing into it? He has not merely said so; he has spoken with anger.

"Therefore, we must now restore Sanskrit to its former position," he declared with passion and fury.

Why this cry of grief? Is Sanskrit not supposedly a uniquely distinguished, jewel-like language? Why must it be allowed to mix with other languages? Why grieve that Sanskrit has faded because its admixture in other languages has declined? Why not cultivate Sanskrit itself properly and make it prosper?

But it is a secret known not only to Achariyar but to everyone that this cannot be done. Sanskrit exists only in written form. He therefore appears to think that by joining it to other languages possessing both spoken and written forms, he can grant it special distinction and thereby put Manu's "reform" ideas into practice in the country.

However much Sanskrit, a language existing only in writing and dead in ordinary speech, mingles and associates with languages that possess both written and spoken forms, it can never regain the esteem it has lost. It certainly cannot.

Although Achariyar understands this, when he reads the *Manu Dharmashastra* and other Sanskrit works, he seems to offer Sanskrit reassurance:

"Ah, Sanskrit! How great is your glory? Have you, endowed with such power, fallen to this condition? Am I, a descendant of the ancestors who created you, to stand watching you in this state? Though I now lack the authority to restore you to your former position, my dear friend Madhava Menon is the Education Minister of this province. With his assistance, I shall return you to your former place. Do not worry."

Education Minister Madhava Menon answers, "Why fear while I am here?"

He says:

"The present educational plan does not provide sufficient opportunity to learn Sanskrit. Only two hours a week have been allotted to it. I am therefore planning to take the necessary steps to increase that time. People educated in Sanskrit cannot obtain certain jobs. Sanskrit's progress has been obstructed for this reason.

"I do not know who caused Sanskrit to be pushed backwards in this manner. I am therefore striving to repeal the law responsible."

Through these words, Education Minister Madhava Menon grants protection and support to Achariyar, who spoke as though he had taken refuge with him.

Having said so much, Madhava Menon also revealed one truth without concealing it:

"In the Malayalam region, everyone once learnt Sanskrit. But only a few study it now."

If the Education Minister does not know the reason, we shall tell him. People have clearly understood that when other languages such as Sanskrit mix with their own, their mother tongue is damaged. That is why an unavoidable aversion to learning Sanskrit has arisen everywhere. Even those who once learnt it willingly no longer so much as glance at it.

The Education Minister should understand this. He should also know that any attempt to spread Sanskrit by relying upon official power will end as a fruitless undertaking.

The Education Minister worries that he does not know who caused Sanskrit's decline. No individual caused it. Changing times and the people's attachment to their own languages are responsible. The power conferred by ministerial office cannot alter that cause.

That is why we say Madhava Menon's medicine and Achariyar's prediction will not work.

*Dravida Nadu*, 26 March 1950.

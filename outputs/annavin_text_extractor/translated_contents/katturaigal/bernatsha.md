## English Translation

Writers may walk with their chests held high. It need not be said that only merchant princes, landed magnates, and officials can accumulate great wealth while writers must remain poor until the end. Writers can now gain hope that they too may become wealthy enough to arouse the envy of magnates. George Bernard Shaw has created such an awakening for the community of writers by accumulating and leaving behind immense wealth through his writing.

Bernard Shaw was a writer of a new kind, gifted in presenting rational thought with flavour. He criticised old ideas through mockery and satire and worked for the spread of social equality throughout the world. His books were praised across the globe. Acting in his plays was regarded as a great honour for performers. Many of his stories were made into films, and his books were translated into many languages.

Because his books sold in their hundreds of thousands, Shaw received enormous income from them. The estate he left is valued at 600,000 pounds. No one would have expected a writer and playwright to accumulate wealth of such magnitude.

Under the system of death duties in force in Britain, seventy percent of the estate Bernard Shaw left will go to the government as tax. The tax amounts to 420,000 pounds. Shaw arranged for the remaining wealth to be used for an unusual purpose.

He set aside a large portion of his estate for the work of reforming the English language into a system containing forty letters. He also arranged for the British Museum, a theatrical association, and an art gallery in Ireland to receive shares of his estate.

Just as George Bernard Shaw caused many intellectuals throughout the world to think through his ideas, he has also created a difficult question through the arrangements he made for spending his property.

The courts will soon examine whether his bequest of a large part of the estate to advance a forty-letter English language is legally valid.

Although many have been praised as monarchs of the pen, few have been able to accumulate great wealth through the pen as Shaw did.

The world of writers may point to Shaw, reflect upon him, and rejoice. It can celebrate the proof that writing can generate great wealth.

If anyone speaks mockingly and says, "He is only a writer," one may respond proudly: "Is being a writer contemptible? Do you know how much our Bernard Shaw earned through writing? Six hundred thousand pounds."

How was it possible to accumulate so much wealth?

The English language possesses so vast and extensive an empire. English publications can spread from Australia at one end to Canada at the other. The people are widely educated and take pleasure and pride in buying books. They do not think only that their mansions must have decoration, their bodies ornaments, and their vehicles brilliance. They believe that their home must also be a place of reading and that only then will it possess beauty and delight, flavour and benefit.

They buy publications in abundance. Consequently, works that offer a feast of ideas are printed and sold in hundreds of thousands of copies. Publishers can earn good income, and writers can accumulate abundant wealth.

Here, if a total of two thousand copies are sold, the writer is considered a fortunate man.

If, by rare chance, someone possesses the skill to create a portrait of ideas that captures the people's hearts and his publications gain a little momentum and sell, there are virtuous souls who pour out their jealousy, beat their mouths and stomachs, and lament: "He makes a living by writing! He lives from his writing!"

There, Bernard Shaw was able to accumulate 600,000 pounds because he shone as a monarch of the literary world.

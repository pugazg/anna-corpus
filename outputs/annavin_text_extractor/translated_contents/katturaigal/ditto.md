## English Translation

Qutub Minar!

The Taj Mahal!

The sculptures of Ajanta!

His family was so humble and ordinary that he could scarcely have had an opportunity even to hear these names. During the days when he struggled through work in a hotel, a metal factory and the battlefield, he could not even have imagined that he might one day make triumphant tours abroad, confer with statesmen of many nations and address foreign parliaments.

Tito, who came from such a life, has now arrived aboard the ship *Galeb*. The Indian Government sent ships such as the *Godavari* and *Jamuna* as a ceremonial escort to welcome it.

The point to consider is not what benefit Marshal Tito's visit will produce, but what kind of person attained such an elevated position.

Fifteen children; Tito was the ninth!

Tito was not the name his parents gave him. It was an alias he adopted after becoming a revolutionary, when police hunted him and he had to live underground. His original name has now fallen out of use, and the world calls him Tito.

He was born in 1892, and his parents named him Josip Broz.

In a family of fifteen children, Josip could not be raised in comfort. Imagine his position as the ninth child.

He had the means to complete only primary school. He then had to earn a hard living by labouring in the fields, a workshop or wherever work could be found.

No one would have imagined that this boy might become the unrivalled leader of a nation and gain worldwide fame and influence.

His parents could only have worried that the Heavenly Father had placed a great burden upon them: how were they to raise fifteen children?

Josip had to begin working at the age of twelve. His parents thought it enough if he did any work and earned the bread his own stomach required. At one time he reportedly worked as a boy in a hotel.

Later he joined an engineering works as a labourer. He worked and learned a trade there until the age of seventeen, then returned to his village of Kumrovec. Many occupations, constant wandering, all merely to keep the vehicle of life moving.

After he passed the age of twenty, Josip entered compulsory military service and went to the battlefield in 1914.

Sergeant Major Josip Broz fought fiercely, was gravely wounded and lay for thirteen months in a military hospital housed in a monastery. When Josip found an opportunity to condemn administrative disorder, he provoked a superior's anger. That officer found a pretext and had him sentenced to prison.

But at that same moment the Russian Revolution erupted. Josip gained freedom. Was it merely freedom? He gained a new heart!

Before the young man's eyes, the Tsarist regime that had dominated Russia collapsed with a crash. He saw the common people who had lain submissively stand upright. Ordinary people laughed at prison and smiled at the gallows. He heard the formerly voiceless speak loudly. Those who had bent low and performed menial service now marched proudly and trumpeted, “We will no longer allow our rights to be destroyed.” Palace-dwelling fanatics, who had imagined that a mere frown would crush revolution, kissed their wine cups one last time and fled headlong.

Josip had the opportunity to witness the incomparable Russian Revolution that astonished the world. What better university could there be? He gained a new spirit.

As he walked through the garden in which revolution had blossomed, a new ideal formed in his young heart. In that same revolutionary garden he also found the fruit of love and married a sixteen-year-old girl named Pelagija. His present wife is his second.

Josip already cared deeply about organising the workers' camp. The renewed enthusiasm produced by witnessing the Russian Revolution led him to engage in the labour movement with greater energy and confidence. The Metalworkers' Union gave him his connection with and training in that movement.

Josip worked to bring scattered workers into a single formation and give strength and maturity to many trade unions. He acquired the standing of a workers' leader and also attracted the government's attention. Consequently, in 1928 the government sentenced him to five years in prison.

He was released in 1934, but on condition that he remain under police surveillance and not leave his native village.

Josip evaded the police and departed. It was then that he gave himself the name Tito. From 1937 onward, that name became permanent.

While underground he first adopted the name Rudi. Because someone else already used the same pseudonym, he had to change it to Tito.

When efforts were made from Moscow to turn the Yugoslav workers' movement into a fighting organisation, Tito went to Moscow for training. There he was given the name Walter.

Tito's influence grew until revolutionary circles declared with confidence that he could liberate Yugoslavia. Through tireless labour he became secretary of the Yugoslav Communist Party.

The great war ignited by Hitler became Tito's opportunity to realise his ideal.

Prince Paul, driven by the ignoble thought that palace life and luxury were all that mattered and that it made no difference whether power rested with him or with the stronger Hitler, reached an accommodation with Hitler and agreed to permit a German base in Yugoslavia.

Tito's heart seethed. True patriots observed a day of mourning and expressed their condemnation. People of feeling rose throughout the country. Some young men in the air force acted. Paul was arrested and exiled. The ruler who had surrendered was removed.

German and Italian armies marched in.

Tito realised that the moment had come. He filled workers and the public with heroic fervour.

A liberation war committee was established, with Tito as its leader. Tito was also commander of the army. It was then that he became Marshal Tito.

Through Tito's extraordinary ability, by 1943 the liberation army had grown vigorous enough to become a headache for Hitler. The country began to move according to Tito's word.

When the three great leaders Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin met at the Tehran Conference, they recognised Marshal Tito's liberation army and declared Yugoslavia an Allied country.

After the war Tito's future was secure. Elections were held for a constituent assembly; Tito's position became unrivalled, and Marshal Tito began governing Yugoslavia.

In 1948, tension developed between Moscow and Tito.

“I am a communist, but not Moscow's nodding doll,” Tito said.

“If you are a true communist, you must act as Moscow says,” Moscow replied.

“Yugoslavia is an independent country with its own government. I will not depart from the doctrine of common ownership. But if the controlling reins are to remain in Moscow, find someone else for the job,” Tito stated firmly.

“Is finding another person difficult? Just watch,” Moscow muttered, and displayed its skill in every field. It attempted to frighten Yugoslavia into submission by creating domestic opposition, producing economic pressure and massing troops nearby.

“Tito is the name I gave myself. Moscow named me Walter; even that name has become unnecessary. Am I now to accept Moscow's domination alone? Impossible,” he declared. He strengthened power and unity within Yugoslavia and firmly demonstrated its capacity to stand independently.

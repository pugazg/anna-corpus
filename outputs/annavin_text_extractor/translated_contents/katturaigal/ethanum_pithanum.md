## English Translation

"I possess two crores in cash. Take it; here is the key to the iron safe. I shall transfer all four of my two-storeyed houses into your name. There is gold jewellery that belonged to my deceased wife; take that too. I own many acres of wet and dry land. It is all yours. There are numerous beautiful groves and gardens; I have made those yours as well. There is a car; ride in it. But you must do me one great favour. I shall beg from four houses and bring back what I receive. Give me a place on this veranda to sit and eat it, and permit me to lie down here."

He spoke, pleaded, and performed. He was a madman.

"All right. Let all these things remain with me. But I do not like that final request. Give, but do not ask. I shall enjoy the property; you must stop after giving it. Do not tell me to take this or give you that."

The other man spoke firmly. He was a cheat.

If a madman and a cheat spoke like this, we would laugh. But if it actually happened, would we not be stunned?

In India it has become a perpetual drama. We watch it and despise it, while some still enjoy it with noisy enthusiasm.

The madman only spoke, but our provincial governments actually do what he said.

They gathered all tax revenue in Delhi, bowed before it, and returned. They handed every power to those "modern nawabs" and signed a bond of slavery declaring, "We shall remain submissive; we require no rights of our own." They surrendered commerce to their will, justice to their pronouncements, and administration to their designs. Law belongs to them, and so does the authority to demand an account. Having given away everything, the provinces take pride in begging and receiving.

India's new constitutional scheme has turned the provinces into logs of wood. Their condition has therefore become one of crying, "Bhavati, bhiksham dehi", "Lady, give me alms." Yet without a grain of shame or an atom of pain, these rulers roam about disregarding the people and treating the ministerial chair as the only reality.

The madman in the story could not have done what he proposed because, in truth, he possessed no such property. But under the government of this country, prosperous Dravidam really does lie trapped. They hand it to the northern cheat and then ask for a place to sit on his outer veranda.

The cheat in the tale refused the madman's tiny request merely for comic effect. India's central government says the same thing in earnest, without the slightest fear.

They proclaimed one India and spoke of *Akhil Bharat*. They said Delhi must be its heart of authority and that all powers, taxes, influence, distinction, splendour, honour, and fame must therefore be concentrated there. Delhi received everything, beyond even what it expected, enough to make its rulers faint with astonishment.

Anyone who read Prime Minister Nehru's speech in the Indian legislature last week would remember this performance by the madman and the cheat.

He spoke in the manner of saying that provincial governments depend far too heavily upon the central government and that this must not continue. Nehru spoke even more forcefully than the cheat who said, "Give me everything and ask for nothing."

Today the provinces are mere clay dolls. Their powers have been sucked away, their rights have dried up, and self-government has died. The character and habit of acting for themselves have been lost; their capacity to decide anything for themselves has fallen asleep.

Our plan is that the provinces must not be reduced to madness and the central government must not earn the accusation of being a cheat.

That is why we conduct our journey towards the city of our ideal with the resolve that we shall not sleep until we make the Dravidam in which we were born our own, establish our own government within it, and make it shine as one among the many nations of the world.

Only when Dravida Nadu separates will the people's suffering end, sorrow depart, and happiness blossom. It will take time for everyone to understand this, but its first echoes can already be heard everywhere.

The cooperation between provincial and central governments today resembles the children's tale in which one person says, "I shall supply the chaff; you bring the flattened rice and pour it into the chaff; then the two of us will keep blowing and eat." It is a ridiculous union, and a disastrous separation must arise one day.

Without the slightest tremor in his heart, Nehru says the provincial governments should not seek the central government's assistance for anything. But what is the patient to do? The Indian Prime Minister's statement presents the pitiful spectacle of one who first makes a person ill, then acts as the doctor, and finally beats the patient and drives him away.

If a cheat first turns a good man into a madman, then seizes his property and casts him out, the horror is unbearable even to hear. That is precisely the condition today.

## English Translation

“Throw me into prison. I shall remain there. For this young woman, suffering in a harsh prison would be far better than becoming the wife of this old man!”

A woman spoke before a court, with a courage that made the men who imagine themselves endowed with the power to control and rule feel fear and astonishment. The beautiful woman said, “Throw me into prison, but do not throw me into the abyss of an incompatible marriage.”

She was young. Arrangements had been made to marry her to a fifty-year-old man. Those responsible thought: is this a custom unknown in the country, an event absent from our sacred books? That is why they dared make a young woman an old man’s wife. The scene has occurred countless times. Her slender waist may sway with youth while age coils through his body, yet both are placed in the marriage pavilion. Agni stands before them; an Iyer chants mantras; relatives and parents roll their eyes; village elders stand mute; there is a marriage thread and there are drums. She becomes his wife. A green shoot is grafted onto a dying tree.

The woman weeps, unable to speak; the old man rejoices that another victory has been won. Knowing she does not desire his age, the wealthy old man may give her the necklace she wants, golden bangles, and a jewelled serpent ornament for her hair. Her parents delight themselves by saying, “The bridegroom may be old, but the ornaments he fastened are gold.”

They imagined this marriage would proceed in the same way, those fools unaware that womanhood had awakened.

The woman spoke. A voice expressing the mind of women was heard. The call rang out, the drum of modernity sounded.

“I think imprisonment is better than marrying this old man,” she said.

The notables were stunned. Men curled within antiquity must have writhed as though touched by fire. She had spoken the truth with inner strength: prison is better than this cruel life.

“What audacity!” men seeking domination may have cried. Women who had gained some clarity would have smiled and rejoiced that one woman had spoken bravely.

The marriage was reportedly stopped. Friends may have seen the report in the newspapers a few days ago.

We praise the conduct of a woman of the new world.

But can everyone acquire the mighty strength to oppose a tiger? How many women, frightened by the anger of narrow-minded people, are given in marriage to old men, invalids, madmen, habitual drunkards, and men disposed to cruelty? How many suffer, become living corpses despised by society, lose the proper path of life, and fall into degradation? Can all of them speak like that woman and say, “Throw me into prison”? They shed tears and lament their fate.

We see plenty of women who still struggle to move beyond the habit of surrendering their hearts to men’s adventurous talk. These are women who have only partly escaped antiquity’s grip. How can women in families wholly trapped by it escape incompatible marriage and the pain of marrying someone they do not desire?

Can every woman say, “Throw me into prison if you wish”? Speech may falter and eyes alone speak, but theirs is an unintelligible language to the people of the city of perversity.

If preventing this disaster depends solely on each woman’s courage, it is doubtful whether even one in ten thousand will escape the abyss. Rationalists must create conditions and build a society in which this great danger cannot approach women.

Would it become a government to say, “No one knows what wicked beast may arrive from which direction and at what time; citizens must possess the courage and strength to strike it dead or drive it away if they wish to survive”? Is it not the mark of government to establish beforehand protection and conditions preventing wild beasts from entering the country? Likewise, is it not foolish, and more than foolish, a great deceit, to let systems that ruin women roam freely and then say a courageous woman may escape while others must endure whatever comes?

The moon is overhead. A gentle breeze blows all around and flowers spread fragrance. Music fills the lovers’ eyes as they approach the gate of the city of delight. Smiles, unfinished sentences, enchanting glances: the heart becomes an ocean of joy.

“This is the feast I awaited. How many days I lay longing to receive it; how many nights I spent sleepless in sorrow. How many troubles, griefs, dangers, and barriers I crossed to enter this garden. Today I have attained the purpose of birth; this is the victory life must yield. Now I shall see heaven here on earth. My joy, my treasure, my honey, my breeze, my unwasting moon, my unfading flower!” He does not merely speak; he sings.

She says less, but her glance says more; her smile becomes a new dictionary.

Both wander happily, imagining they have attained a state unequalled in the world. They have triumphed in love. Their future life appears beautiful before their eyes. It is spring.

Autumn comes. More than that, the great wind that tears open the knots of life begins to blow. After the same lovers become a “married couple,” they are caught in that gale. Then what?

“You curse! Is causing trouble your only work? I do not know why I became trapped like this. It would be better for my fate if I put on ochre robes and went to Kasi or Rameswaram,” he says.

Yes, it is the same man who once combed her hair, wiped perspiration from her brow, and swore he would not look at another long-eyed woman. The lover became a husband and launched the vessel called family upon the sea of life. A cyclone drove it toward the city of disaster. The man who spoke words of love now speaks in fury and makes her weep. Alas, how destructive those tears are: they have turned her mirror-like cheeks into a miserable sight.

Anger in the hall, sorrow in the kitchen, tears by the well, anguish when she sees the prattling children: this is the condition of the woman deceived after expecting happiness from life.

Can calls of “My love! My joy!” remain? Arrows of abuse pierce her ears: “Dog! Devil! Destroyer! Deceiver!”

What would those who once saw them as lovers think and say upon seeing them now? “Are these the people who stood as examples of the nobility of love and told many that heaven could be obtained on earth? They sang and danced joyfully in grove and street, but now quarrel and fight. Their home, filled with shrieks and wailing, resembles a little forest. Why did they reach this condition?”

The woman says nothing, fearing the town’s blame and relatives’ abuse. But if she finds a trustworthy friend with a pure heart that listens and wipes away grief, she slowly recounts how the flower garden became a desert, how the man who led her to a feast brought disaster, how life became the opposite of what she expected, and why she shed tears.

“He swore a thousand times that his love would never diminish. His torment is why I am now only skin and bone. He called his dwelling an upstairs house; only after I arrived did I discover it was a cattle shed. He said he owned fertile fields; I find only empty land, and even that is pledged to another for debt. He said his buffalo gave a measure of milk; it is barren. He said he served as village watchman; he only wanders the village and has no job. He said there was gold, jewels, and sandal paste to apply. All of it proved merely pretty speech along the riverbank. He has no work, loses his senses at the sight of a woman in a sari, and believes a household is best governed with a stick. I became his wife and reached this fate,” she says through tears.

Such hardships are numerous in family life, especially in our country and in an age when clarity and prosperity have not entered our marriage system.

The maxim and method of the “wise” is to tell a thousand lies if necessary to light one wedding lamp. We see its result in countless families as crying and beating. Because women are not given adequate knowledge and preparation, they bear life’s burden while rocking the cradle and importing beliefs that Murugan will wipe their tears, Kali will help, the Karthigai fast will bear fruit, or his planetary position will improve within Adi or Avani.

If lovers were clearly shown the true condition of life, the environment surrounding married life, and the obstacles likely to arise, their garden wanderings would not turn into torment after marriage.

The road is hard and full of stones and thorns; there are many difficulties, but if they are expected, consolation, mutual compassion to remove them, and a determination not to magnify them give a family strength to endure. Instead, if the man lies during courtship and conducts the woman into marriage, the house inevitably becomes a forest and suffering increases. The marriage has occurred, so must she accept every longing, sorrow, and hardship? May she not even weep?

In courtship he speaks without revealing life’s actual circumstances. Later the astrologer supplies a leaf, there is vermilion and turmeric, and a marriage takes place between a supposedly virtuous woman who never raises her bowed head and a hunchback of coarse understanding. How many cruelties arise in the resulting family? How many do we see in life?

Ask, “How did he ever marry her?” and someone replies, “What a strange question! Could the writing that destined her to become his wife have been wrong?”

She is golden, he is dark; she is beautiful, he is otherwise; she is wealthy, he is poor. If one offers reasons why he is unsuitable, people shamelessly say, “Who can know who is destined for whom?”

The frenzied man beats; the quick-tempered woman weeps; the drunk wanders; the chaste wife laments. People observe that their life is filled with suffering, but conclude, “Her destiny was written that way.”

Marriages conducted without thought and without compatibility have scorched womanhood. Women pour out tears and fold their hands before every temple. They fall into wells, seek rope, swallow poison, burn themselves, and die. Nor are these the only cruelties; some fall even into the abyss of prostitution.

Those distressed by repeatedly witnessing these disasters and wishing to save women studied the course of the modern intellectual world and proposed remedies.

For years those proposals were explained, debated, and defended against orthodox objections. Only then did they acquire the form and condition required for legislation. Thus the Sarda Act prohibiting child marriage was born as necessary to women’s liberation. Orthodox men rose shouting, “Sanatana has fallen! Our religion has collapsed!” The law ignored their noise. It helped wipe away women’s tears to some extent.

Cruel scenes diminished in which a fruit was placed in a child’s hand, a crow or sparrow shown to distract her, and a marriage thread tied around her neck. So too did the tragedy of a girl, only after reaching the age of flowers in her hair, darkened eyes, and awakening desire, hearing grandmothers say: “Child, you do not know. The bridegroom who garlanded you has died of a heart attack. Peacock, do not weep; cruel fate did it. Cast away the flower, worship the Mother, read *Bhakta Vijayam*, and you will join your husband’s feet.” Only then learning she was considered inauspicious, the innocent girl widowed by her parents’ ignorance suffered as though a swarm of red scorpions stung her heart.

But these measures were insufficient to end women’s great sorrow. Central and provincial governments began to move toward fundamental rights needed to improve their lives: prohibiting bigamy, enabling divorce, and granting property rights.

Movements such as the Brahmo Samaj did some work in parts of India so women might recognize their suffering and men understand and sympathize. But in the South, no one except Self-Respecters spent the preceding twenty-five years explaining this problem publicly and gathering support for reform. Some individuals may have spoken of women’s freedom and practised it within their families. Yet it was the reviled Self-Respecters who boldly and continuously declared, without regard for orthodox abuse, Sanatanist curses, villainous attacks, the unworthy person’s insults, popular fear, or opponents’ lamentation:

“Women must be liberated. It is not enough to praise them in poetry as eyes, sugar cane, and fruit juice, or bless them with the names Savitri, Sita, Ahalya, and Anasuya. The injustice that makes them men’s prey must be abolished. They must be given the right to divorce and the right to property.”

A law incorporating divorce and property rights was drafted, delighting them, gladdening the intellectual world, and giving women new hope that their sex would escape cruelty. Many said that when the country had obtained freedom from bondage, it was entirely appropriate to remove the chains locked upon women. Orthodox forces opposed it as usual, offering the same stale reasons.

Ancient doctrine would disappear! It was the product of infatuation with novelty! Religion would be ruined! Dharma would perish!

The men who screeched against opening temples had said the same. They now cried against this measure and have not stopped. They hurl the Agamas and summon ancient dharma as a witness. As though these were insufficient, they supply one more strange reason: women themselves do not want it.

Women supposedly do not desire liberation. Perhaps they prefer turmoil in the family. Perhaps they desire a life of suffering as wives to wandering-minded men, lacking even the courage to ask why, and being beaten and kicked if they do ask. What evidence do these honourable gentlemen offer that women do not want freedom? How they discovered it is unknown.

Before self-rule was explained and demonstrated, did the ordinary person in village and hamlet ask for it? Did he believe when told that self-rule would come? Only after a national freedom army had been built and explanations given for years did the ordinary mind begin to understand liberty’s greatness. Did freedom fighters refrain from beginning because the ordinary person’s mind was unclear?

Until the last moment, the white man said that only people seeking political office demanded self-rule and that the Indian citizen preferred white rule. Such claims were made in Parliament itself. Did we respect them? Would anyone devoted to national freedom do so?

Today orthodox forces repeat the white man’s argument: women themselves do not want reform. How can we respect that claim, the speech of Sanatana Churchills? Why should we?

Do they remain idle? Would they? They strive to turn every available opportunity to their advantage.

To make at least some people regard the speech and conduct of these Sanatana Churchills as the product of intelligence, *The Hindu*, which spreads antiquity using paper instead of palm leaf, writes:

There must be no haste. It is supposedly unwise to enact new laws one after another from enthusiasm for women’s liberation. The social structure will collapse. Sanatana doctrine is no ordinary thing.

Thus writes *The Hindu*.

The sacred-thread drum works vigorously to obstruct and break every effort to wipe away women’s sorrow.

We deeply regret that Babu Rajendra Prasad, a gentle and conciliatory man, has fallen into this Sanatana conspiracy.

They say that granting the right to divorce will abolish the institution of family and produce disastrous consequences. We do not know what disaster they mean. Men in the sacred stories staked their wives in gambling, abandoned them blindfolded in forests, and while afflicted with leprosy were carried by their wives to courtesans’ houses. Are women to be left to suffer such exemplary lives?

The old strongmen rejoice that they have made Babu Rajendra Prasad speak for antiquity. Young people, especially those in Congress, bear the great responsibility of showing them the country’s true heart. Orthodox forces believe they can warm themselves for a while using the opinion of a Babu who holds respect and influence in Congress. Recognizing their deceit, the country must immediately declare clearly and firmly: we shall not allow mad lovers of antiquity to obstruct women’s liberation.

Not only the tears of the poor but also the tears shed by women will turn into poisonous water that destroys our society.

30-1-49

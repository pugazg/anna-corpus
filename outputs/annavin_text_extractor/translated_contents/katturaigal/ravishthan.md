## English Translation

“The Sikhs do not ask for a separate country. They do not press a demand like Pakistan. The Sikhs do not wish the country to be divided again. I state this firmly: the Sikhs are not going to demand Sikhistan. But...” Sardar Baldev Singh spoke in this manner before Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru a few days ago during the Guru Nanak celebration.

Numerous Sikhs had gathered for the festival. It was then that Sardar Baldev Singh gave Pandit Nehru this assurance.

Why was such an assurance needed? This speech did not arise without a reason. Why did Sardar Baldev Singh find it necessary to speak about Sikhistan?

If information had not come from various quarters alleging that Sikhs were occupied with the idea of seeking a separate country, why would a speech declaring that they would not demand one and would not divide the country arise at a Guru Nanak celebration, where one ought to speak about the dignity of the Sikh faith and the greatness of Guru Nanak's teaching?

Only when the sky darkens, the cold wind blows and thunder is heard does talk begin about whether rain will come. Likewise, some sign of concern is visible there. Sardar Baldev Singh feels that Nehru and others may become alarmed by it. That is why he reassures Nehru and cools his heart by saying the Sikhs will not demand a separate country.

What is the actual condition there? However much it is concealed, small cracks have begun to appear. The Sikhs are not content. They are thinking and speaking about some ideal. The talk has not merely reached Delhi; it has gained enough force to make Nehru anxious and Baldev Singh offer consolation.

Sikhs are not Hindus. They reject caste, Vedic ritual, doctrines of the Vedic age and the varna-ashrama system. They do not practise image worship.

To obtain God's grace, Sikhs regard a good life, moral conduct and good deeds as important, not ritual bathing and worship.

The teaching of Guru Nanak and of the Sikh Gurus who spread it further prepared the way not merely for a new religion in the North but for a new society. Sikhism was not formed merely as an occasional cleansing act or reform intended to remove stains that time and the cunning of wrongdoers had placed upon Hinduism.

It cut away the taproot of Hinduism and established a new path. It rejected varna-ashrama and declared that caste must not exist. There are Sikhs, but among them there is no ordering as Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya and Shudra.

All are precious children of God, and caste must not be introduced among them. Sikhism arose as a revolutionary faith declaring this.

After the age of the Buddha, Sikhism was the intellectual revolution in this subcontinent that abolished varna-ashrama, which had been asserted as the foundation of Hinduism.

The Sikhs hated and completely abandoned the varna-ashrama system that Vyasa, Vasishtha, Manu and Mandhata upheld, and which Hindus had believed sacred and akin to God's command for a very long time.

Sikhism also abandoned another foundational principle of Hinduism, image worship. Sikhs did not adopt worship of forms such as a flute-playing god, a deity dancing with the left leg raised, or Murugan riding a peacock. They said that righteous conduct was what one needed to obtain God's grace.

At a time when Islam, without caste or image worship, had arrived from abroad on one side, and Hinduism with its Vedic varna-ashrama system rooted from ancient times stood within the country on the other, Sikhism arose and made the people virtuous and courageous. Because varna-ashrama was abolished, Sikhs became a disciplined and united society. Because they did not adopt image worship, they were under no compulsion to engage in counterfeit religious practices. They shone with a new radiance, regarding purity, truth and work to relieve the people's suffering as most important.

The terrible attempts of Muslim rulers to destroy their rise, and the hardships and losses consequently suffered by Sikh Gurus and leaders, cultivated an unending enmity between Sikhs and Muslims. Yet if one considers only the religious sphere, the two paths are constructed so that there is no necessity for them to oppose one another.

The same cannot be said of Sikhism and Hinduism. Hinduism rests upon varna-ashrama; to Sikhs, caste must not exist. Hindus practise image worship; Sikhs do not.

Sikhs now realise that they are bound within a Hindu state. That condition does not give them satisfaction, and it will not.

No Sikh can avoid swelling with pride when thinking of the old kingdoms they established and ruled with courage near the banks of the Ravi, or the worthy wars fought by their ancestors. Pride comes first. Then, when they consider the present arrangement that requires them to remain merely a “minority community” in a Hindu state, a deep sigh arises.

Can waves of longing fail to rise in the mind: “In those days! In our own kingdom!”

“Whatever else may be true, they are Hindus and we are Sikhs. Do they not stand as givers and we as recipients? Why should we not possess a homeland and a separate government of our own? We shall remain inseparable friends with Hindus, but can we not obtain a separate country without losing that friendship?” They think in this manner.

“Why not? Do Tibet and Nepal not exist?” comes the answer.

This talk has reached Delhi, and reached it with force. Because he is a member of the government, Baldev Singh must say that the Sikhs will not demand a separate country. It is his duty. But even he has added a “but.”

What is that “but”?

“The Sikhs do not demand a separate country. But they fear that, under the cover of abolishing communalism, even the rights justly due to them may be taken away. The new constitutional scheme gives rise to this fear,” Sardar Baldev Singh said.

Baldev Singh himself says there is fear that rights may be lost. He asks that just rights not be taken away. He reminds his listeners that Sikhs fear the new political scheme may destroy their legitimate rights. Yet he assures them that they do not demand a separate country.

The “but” he uses has become “therefore” for another leader.

Baldev Singh says: “There is fear that, using the abolition of communalism as a pretext, the new constitution will deprive Sikhs of rights justly due to them. But...” Master Tara Singh says: “There is fear that our rights will be lost. Therefore the Sikhs need a separate country and separate government.”

Master Tara Singh is a Sikh leader, one who has led the Sikhs since Baldev Singh's youth. His cry for rights creates fear in Delhi circles. Hearing his courageous words, innumerable Sikhs have begun pressing the ideal that Ravistan must be established.

Master Tara Singh's speech tastes bitter to Delhi. Newspapers from the Himalayas to Kanyakumari have therefore imposed a blackout, and we do not know the extent of his campaign.

But we do know that talk of Ravistan has spread far enough to require Baldev Singh to speak at the Guru Nanak celebration in a manner intended to cool a seething heart.

We are not now discussing whether the demand for Ravistan or Sikhistan will succeed. We cite it to show that voices of awakening have arisen in many parts of the Indian subcontinent, insisting that rights must not be lost.

Master Tara Singh and Sardar Baldev Singh may differ, and do differ, over the method and plan for safeguarding Sikh rights. But their objective is the same: Sikh rights must not be taken away, and Sikhs must not be ruined under the pretext of abolishing communalism.

## A Storm of Strikes

1. Railway workers.
2. Port workers.
3. Postal department workers.

It appears that workers in these three important sectors will go on strike. It also appears that plans are being made to conduct all three strikes at the same time.

If a storm of strikes blows through these three sectors, it will cause not merely great trouble but a crisis for the country. The esteem in which today's rulers are held abroad will fade. The storm is capable of causing immense difficulty and distress to both the public and workers.

This crisis must be prevented quickly, not by beating people or by repression, nor by imprisoning many leaders, but with the aid of an honourable course and by gathering the affection of the workers.

(*Dravida Nadu*, 5 December 1948)

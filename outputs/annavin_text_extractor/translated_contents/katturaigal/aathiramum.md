## English Translation

Tamil title: ஆத்திரமும் ஆயாசமும் நமக்கு வேண்டாம்

Would a person who must perform the great task of labouring for the liberation of Dravidian society also need to undertake the separate work of eliminating a traitor? I would have said, "No, let him be," and come away.

I have never been accustomed to asking for a place or standing and knocking at a door. Even now, when a railway carriage is crowded, I simply remain standing; I am not one who pushes and forces his way inside. Had I received even the slightest hint that the leader of the Kazhagam suspected me or regarded me as a traitor, I would have thanked him for all the affection and support he had shown until then and come away.

As I have explained many times, this broad world has room even for ordinary people like me. Are there not mendicants who live without the support of any religious endowment? I would have lived like them. But he never showed even the faintest sign of such suspicion.

He has reproved me as lazy. He has remarked that I roam about with young people. But he never called me a traitor, nor did I ever have occasion even to imagine that such a thought might exist in his heart. The affection and respect he showed me often made me feel embarrassed.

I remember it well: the first time he invited me to Erode; the way he described the special importance of entering public life; and, when I later prepared to return home, how he had the large carriage from his house harnessed, seated me in it, and followed behind for about ten houses as it departed. I grew flustered inside the carriage and repeatedly said, "Please stop; do not come any farther," feeling deeply embarrassed.

From that day until the marriage issue assumed its frightening form, his conduct towards me never created any need to wonder whether he held a contemptuous or malicious opinion of me. He has been angry with me many times, and I too have been angry. Many years ago, when *Viduthalai* was being published from Erode, I became angry and returned to my own town. I still possess the separate letters written to reconcile me by Periyar and by his elder brother.

Today, when Periyar speaks of placing me on a list of traitors, I remember those letters and find peace. Truly, I even feel happy. Though today, in anger and disappointment, he denounces me as a traitor and a selfish man, I feel only happiness when I remember the affection and attachment he showed in earlier days.

Every one of you will have had similar experiences, for he has spoken about all of us with such pride. That is why I ask you not to attach importance to his present abuse.

An unexpected situation has arisen for him. He has therefore been compelled to use language contrary to his nature. That is all. By continuing our work and demonstrating it, we must enable the country to recognize our good intentions. That is our duty. I emphasize again and again that, on hearing his angry words, we must neither become angry ourselves nor grow weary.

His supporters are conducting cunning propaganda. The purpose demonstrated by the new Kazhagam we have now formed is only that our cooperation and collective labour will no longer belong to the Dravidar Kazhagam. We are not deranged enough to claim that Periyar will be unable to tour, that no meetings will be held, or that no one anywhere will join him.

Even after thirty years of Self-Respect propaganda, do not men like Kirupananda Variyar still travel here and there delivering discourses on the Puranas? Our former friend K. M. Balasubramaniam has forgotten Ingersoll, embraced Manikkavasagar, and continues preaching Saivism: Aduthurai on the sixth, Papanasam on the seventh, Kottaiyur on the eighth, and so forth.

When all this is possible even for them, is it astonishing that a leader should have opportunities to campaign throughout the country when he has performed public work for thirty years, serves that cause full-time by his own account, has had people like us as advance parties and heralds, and possesses strength in newspapers, money and friendships on a scale available to no one else in the movement?

Some claim to support him, point to his tour, and sing, "Look! Look how magnificent it is!" Is that truly a way of honouring him? Comrades everywhere must think about it.

What mighty tasks he ought to accomplish! How many problems stand before him! This is the hour to attend to the Brahmin-non-Brahmin question, which has taken a form that gives hope of settlement within our generation, and to bring it to an orderly conclusion.

This is the time to consult Andhra leaders, Kerala leaders and Karnataka organizations and formulate a plan for a Dravidian federation. This is the time to meet Ceylon's political leaders and show a sound path to the Tamils being ruined there. What immense problems await!

Forgetting all these, is the task we expect of so great a leader merely to declare at Arur and nearby places, "I have the power to eliminate those who oppose me, and I have a few loyal followers who will support me"? Should his strength and the support available to him be used only to destroy me and abuse those who dared join me?

That power should be used to suppress Achariyar's propaganda for varnashrama, restrain Patel's fascist course, and break the arrogance of northern imperialists. After travelling continually for years through every village and hamlet, without regard for day or night, should the strength he acquired be used at last merely to test whether it is sufficient to eliminate me?

It pains even me to see his present course. It resembles sharpening and testing a sword meant for the battlefield, only to use it to cut down a banana plant growing in the home garden. This course is unnecessary. His aim should not be directed against ordinary people like us. His strength must strike at Sardar Patel, Pandit Nehru, Achariyar, Bajaj, Birla, Sankara Thambiran, repressive legislation and the rulers' ever-new schemes. I remind Periyar that his power exists for those purposes.

The reason his "position" remains limited to that of a provincial leader, and even then only one active among opponents, is precisely this tendency: he repeatedly casts his strength against people like me and brings useless exhaustion upon himself. Even today he regards all those episodes as "victories." No, no! They were occasions on which strength was squandered. Anyone who examines them can understand this clearly.

Again and again, he has used his power to prevent democracy from budding within the Kazhagam. For many years he has spent his gathered strength delighting in the questions, "That man opposed me; what became of him? This man opposed me; what became of him?"

To what use did that power finally come? Merely to rejoice in saying, "I possess the strength to eliminate anyone who refuses to obey me."

He is over seventy years old. He has carried out more propaganda work than any other leader and obtained collective comrades in a manner available to no other leader. Yet what does he say with delight? "In my kingdom, I alone am dictator." Is that all? Must so much strength serve only this?

If all his time and all his strength are spent watching who raises a head in the Kazhagam and who grows in what manner, where is the time to attend to immense questions, and how can the thought of them arise?

What is Chittarasu doing? Where did Mathiazhagan speak? Why was Karunanidhi invited? Why do people support Annadurai? How did Lingam get into the intermediate class? How did Manimozhiyar obtain paper? Why was the women's conference convened? How much profit did those who sold the *Kural* make?

If such matters are treated as great problems and all thought and skill are spent upon them, what fate awaits the great questions that a great leader would naturally attend to and that only a great leader can address? It is because all ability was squandered on these trifles that Dravida has not been liberated despite the existence of such power.

Ignoring this enormous loss, Periyar and his supporters proudly and happily recount incidents in which some people within the Kazhagam were brought down. Given Periyar's power, experience, labour and resources, should all these petty matters appear to his eyes as problems?

There are Nehru and Truman discussing the state of the world. Moscow prepares to welcome Liaquat Ali Khan. Mao Zedong is engaged in making China the socialist fortress of Asia. They are preparing a political order like an iron grip for the Indian subcontinent. Are these not the matters that should appear before Periyar's eyes?

Instead, he files a charge-sheet saying that someone carried away the minutes book, occupied the tables and chairs, or lowered the flag. Is that work commensurate with his power?

His energy ought to be used to formulate plans, offer explanations and seek support on questions such as these: the available paths towards Dravida's separation; the new difficulties that will arise if the new constitution becomes law; the new manner in which the question of separating Dravida Nadu may then need to be considered; the systems a sovereign Dravida would possess; and what its industrial and educational plans would be.

Must that energy instead be used only to repeat, again and again, the same claims about the purpose of the marriage, its explanation, and a supposed scheme of conspiracy by enemies?

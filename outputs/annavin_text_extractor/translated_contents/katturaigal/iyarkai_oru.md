## English Translation

Our country is a place of abundant natural beauty. Yet our people have neither the time to see that splendour nor thoughts that turn in its direction. They will submit to being battered and tormented in order to attend some religious festival, but they will not go to see beautiful scenes in nature. Such sights seem meaningless to them, and they regard viewing them as wasted effort.

I am not referring only to illiterate people condemned to unending labour throughout the day merely to survive.

I mean educated people too, those who work by watching the clock and receive their salary by watching the calendar. Even they do not feel a desire to enjoy natural scenery. If, with great difficulty, they obtain “four days’ leave,” they will go to Tirupati to shave a child’s head or to the Kaveri for their mother’s ancestral rites. No one spends the holiday seeking pleasure in the lovely pictures nature displays. Nature scatters its beauty lavishly and lies unnoticed, like a beautiful young widow.

Open books and you find countless verbal paintings of nature. People have no time to read those either; even when read, many are not understood.

Thus natural beauty lies unused. Even filmmakers, who pile up mountains of profit from making pictures, do not use it. Their forests and countries, hills and valleys, all end within an eight-foot enclosure. Do they depend upon these things to make films? They place more trust in roving eyes, swaying waists and the like, and in *thodi*, *modi* and such devices, than in a mountainside, a pool, a rushing river or a green hill.

It is said that people lack time to see these sights, which delight both eye and mind, because our people work harder and suffer greater fatigue than those of other countries. That is true, but not the whole truth. If there is no time because work is heavy and difficult, and no interest for the same reason, could our country hold a different religious festival every day? Could vast crowds attend them?

People can attend festivals because they consider it a duty. However many hardships arise, they feel they must go. Why? It is a religious undertaking, a means of obtaining Mahesan’s love, a way to cast off sin and a path to earn merit. Knowing this, even railway companies advertise “pilgrimage routes.”

Thus people find the time and possess the will to travel however far. Somehow they go, even by falling into debt. However many obstacles the journey presents, they are prepared to endure them. But do they use even one holiday to see and enjoy natural scenery? No.

“That wretched rain kept pouring and pouring. Nothing but mud. Was there food? Was there water? Was there even a place to lie down? A curse on it! It became a great calamity; the suffering cannot be described. There was a fight on the train, a fight at the pilgrims’ rest house, and a single crush of people in the temple. Someone stole the child’s bracelet. Some fool trod upon my wife’s foot and crushed it. We endured all this to see the procession of the bull mount.

“And look what happened that day! Because of the heavy rain, they said the golden bull was too large to carry, so the deity came upon the smaller silver bull. After spending so much and suffering so greatly, we did not even get to see the golden bull mount. We were not blessed. If we remain alive somehow, we must at least go and worship next year.”

That is how someone speaks after returning from a festival. That is the prevalent attitude.

Because people are interested only in pilgrimages to holy places in search of merit, they do not wish to travel anywhere to see natural scenery. It has come to be regarded as needless work.

Consequently, people not only lose the nectar nature can give them; the artistic feeling that nectar could awaken, and the taste for poetry containing that feeling, become useless to them as well.

We have built “holy places” so profusely throughout the country that there is no opportunity to see places of rest and natural beauty: hills and artificial hillocks, rivers and forest streams, mountains, slopes, woods and uplands.

“A little hill! I sat upon it in the evening as the sunlight faded. I heard a sound and looked towards the forest path some distance below the hill. Do you know what a wonderful sight appeared? I saw a mother elephant run past with two calves following her. After the elephant had gone, a jackal that had until then hidden in a nearby bush sprang out and ran away.

“I saw a peacock dance. I saw a rock pigeon upon a tree branch. I watched a mongoose chase a snake and a python roll along. I heard a wild buffalo bellow. There was a growl; they said it was a tiger.

“I saw a rainbow. Beside the curving bank of a little stream, I saw a long-beaked crane. I heard the flute of a boy grazing a flock on the mountainside and drank in its sweet music. Groves stood on either side, with a river between them and a blue sky above. Sparks glittered there, while fish leapt in the water.”

Can we hear such speech from our people? Writers place it in short stories and readers find it in Sangam literature, but we cannot hear people themselves speak this way. They have never seen these sights, do not think they ought to see them, and do not regard them as precious when someone who has seen them speaks. With a faint mocking laugh, they say, “So there are people who waste their time like this!”

How can literature grow? From where and in what manner can the art of painting arise? For most people, the mind receives none of the joy nature provides.

They speak of the procession of the swan mount, the Lord in mendicant form, the goddess whose sight sends a thrill through the body, or the yogi appearing in saffron robes. How can they speak of nature? Nor is inability the only problem. Because they cannot do so, every scene radiant with natural beauty lies neglected.

In some remote hamlet, a trickster will tell fortunes and a crowd will gather. Nearby there may be a rare natural sight capable of filling any viewer’s mind with delight, yet no one comes to see it.

How many places rich in natural beauty lie neglected in this manner! Westerners identify such places and establish resorts, using them for recreation, pleasure and the enjoyment of nature. Here, despite their beauty, their power to arouse interest and their capacity to create an artistic heart, they lie unnoticed. They resemble the widows of our country, dimmed despite youth and beauty because they have no husband. They are ready to remarry. But you? You have no time!

In Chithirai and Vaikasi one must go to Kanchi for the Garuda procession; in Aani comes the sacred-bath procession; in Aadi, the Aadipooram festival; Avani brings Avittam; Purattasi means Tirupati; Aippasi and Karthigai mean Tiruvannamalai; Margazhi brings Thiruvathirai at Chidambaram; and in the month of Thai...

Every month thus brings a festival in another town, another undertaking for earning merit. Where is the time to climb a hill, travel by boat, walk along a forest path or wander beside a woodland stream?

Nature does not speak. Sometimes a fierce wind does blow; perhaps it is a sign of nature’s anger at us for ignoring her while attending to other business.

At the seashore, a poet stands and watches the sun rise. Is it the sun that he sees? No, a shower of gold! Seeing it, the poet calls, “Younger brother, drink in this new sight!”

Did the younger brother see that glittering brilliance? Would they let him see it? Alas, from his “childhood” onward they took him to the procession of the golden peacock mount and the festival in which Vinayagar comes upon a silver bandicoot. They trained him in those things. He is the very man who has now become the “younger brother” addressed by the bard of Tamil. If the poet calls, “Come, younger brother, see the shower of gold,” how will he come? How is he to know that such a sight exists and that joy will overflow when he sees it?

The procession of the golden peacock mount that he saw from his mother’s hip is the very procession he now attends with his bright-eyed wife, who will bear his child. He will therefore not come to see the sun scatter gold upon the sea.

Not only the poet’s eyes but his ears too receive pleasure. Sea and blue sky join hands, and between them lies the flood of water. That is the veena. What is the blowing wind? The vibration of its strings. What results? Sweet music. Who hears it? The younger brother? How can he hear it, when repeated explosions from temple festivals have ruined the musical sensitivity of his ears? That music falls upon the poet’s ear, and he says, “Younger brother, listen to the generous sea sing its mode!”

In the evening, when the moon has risen, the poet returns to the place where he heard the music and saw the shower of gold. In the morning, had not the sea-maiden worn a golden sari? Now it is evening, with the moon above. “Look, the sea-maiden has put on a new sari of pearls,” says the poet. Having surrendered his heart to that joyous sight, he does not stop at offering his own praise. Tenderly he asks, “Younger brother, praise the sea!”

Can the younger brother do so? There he goes to the temple of Ishta Siddhi Vinayagar. Whether or not he understands the meaning, he is going there to offer praise:

“Mouse-mounted one,
enchanting one, hail...”

In the rare book *The Smile of Beauty*, our dear Bharathidasan paints the loveliness of nature. To feel that sweetness fully, the “younger brother” must obtain the time, inclination and means to see natural scenes. Only then can he understand that poetry emerges not through the charity of rhyme and alliteration, nor merely by weaving words together, but as the consequence of joy imparted by nature. He will understand not only the poem’s greatness but its life. Today, for this reason, countless poets’ portrayals of nature remain unable to yield their full benefit.

I called natural beauty a lovely young widow. Our poets are that widow’s companions and nurses. They describe her beauty and worth, and continue describing them. But when will the outlook of the country’s people change so that they seek out such natural loveliness?

Most of the country’s people have not seen that captivating woman called nature. The poet saw her. Where? Everywhere. Here is what our poet says:

“In the young sun of morning I saw her;
across the sea, in the flood of light, I saw her.
In that grove, in flowers, upon their tender shoots,
wherever I touched, she struck my eye.

At evening, in the west’s softening
ruby flame, she was present. Along the banyan road,
in the flock of parrots upon every branch,
the woman called Beauty gave me poetry.”

The morning sun, the sea, the grove, flower and shoot, the moon, the banyan avenue and the lovely parrot: he saw them all, and in every one he saw her. She gave him poetry. He received it, delighted in it and tells us of it. What of us? Shall we at least now try to see her, or do we still have a few pilgrimages left to complete?

12 January 1947

## Translator's Notes

The essay contrasts pilgrimage culture with attentive enjoyment of nature and discusses Bharathidasan’s *Azhagin Sirippu* (*The Smile of Beauty*). Festival names and devotional phrases are retained or translated contextually. The author’s young-widow metaphor is preserved as a historical feature of the source. One damaged numerical phrase describing how widespread the pilgrims’ attitude was remains unresolved, so the English does not invent a percentage.

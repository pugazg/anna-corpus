''நான் சினிமாவுக்கு வாடான்னு கூப்பிட்டேன். வந்தியோ? இப்ப

போடா!"

சிறு குழந்தைகளின், சில்லரைச் சச்சரவுகளின் போது, சிந்தப்படும்
இந்தச் சொற்கள் சாதாரணமானவைதான்! ''சிறுசுகள் வயதாகிவிட்டால்
இப்படியெல்லாமா, விளையாடப் போகிறதுகள்'' என்று காட்சியைக்
காண்போர், பேசிக்கொள்வர், சிறு விஷயம், என்ற அலட்சிய
மனோபாவத்தோடு. ்

கொஞ்சம் தாராள மனதுள்ளவர்கள் அருகிலிருந்தால், ''ஏய்'
கொடுடா - உன்னைப்போல் அதுவும் குழந்தைதானே. கொடேன்!'' என்று,
அதட்டுவர்.

பெரிய மனிதத்தன்மை - குழந்தைகளிடம் காணமுடியாது என்பதால்,
அலட்சியமும்; குழந்தையின் மனத்தில், தாராள மனோபாவம் ஏற்பட
வேண்டும் என்ற ஆசையால் அதட்டுதலும், அதுபோன்ற நேரங்களில், :
உருவாகும்.

ஆனால், குழந்தைப் புத்தி, தெரியாத்தனம் என்று சிறுவர்களின் '
செயலைச் 'சிறுபிள்ளைத்தனம்' என்று கருதுவோரின் அன்றாட
வாழ்க்கையைக் கவனித்தால் ருசிகரமான நிகழ்ச்சிகள் கிடைக்கும்!

“ரொம்ப யோக்கியம் போல வந்துட்டீயே! நமக்குத்தான் நிலம்
நம்புங்க. எது எப்படியானாலும் உங்களுக்கே முடிச்சுவைக்கிறேங்க.
அப்படி இப்படின்னு அளந்துவிட்டுப் பிறகு பெருமாள் செட்டியாருக்கு
நிலத்தை வாங்கித்தந்த 'பெரிய மனுஷருல்லே இவரு! கேட்க வந்துட்டாரு.
. உதவி வேணுமாம். உதவி! போய்யா - அவன்கிட்டேயே போம்.
செய்வான். ''

நிலத்தை வாங்கித் தராததோடு, இன்னொருவனுக்கு முடித்து
வைத்தான் என்பதால் மிராசுதார் இரைந்தார்!

இரண்டு நிகழ்ச்சியிலும் ஆளும் உருவமும்தான் மாற்றமே ஒழிய -
தீரம் ஒன்றுதான்.

தான் சொன்னபடி கேட்கவில்லை என்பதால் கோபம் வந்தது
குழந்தைக்கு. 'தனக்கு நிலம் சேரும் ' என்று எதிர்பார்த்துக் கிடந்து,
கடைசியில் கைநழுவிவிடும்போல் தோன்றியதும் எரிச்சல் வந்தது,
நிலப்பிரபுவுக்கு.

மிட்டாய் தர மறுத்தபோது 'குழந்தைச் செயல்' என்று கூறிய பிரபு,
தீன் சகா தனக்கு நிலத்தை முடித்துத் தரவில்லை என்றதும் சீறினார் -
சினத்தார்.

சீற்றமும் சினமும் அவருக்கு உருவாகியதன் காரணம், தனது சுய
வாழ்வு பாதிக்கப்படுகிறதே, என்ற ஆத்திரம்தான்.

நிலம் கிடைத்தால் - வளம் பெருகும்! - இது அவர் நினைத்தது.

நினைத்தபடி, நடக்கவில்லை. அவரது சுயநலம் பாதிக்கப்பட்டது
எனவே, சீறினார்.

## English Translation

“I asked you to come to the cinema. Did you come? Yet now you ask me for sweets. Do you think I will give you any? Go away!”

Words like these, spilled during the petty quarrels of small children, are perfectly ordinary. Those watching the scene remark dismissively, “Will these little ones play like this when they grow older?”

If someone a little more generous is nearby, he scolds the child: “Hey, give him some! He is a child just like you. Go on, give it.”

Such occasions produce indifference because great humanity cannot be expected from children, and rebuke because one wishes to cultivate generosity in a child's mind.

But if we observe the daily lives of those who dismiss children's acts as “childishness,” born of a child's mind and ignorance, we encounter some interesting scenes.

“You have come here as though you were very honourable! You kept telling me, ‘The land is certainly ours. Whatever happens, I shall arrange it for you.’ Then, after all that talk, you bought it for Perumal Chettiar. And now this ‘great man’ has come asking me for help. Help indeed! Go away. Go to him; he will help you.”

Or this:

“Why, man? I gave you hundreds whenever you asked, and in the end you simply spread your empty hands. If you had been honest and told me at the outset that I could not have that woman, what would have happened? How much money and time have been wasted! You have all become corrupt; you have forgotten gratitude!”

We can see and hear such scenes among “grown men.”

The boy withheld sweets because the other child did not accompany him to the cinema.

The mirasdar roared because a man not only failed to secure land for him but arranged it for somebody else.

Only the persons and their forms differ between the two events; the underlying character is the same.

The child became angry because the other did not do as he asked. The landlord waited expecting that the land would become his, then grew irritated when it appeared to slip from his grasp.

The lord who called the refusal to share sweets “a child's act” raged when his associate failed to secure the land for him.

His rage and anger arose from indignation that his own welfare was being affected.

If he obtained the land, his wealth would increase. That was his thought.

Events did not proceed as he expected. His self-interest was injured, so he raged.

The “lord” who said the child withheld sweets out of ignorance became the embodiment of anger when his own desire was not fulfilled.

The man who gave the child a sermon on virtue grew angry when “profit” did not come to him through his associate.

There are many people of this type, at many levels. They cannot be called good people. It would be neither fitting nor proper to praise as large-hearted those who preach goodness in one place and become angry elsewhere because their own prosperous life has been obstructed.

Yet the strange condition of society has made it a cage for just such unworthy insects.

People with a disposition to repay injury with injury and alter their position to suit the occasion are the ones found in abundance.

Only a few figures in society embrace with love those who have harmed them, saying: “Let it go. Think about it yourself. Was it right to promise me the land and give it to another? Very well, never mind. Do you need paddy? How many sacks? Shall I have it brought to your house?” Such people are like lamps hidden inside a pot.

Those who parade in showy light possess calculating minds. Put briefly, that very cunning enables them to move about amid dazzling light.

These glittering figures generally belong to wealthy lineages. The loving heart and supportive speech found in a poor labourer who works until his back breaks cannot be found in a seven-storey mansion.

The reason is that the poor person expects nothing through others; he trusts himself.

The lord lives through others. Deceiving them is his daily occupation.

Through its actions, America is now teaching the world this valuable lesson.

“Do you know how terribly small countries suffer? Merely seeing the fate of those shattered by the World War brings tears to one's eyes. We cannot bear to see their people's anguish. Relieving it and lighting the lamp of prosperity throughout those lands is a good deed. America is going to do precisely that.” America was the great nation that beat its chest and spoke in different ways every day.

During the closing stage of the conflict between Britain and India, and while the Jewish-Arab conflict was growing, America behaved on many occasions like the lord who witnessed the child's act.

America proclaimed to the skies that its purpose was to aid poor countries and lift backward nations. It prepared schemes such as the Marshall Plan and accordingly provided aid and money to many countries.

Many nations were enchanted by these arrangements. They sang hymns of praise to America as the poor man's partner. Many countries became caught in the alluring net.

Countries afflicted by poverty and the ruin caused by war looked up toward America.

Garlands of verse about Truman, devotional songs about American goodwill and explanations of the benefits the Marshall Plan would produce began to appear.

The scent of America spread through many lands.

American influence expanded with particular speed in newly independent countries such as India, Burma and Pakistan, which struggled without sufficient ability or resources to govern themselves.

Under a programme called “aid,” America was drawing large countries into its anti-Russian camp.

Seeing this, people like us described America as a smiling spider.

We warned against being deceived by its enchanting web.

We explained that it was a crooked design, that treating it as a true friend would yield no benefit and would instead produce grave harm.

Nationalist friends and journals, lost in chanting Truman's names, publicizing the honour America had shown Vijayalakshmi, and singing praise of Nehru's visit, laughed derisively at us.

They clamoured that we were jealous of the respect shown to Nehru.

“America is the poor man's partner; Russia is a dictatorship that breaks people's backs. We must therefore not lean toward Russia.” Our nationalist journals wrote editorials of this kind.

“America is a democracy. Russia is a dictatorship.” Famous leaders preached in this fashion.

They cried, “There come tens of millions of rupees! Here the machinery has already arrived!”

Conches sounded with slogans such as “We shall eradicate communism; we shall not allow the Russian scent into the country.”

Treating common ownership as though it were Russia's private property that must not show its head anywhere else, they spoke heatedly against Russia and hailed America as a friend in adversity.

We grieved on hearing their cries. We warned that impatient enthusiasm was making them fall into the capitalist camp.

We said that America's promise and appearance of rendering good aid were motivated by self-interest. America was a seductive enchantress, a treacherous net that displayed affection while drawing its victim toward a pit.

An incident has now emerged to explain matters to the friends who mocked us for saying so.

Rather than an “incident,” it might more fittingly be called an episode in which India's honour was stripped away.

About a month ago, Nehru's government, struggling to cope with India's food crisis, sent an appeal to America:

“India is in an extremely grave condition. Famine and misery are subjecting it to unbearable suffering. Please therefore send two million tons from the heaps of wheat available there.”

It issued an appeal moving enough to melt stone.

India did not ask America to give the wheat free of charge.

Nehru's government explained and emphasized in its request: “We shall pay the price of 950 million rupees for this wheat. If you allow instalments, we shall settle the amount little by little. In our present condition we cannot pay 250 million immediately.”

It is true that America possesses surplus wheat.

It is also true that America has the resources to provide it without taking cash in hand.

America could even give India the requested wheat as a gift.

Nine hundred and fifty million rupees is no great sum to that gold-producing country. Yet no answer of any kind has arrived.

Not only has there been no answer; they are reportedly debating whether India's request even deserves consideration.

India pleads pitifully that it has no food.

America coolly deliberates: should we help, and is it necessary?

Friends who praised America as a friend in adversity and a cherished companion now stand bewildered.

America's conduct has created a sensation throughout the world.

“How cordial India and America were!”

“How strange! Has America really not sent wheat to India?”

“It is impossible to believe.”

The nations of the world speak in many such ways.

Even the Indian government has been touched by some bewilderment and disgust.

Why has America, which proclaimed that its mission was relieving the distress of poor countries, failed to send wheat even after hearing people's cry of hunger? Many suspicions have begun to circulate.

America refused aid to India, which cried “Hunger! Hunger!” and begged for some of its available wheat. Yet it has lent South Africa eighty million dollars, approximately 3.8 billion rupees.

America hesitates to send wheat worth 950 million rupees, but has given South Africa 3.8 billion.

When we ask why it refused India while giving abundant money to South Africa at the same time, we recall the character of the grown men mentioned at the beginning: the landlord who raged as soon as his self-interest was harmed.

The United Nations is the peace organization created after the war. Five years have passed since it came into existence.

Throughout those five years it has served as an arena in which the Russian and American camps test their strength. Until now, America has won those trials and accomplished whatever it wished. No resolution introduced by America had failed to pass.

Recently, however, America introduced a resolution concerning China and the Korean question.

It sought to declare Communist China, which was involved in the Korean War, an aggressor nation, take action against it and preserve the unity of non-communist countries.

India did not support the resolution.

India had declared that the Korean question should be resolved in another manner.

America's preparation was first to proclaim Communist China an aggressor and then launch the weapons of war against it.

Believing this course would lead to a third world war, India did not support the resolution and placed an obstacle in its path.

That was the position at the United Nations.

America expected that at least the Commonwealth Conference in London would provide a way to achieve its purpose.

The conference had been convened to decide how to create a powerful united front against Russia. Britain assembled it with that very intention. But the design was not fulfilled. There too India spoke only as a messenger of peace.

Anger has therefore begun to arise in America's mind.

America had relied chiefly upon two Asian countries in its effort to oppose and eradicate communism: China and India.

But China became a communist country. America therefore placed great reliance upon India for its anti-communist purpose. It tempted India with promises of giving this and that and acted like a friend in adversity.

America dislikes India's present conduct in opposing the Korean resolution and supporting China, because it unsettles that expectation.

India's purpose in opposing the Korean resolution was simply to prevent conditions for a third world war from developing.

Prime Minister Nehru now possesses a deep concern that war must be avoided and peace secured. Whatever the cause of that concern, no one will fail to praise him for it.

Countries immersed in suffering naturally wish to prevent another war. American capitalism, however, is interested in creating war. It is intensely occupied in recruiting people and making preparations for it.

Its flattering promises to help countries such as India and provide backward nations with the money they need arose from its desire to prepare all those countries as its supporters and Russia's enemies.

That is why it praised and honoured countries such as India, invited Nehru, held banquets for him and made other arrangements.

India's decision to begin acting according to its own judgment has now shocked America.

America is angry that India has not obeyed its word.

Not only the American government, but even American newspapers that praised India until yesterday have begun to revile it.

Having trapped other countries in its camp under the name of “financial aid,” America now threatens them with that same money missile.

Its hesitation to provide wheat is a demonstration of that threat.

What is now taking place is a warning episode directed at other nations: “If you obey me, I will do whatever you desire. If not, I shall give you nothing.”

America has given South Africa 3.8 billion rupees as if to say: “Look here! You defied me, so I told you ‘No.’ South Africa obeyed me and supported the Korean resolution. Therefore I gave it money and helped it.”

Suppressing the voices of the poor with the weapon of money is the authoritarian nature of capitalists.

This wheat incident demonstrates that capitalist tendency very clearly.

India, which lies immersed in capitalism's seductive smile, must understand this lesson. Only then will freedom endure and a life without affliction flourish.

Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 4 February 1951.

## Translator's Notes

The title `பண பாணம்` is a compact play on money (`பணம்`) and an arrow or projectile (`பாணம்`): financial aid wielded as a coercive weapon. “The Money Missile” preserves that image.

The OCR phrase requesting wheat reads `இருபது இலட்சத்துடன்`, evidently missing or corrupting its unit. The English uses “two million tons” from the surrounding price and historical context, but this quantity should be verified against the scan or contemporary record before being treated as definitive.

The article reflects its publication date and political interpretation of the Korean War, the United Nations vote, American aid and South Africa. Historical claims are translated as the author's argument, not independently endorsed or modernized.

## English Translation

"They reviled him; he did not tire! They attacked him; he did not falter!"

"The hardships I endured were no small thing. I have shed both blood and tears. Anxiety within the family, trouble among those around me, a shower of abuse calling me a 'good-for-nothing' whenever I walked down the street, and the townspeople's mockery: 'Look, there he goes!' I bore all of it. I laboured on without pause or obstruction, telling myself, 'My duty is to lie here carrying out my work.' I wore myself away; I died. But my ideas did not die. They continue to sprout and flourish."

K. K. Neelamegam, chairman of the reception committee for the Thanjavur District Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Conference, proudly said that the portrait of Mayavaram C. Natarajan, painted as though it were uttering these words, would be displayed in the conference pavilion.

"Is that all? We have named the conference pavilion after elder brother Natarajan. Our district's other eye, a great hero who could have savoured a life as sweet as honey but ran in search of water across a parched desert and died there, was the fearless and precious warrior elder brother Alagiri. The conference stage bears his name. We shall display his portrait too. Do you know how splendid it will be?" said Mayavaram Palanisamy and Kumbakonam Parangusam, the conference secretaries who stood nearby.

Mayavaram Natarajan.

The great warrior Alagiri.

Anyone who knows the history of the Self-Respect Movement will shed tears merely on hearing the names of these two warriors for the ideal. The suffering they endured to lay a path for rationalist work was no small thing. The storms and smoke through which they had to pass in order to build the fortress of knowledge were not slight. Our conference is to be held in the district that gave such great heroes to the country.

Then as now, Thanjavur has held a special place in the history of our movement.

It is fertile country that has furnished the nation's work with masters of speech and tireless heroes.

That is why, when we heard descriptions such as, "Do you know what the conference decorations will be like? We have made all these different arrangements for food! An immense pavilion!" our enthusiasm and desire began to dance. We rejoiced at the thought of the kind of warriors for the ideal whom the path of rationalism has gained.

The first Thanjavur District Conference of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, to be held at Mayavaram on the coming 3rd and 4th of March, will reflect our victory. The effort and fervour with which Thanjavur's heroes have undertaken its great work are immense.

The Thanjavur conference meets at a critical moment. The government keeps its eye fixed on our Kazhagam and plots, "When can we pounce? How can we pounce?" The hawk of authority circles overhead, beating its wicked wings. Beneath it, the people writhe like worms from hunger and suffering. The government watching them makes no effort to relieve their distress; it is playing blind man's buff.

The farmer weeps. Those who provide clothing are dying. Labourers sing a song of anguish: "There is no place to live, no house in which to stay." The cultivator rises in the morning, carries his plough to the field, works until evening, and returns after dark, but there is no joy in his life. There is no path by which "distress may end and happiness be attained." They are tormented. The son herds cattle, the daughter gathers dung, and the father guards the crop: this alone keeps turning as their wheel of "occupation"; the fragrance of education has not blossomed in their lives.

The fields bend under their grain, display their golden expanse, and sustain the world. Yet, like the lines of worry upon the brow of the exhausted farmer, they lie cracked in a dreadful condition. Even in Thanjavur, where Mother Kaveri dances in delight, there are forests, dry tracts, and regions without water. Such conditions hang over many parts of Thanjavur district.

In virgin soil that could yield clusters upon clusters of rice grain, many regions lie beginning to turn into wilderness, like rulers with uncultivated minds.

This is a time when the storm is driving away those who seek white fly-whisks; a time when the cry of freedom has begun to chase away the seekers of comfort.

At this beautiful hour, we are going to gather in Thanjavur. We may have crawled and played in Thanjavur many times before, but there is indeed a difference between those occasions and our gathering now.

We are now going to explain our purpose, our duty, and our future properly to the people of this fertile land.

The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is going to show itself to the people of Thanjavur.

It is everyone's duty to take part in this splendid spectacle.

*Dravida Nadu*, 25 February 1951.

## Translator's Notes

“Granary” carries the title's reference to fertile Thanjavur, traditionally identified with rice abundance. The source's contrast between `வெண்சாமரம் தேடிகள்` and `சுகந்தேடிகள்` is retained as “seekers of white fly-whisks” and “seekers of comfort”: the first evokes people pursuing the ceremonial privileges of power. The article's quoted phrase about ending distress is rendered in its elevated register.

## English Translation

Congress members used to say that party politics should not be introduced into municipal councils. Until only a few days ago, those who stood at the forefront of Congress did not fail to emphasise this assertion.

“Councils exist to attend to the development and public health of their respective towns. There is no place there for party disputes. The Legislative Assembly exists to deal with those. The Assembly, not municipal councils, is where the various parties must state and debate their principles,” they said clearly.

Even if people possessed a political purpose, they were told to take off that “coat” when they went to serve in municipal councils. Only then could they work for urban development without likes and dislikes. If town councils became political platforms, unnecessary disputes, partialities and animosities would grow while public benefits diminished. Day after day, division, hostility and resentment would be produced, harming the very purposes for which municipal chambers had been established.

Minister Subramaniam often appeared to understand this truth. He repeatedly argued that party politics should not enter municipal councils or similar small institutions of local government. On one occasion in the Legislative Assembly itself, he said: “I do not like political parties contesting local-body elections. It would be good if all political parties met and agreed not to field anyone in these elections under their respective party names.”

The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam did more than welcome this statement; it gave an assurance. We declared that if, as the minister wished, all political parties reached such a common decision, we were ready to accept it. Everyone who believed party politics should not enter municipal councils and their associated village and town bodies considered and desired such an agreement to be welcome.

It has now become clear, however, that a faction within Congress itself does not desire this. It is also evident that Minister Subramaniam has no influence over that faction.

Others may have considered the minister's statement and wish good and worthy of welcome. But those in Congress who pass their time targeting elections, act as stage-managers and ply their trade during them, seek a position for themselves here and there, conduct political hunts, and keep the necessary flow of money in circulation did not like his proposal. Minister Subramaniam apparently lacks the “power” to compel such a faction to agree.

Congress does not regard Comrade Subramaniam as a lord who formulates policy. The position granted to him there is merely that of a clerk who takes notes.

Even if our beloved friend longs to step inside the fortress where policy is made, his routes are blocked by hunters and miraculous political remedies.

His trumpet call in the Assembly was blown into deaf ears. The powerful men who make Congress dance have decided to contest the coming municipal elections explicitly in the party's name.

There is no indication that Minister Subramaniam opposed or objected to the Tamil Nadu Congress reaching this decision. One wonders whether he said nothing because he thought, “Who will listen if I speak?” Congress has decisively resolved to contest the municipal elections and has begun beating the election drum.

Although the DMK has long argued that party politics should not enter municipal councils, Congress's conduct has compelled it to reconsider. Do not autocracy and one-party rule injure the dignity of democracy? Those who value democracy have a duty to prevent that condition from taking shape in local-government institutions too.

Ask why Congress has reached this decision and the answer comes easily. The chief reason must be to provide some boon of office to those who have received “nothing” in places such as the Legislative Assembly.

Many prominent figures in the towns are useful to Congress in various ways, and it does not wish to make them dissatisfied. It wants to use these bodies to draw in comfortable and wealthy people in each locality.

Emissaries have cast their nets and begun searching for such men. Congress gentlemen who spend their lives moving from one election to another have begun preparing for the hunt. The custom of offering ceremonial betel to the wealthy has commenced. This net of offices, designed to keep aside both the person who laboured and the old Congress worker, is being spread vigorously in every town.

As the party in power, Congress can do this skilfully. It can easily obtain the services of wealthy gentlemen adept at accomplishing clever business with the government. The DMK not only lacks this opportunity; it does not desire it.

Anyone can become a Congress candidate!

A person may never have touched khadi, may have run a liquor shop, may possess wealth, may be a reactionary, may have amassed predatory profit on the black market, or may be an enemy of society. Anyone with the opportunity and means to spend money can become a Congress candidate.

The DMK is not a movement that operates for such a purpose. It recognises worthy people and volunteers who labour for the public, using the quality of their work as its measure. It will not hold an auction promising places to those who give or spend money. The DMK will assess merit and service alone.

When Congress announced that it would conduct these elections on a party basis, enthusiastic comrades in the DMK said that we too must participate in the same fashion.

If we do not, Congress will establish one-party rule in municipal councils as well. They will become places for wealthy notables and halls for those who spend money.

Either by standing in the elections ourselves or by supporting those prepared to compete against Congress, we must place a nose-rope upon Congress autocracy.

When such views came from our comrades, our General Secretary convened prominent members and comrades from other towns to consult about the matter. Valuable advice was exchanged at the meeting.

We are not unaware that money does the playing and caste runs wild in municipal elections. Our comrades know that certain people always reserve a few seats in each town as their own, and that there are places where merely invoking caste is enough to “win.”

In our country, municipal elections have generally remained the business of a few rich men. Even today, half a dozen wealthy families in a town continue to divide its “destiny” among themselves.

These are not like Assembly elections, where people vote after hearing explanations of policy. In each ward there are only a limited number of voters. People of means can still believe that by spending a few thousand more and bargaining at one or two rupees per vote they can secure victory.

The comrades who advised the General Secretary that the DMK should participate knew all these difficulties. They also understood what further frauds “great men” would commit. Nevertheless, they argued that we must do what a democratic organisation is obliged to do.

Seeing their enthusiasm, the General Secretary held another consultation to decide what course should be taken. The comrades' concerns were discussed thoroughly and clearly. As a result, the General Secretary has established separate committees to report the conditions in each locality and provide appropriate advice. These committees will tell headquarters whom to field and where.

The time has come for everyone who must teach Congress a lesson to perform two tasks. Kazhagam comrades in every town must regard this as an opportunity to protect our cause and the growth of democracy.

It is better to fight honestly and lose than to capture a few places at the cost of party principle. Ours is a growing movement. Our life is unlike that of those in Congress. Today's Congress is in a condition where it must somehow demonstrate victory in fields like this merely to conceal its weakness. In that agitation it says, “Anyone will do; if he will spend money, make him a Congress candidate.”

An election is a democratic duty, but elections alone are not important to us. Service, and the great passion to serve the people, are all the wealth we require. We are not in a position where we consider the friendship of unworthy people, undesirable alliances, or alarming opportunistic ties necessary for electoral victory.

We ask every Kazhagam comrade in every town to keep this in mind. Duty and dignity are what matter.

(1 February 1959)

## English Translation

Comrade M. Singaravelar, the sculptor of thought, has died. The hero whose very breath was his ideal has passed away. The first Communist of the Indian subcontinent is gone.

There were three Indian members of the Third International, and he was one of them. Yet khadi-jacketed nationalists and readers of Karl Marx abound who ask, “Did such a person exist?”

Before Communist government had become firmly established in the Soviet Union, a revolutionary hero was already walking along the Madras shore at Mylapore. Waves of anger rose in his heart at the world's cruelties. Driven by them, he presented a doctrine nobody here had heard before, blending the philosophy of common ownership with revolutionary fire. Those people do not know that this man was Comrade M. Singaravelar.

Even during the several years in which he was prominent in national and labour movements, he did not receive his proper place, nor were his knowledge and ability measured. At a time when locusts and chameleons received praise, the people forgot the revolutionary tiger, and were made to forget him.

If assessed by his talent, knowledge, power and spirit of sacrifice, Singaravelar must be placed in the line of Lenin, Trotsky and Saklatvala. But a people who read and praise both the *Kanda Purana* and the “Gandhi Purana” treated this peerless revolutionary as an ordinary man. With time, one may say that everyone except a few idealists forgot even his name.

Singaravelar was the first hero struck by imperialism, but his fame dimmed because he did not wear the sacred thread. There were no songs of praise or portrait-unveiling ceremonies for him, nor will there be. He was M. Singaravelu Chettiar of the Parathavar fishing community, a lord of the coastal landscape. Had he been Singaravel Sharma, Mylapore would have demanded that his statue be installed in Moscow.

Although Singaravelar has died, his death did not come in an unexpected or startling fashion. Nor would he have been distressed at death by doubt that his labour had gone in vain. He was eighty-four when he died on 11 February 1946, and his eyes closed only after seeing his two foundational ideals, Self-Respect and socialism, rise and grow.

One concern may have remained: why should Self-Respect and socialism exist as separate parties? Was that proper, and would it scatter the strength of the force? Apart from that, we grieve that his name faded, but we do not imagine he thought or yearned over it.

That fearless heart could receive no honour in the bourgeois world. It received none, and that itself is an excellent sign that the quality of his metal never diminished.

Among the vanguard who rose against the iron grip of imperialism upon the Indian subcontinent, Singaravelar was the first hero.

The Sepoy Rebellion against British rule occurred in 1857. When Singaravelar was a child, his family and townspeople must have told him stories of those events, whether for amusement, warning or some other purpose. He would have heard that soldiers resisted the East India Company and that white men shot the resisters.

He did not grow up hearing, as people of our generation did, about the Governor-General's benevolence, Gokhale's clarity or the beauty of an initial reform. We were born when the country had become deeply soaked in servitude. He was born when the armed attack upon British rule was still told as a children's story. Born in 1862, five years after the rebellion was suppressed, he entered a world where the storm had passed but fallen trees still lay across the road.

For Singaravelar, 1857 continued until the end. His speech always attacked imperialism, capitalism, varnashrama and superstition. Not merely his speech but his very gaze had that character.

Mylapore contained many jewels of moderation, genteel nationalists and wealthy men. Had Singaravelar slipped, he could have settled among any of them. He studied law and became an advocate, but to what end? Declaring it a mark of slavery that a foreign ruler should frame the law while we studied it and argued beneath it, he set fire to his lawyer's gown. Refusing to enter the court, he argued the people's case with skill in the public forum.

He knew German, French and Russian. In India, the foremost place in the first rank must be given to him for studying and understanding scientific knowledge and Communist thought, and then using that learning to make others understand them.

His reading never ceased. He laboured intensely to discover cause and effect behind every problem that brought pain to his heart. He disliked grazing only upon the tips of grass. Attempts to plaster over problems made him angry, and he condemned movements that bewildered people with empty spectacle.

Even if he eventually walked alone along the shore, he considered fellowship with principle sufficient. In his final years that old man stood almost alone. Merely contemplating the firm and revolutionary ideas he carried inspires wonder.

Ask him, “Do ghosts, spirits and demons exist?” Beginning with when those words entered usage, he would explain all the way to the conclusion that the true ghost today is the person who lives by another's labour. In the way it dispelled doubts, his mind was an excellent dictionary.

The death of such a person became an ordinary item of news. This country has countless newspapers, and supposedly idealists hold pens in every one of them.

Nobody will deny that he was a brave and resolute man. Agitations were a moonlight meal to him, and imprisonment natural. He feared neither opposition nor imperial attack, and he was not one who entered prison merely as part of a crowd.

People say the distinction of being first arrested in the name of nationalism belongs to Lokmanya Tilak. Yet British imperialism attacked Singaravelar before it touched Tilak. Nationalist noise concealed this fact. In the Kanpur Communist case, British imperialism first arrested Comrade Singaravelar and Maulana Hasrat Mohani on the charge of being Communists.

Singaravelar also deserves the distinction of first teaching the country that a class called “workers” existed. As labour agitation began in England, he began the same kind of agitation here.

Seeing India bent like a hunchback, he identified that its crooked spine forced it to walk stooped and began working to awaken labour. He seethed at the oppression of workers in the names of religion and politics and through superstition.

He learned from railway labour struggles that Annie Besant and her disciples obstructed a settlement between railway authorities and workers and opposed even the appeals by which he and his comrades sought escape from severe sentences. He also saw that the false propaganda of the country's newspaper caste during the railway strike obstructed the awakening of labour.

That experience changed his course. He concluded that, before workers could be freed from white and Indian capitalists, sham labour leaders and newspapers that served as the capitalists' true agents had to disappear. This was why he moved from the labour movement toward the Self-Respect Movement.

Singaravelar was an outstanding non-cooperator. Even while earning a good living, he was among the first Madras lawyers to trust Gandhism and abandon legal practice. Unlike lawyers who left the courts at Congress's command and later returned, he remained a genuine non-cooperator.

One may count four great public agitations in Madras: the Buckingham and Carnatic Mills strike, the boycott of the Prince of Wales, the boycott of the Simon Commission and the boycott of the Duke of Connaught. Singaravelar participated in the first three.

During the same period in America, two Communist comrades, Sacco and Vanzetti, were sentenced in a political case and executed by electricity. Singaravelar arranged a vast public meeting. The Presidency Government, alarmed, deployed its police force. Many who had initially agreed to help and speak stayed away after seeing the police display.

Disappointed, Singaravelar came alone toward People's Park. He did not become afraid merely because all his friends had failed to appear. He had expected no substantial meeting after such intimidation. Instead, he saw a huge gathering, a person singing a socialist song in its midst, and two or three young men on the platform.

Smiling slightly at this unexpected event, he approached the stage. The people upon it were new to him, yet he was astonished to find them helping with the task he had undertaken that day. He praised the Madras public for disregarding police threats, gathering in sympathy with two American workers cruelly killed, and showing support for their doctrine.

At this meeting the late Singaravelar's attention turned toward the Self-Respect Movement. Comrade Pattukkottai Azhagarsami said:

> Two workers born respectively in a shoemaking community and a fishing community have been punished by the American Government. Many such events may have to occur before the working class awakens. Just as the Italian fisherman-worker Vanzetti died, I wish to see our true labour leader Singaravelar punished by this government as the Vanzetti of our country, and to witness the gathering that would follow.

That one statement made Singaravelar a Self-Respecter.

The distinction of explaining socialism in a way suited to the people of this country and making the economic theory of Marxism understandable to ordinary people belongs here to only two men. A great difference remains between the extent to which people in other provinces understand Communism and the understanding among this Presidency's people, especially its labour movement.

Periyar E. V. Ramasamy and Comrade Singaravelar were the two who absorbed Marxism completely and explained it through writing and speech so anyone could understand. Singaravelar laboured greatly for the growth of the Self-Respect Movement. No Self-Respecter will forget his work.

His noble ideal was a happy life for all people. Whatever prevented that result, he longed to smash it to dust.

Without fearing anyone, he declared imperialism and religious dogma twin paralytic diseases of society. Those alarmed by his radicalism called him an atheist. He accepted the name and conducted an Atheists' Conference in Madras, something nobody else in India had done.

To the class that placed responsibility upon religion and God and squeezed ordinary people without fear of sin or blame, that great hero asked:

> Who is responsible for the suffering of living beings in the world? Who commanded the tiger to seize and eat the cow, and the snake to seize and eat the frog? What scene could be more cruel? How greatly do disease, poverty, famine, storm, flood and other natural events torment humanity?
>
> Can these cruelties be forgotten merely by calling God compassionate, universal protector, friend in danger and loving one? Was it not in God's name that Jains were impaled? Was it not in God's name that countless women were beaten and killed as demon-possessed? Was it not in the name of a deity that Christians and Muslims died by the millions during five hundred years of cruel war? How many temples and buildings collapsed, and how many countries and cities were destroyed, in God's name?

Singaravelar asked such questions plainly, leaving his opponent struggling for an answer.

My final sight of him was itself an incident revealing his rare character. Two old men, Periyar E. V. Ramasamy and Comrade Singaravelar, sat upon the same platform at St Mary's Hall in Madras on 20 June 1943. It was Untouchability Abolition Day.

Singaravelar appeared with a weakened body, unsteady gait and grey head. The moment he heard that it was a day against untouchability, he forgot even the infirmity of age and came. That day I saw for the last time the revolutionary hero who struggled for the Self-Respect and well-being of working people.

I later heard that he had died. I was not shocked, but I stood bewildered, wondering whether another hero like him would ever appear.

Let us offer our respects to the departed hero. Let everyone who honours him resolve to preserve the path he laid down without corruption and to labour so that his ideal of people's rule may blossom.

*Dravida Nadu*, 24 February 1946

## English Translation

Riding in a motorcar manufactured and sent by America while praising the age of the Agamas with cries of "Ah! Wonderful!" Participating in the structure of the new world to the extent of sending ambassadors abroad, while at the same time praising the Vedic age!

Whenever we insist that this course is wrong, incompatible with reason, and lacking in honesty, it is customary for orthodox bundles and Mannarsamis to become tremendously angry.

Yet is it not true? Why enjoy modern civilisation while repeatedly praising the old order? Could one not choose one of the two? Ordinary people refuse to consider this, and tricksters turn them into dupes.

Whenever we condemn the practice of riding in a Buick while campaigning for the ancient way of life as resembling a man praising Madhavi's beauty to Kannagi, men of the water pot and men of the earrings crow and threaten: "You cruel man! You depraved thinker! Watch what suffering we shall inflict upon you."

## Sir C. V. Raman

He is a world-renowned scientist and a Brahmin. But he is not a fellow who goes to the temple tank in search of sacred offerings. He is not one of those useless people who boast that the secrets of the universe lie in the Vedas, the truth of the world in the Upanishads, and that the Atharvana contains many verses providing the basis for constructing wondrous devices the whole world would admire, and then wander away after eating a sweet rice ball.

He conducted scientific investigations that astonished even Westerners, using new knowledge and scientific intelligence, not the glory of sacred ash, not by chanting the thousand names, and not by sprinkling tulsi water.

The knowledge and fame he gained were not obtained before the flame of a temple torch or burning camphor. Seated in a laboratory before the long flame of a spirit lamp, he conducted research, discovered many truths, announced them to the world, and even presented the world with certain new truths.

He discovered rare facts while seated in a laboratory fitted with glass tubes, not before a sacrificial fire after gathering ritual twigs. Nor did he discover the truths praised by the world by mixing soil from holy places such as Haridwar, Kashi and Kanchi into sacred waters such as the Ganga and Yamuna. He filled glass tubes with liquids of many colours prepared by scientific methods, conducted research, and found rare truths.

He has travelled through many countries. His fame has reached even those he has not visited.

Such a scholar says: "I am not a politician. It is my habit to state plainly whatever is in my mind. I cannot accept as rational the practice of riding in a brand-new Buick manufactured and sent by America while speaking in praise of a desire to see ancient civilisation and its way of life restored."

The great scholar Raman spoke in this manner in Bangalore last week.

He condemns this course as unintelligent. We are delighted to see Sir C. V. Raman condemn those who seek out and enjoy the benefits of modern civilisation while talking grandly of antiquity, ancient civilisation and ancestral tradition.

Only when scholars like him condemn it frequently, firmly, clearly and fearlessly will the "new-moon days and inauspicious hours" of this country acquire sensitivity.

When we discuss the same issue fully and clearly, it is easy to heap blame upon us and leap at us in fury, abusing us as communalists, haters of Brahmins and atheists. But what abuse can they direct at Sir C. V. Raman? He is himself a Brahmin.

He strikes a mighty blow upon the wooden heads of the orthodox and advises them: "Conduct yourself rationally. Do not continue speaking grandly about antiquity." He mocks people who enjoy modernity while remaining slaves to the past.

"I am not one of those who say the country should return to the old order," Sir C. V. Raman clearly declared at that meeting.

Sir C. V. Raman has won fame by discovering many scientific truths. Yet this discovery is as important and necessary as his scientific discoveries: there are people in this country who bear the title of intellectual, use Western scientific methods, and at the same time babble about ancestral greatness.

How many of our people can understand the excellence and benefit of the rare discoveries made by people like Sir C. V. Raman? Ordinary people marvel at scientific discoveries, but in the very next moment forget the distinction of science and return to thinking about the greatness of neem leaves, the claim that worms do not enter cow dung when it is shaped into Pillaiyar, and the religious duty to pour milk into a snake mound.

Before religion and magic, the greatness of science is made to look utterly ordinary.

Those who create this attitude among ordinary people are precisely the "holy men" whom Sir C. V. Raman condemned: people who ride in Buicks while praising the Puranic age.

Like rats entering a granary and poisonous insects fastening upon a green crop, these "lovers of two spouses" cause harm.

If Sir C. V. Raman were to investigate for a moment the damage they produce, he would be astonished. He would see that while the country's condition rises on one side through scientific development, it decays on the other through infatuation with antiquity.

Suppose a wicked thinker obtained employment in his laboratory, received wages, pretended to serve him, but without his knowledge spoke disparagingly of his very capacity for research and corrupted the institution from within. How much anguish would Sir C. V. Raman feel?

The same is true of those who never forgo even a drop of present-day convenience or the opportunities science provides, yet never cease praising ancient greatness.

Nor are they limited to those who, as the scientist said, ride in Buicks to preach the ancient age. This country contains many such strange people: those who hang a picture of Rama beside the radio and keep the almanac next to an atlas.

If they truly possessed attachment, affection, respect and faith towards antiquity and ancient civilisation, should they not shrink in shame from obtaining, enjoying and benefiting from these civilised, modern scientific conveniences? Yet we see no shame.

They put on spectacles to read the *Ramayana* composed by sage Valmiki. They board a train to make a pilgrimage to Rameswaram. They receive an injection for cholera contracted after attending the Garuda festival. They undergo an operation to cure an illness caused by the shock of witnessing the Ani Thirumanjanam festival. What are we to call their conduct?

People like Sir C. V. Raman must fearlessly expose this condition and condemn their behaviour. Then clarity will arise in the country.

To obtain his scientific knowledge and explain what he learned, Sir C. V. Raman has travelled, and continues to travel, to London, Paris, Berlin, Vienna, Rome, New York and many other places. But if the country is to receive and fully benefit from the fruits of the research made possible by his subtle intelligence, he must also visit Erode from time to time. We mean that he must undertake the work of rationalist propaganda.

(*Dravida Nadu*, 5 December 1948)

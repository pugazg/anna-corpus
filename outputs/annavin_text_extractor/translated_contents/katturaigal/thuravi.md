## English Translation

Though he was a farmer, the father wanted to make his son a lawyer whom the whole town would praise. Without regard for the expense, he continued sending money for the son's legal studies.

The young man was neither given to idle talk and useless pursuits nor dull-witted. His character earned his teachers' praise, and his legal education proceeded properly.

"He will no longer have to wander through hamlets and villages. My son will go to the bar and become a lawyer. Merchants and officials, lords and ladies, will seek him out. His income will be abundant and his status will rise," the father thought with delight.

But...

Some call it an inner prompting, some mental turmoil, and others delusion. Whatever the cause, the young man who had been studying to become a lawyer suddenly decided to become a mendicant. He entered a monastery.

His friends were astonished. The people of the monastery too questioned and cross-questioned him before admitting him.

Had he become a lawyer, he might have lived in a mansion and travelled in a carriage drawn by handsome horses. A beautiful woman or a lady of rank might have become his companion. With wealth and influence, he might have become a prominent person.

He might have shone as a jewel in many assemblies and perhaps even secured a place at the king's court.

What did the monastery offer? A strictly regulated life, severe austerity, various fasts, and unending worship!

He had to remain celibate, restrain every desire and attachment, and live in the garb of a mendicant.

He had to instruct ordinary people: "Do not sin. Do not forget the Supreme Being. Seek salvation." The young man embraced this life.

He received the prescribed training. In his effort "to receive the Lord's grace," he subjected himself to such discipline that even the senior monks who served as his teachers pitied him.

When the required period and severe training were complete, an auspicious day was fixed for him to become a monk.

His father, who had longed to see his son excel in a court of law, was distressed. Nevertheless, because it was only proper to attend the virtuous ceremony at which his son would become a monk, he arrived with his friends.

The ceremony took place. But it was not the kind of festival where joy, merriment, and revelry dance together.

Its meaning was that a pure life, fit to step into the presence of God, and a sacred journey were beginning.

There was therefore no dancing or singing, no praise or presentation of gifts. Instead, there were hymns to the Supreme Being and instruction on attaining spiritual maturity.

The young monk, deeply absorbed in all this, saw only sorrow, bewilderment, and disappointment on his father's face. He pitied him. How ignorant his father was, he thought, not to understand the excellence of this great blessing, becoming a servant of the Compassionate Lord. Out of pity, the son decided to offer his father wise counsel.

"Father, are you angry at what I have done?" he asked.

The son expected his father to say, "No, my son, no! I am proud to see you undertake the sacred service of spreading God's glory and leading everyone along the path of scripture. Being a merchant, warrior, or lawyer amounts merely to service performed for human beings. Such professions may bring plentiful pay, but where among them is the greatness obtained by serving God? My son, I was dull-witted. I meant to make you a lawyer and send you wandering through the world. You are wise and go in search of grace. Go forth and triumph!"

He expected his father to say this at least for form's sake, or out of regard for the place, which was the ceremonial hall of many eminent monks, or because it was a day filled with sanctity.

But...

Until then, the father had suppressed his anger with immense difficulty. It now burst out as a cry of anguish.

"You dare ask me that too, you learned fool! You have made your parents' hearts burn and destroyed all my hopes! Did I stint myself, save every coin, and send money for your expenses so that you could become a mendicant? You promised me you would become a lawyer and made me believe you. Now you become a monk and ask me to admire your accomplishment! You destroy the truth of the saying that no mantra surpasses a father's word, and announce that you are becoming a 'holy man'! Is the 'gospel' you studied here one that teaches betrayal of your parents?"

Thus he fired an unbroken volley of condemnation.

The son smiled. What else could he do?

"How can this ignorant man, who wanders through fields and forests believing whatever he sees to be the whole spectacle of life, understand that by joining the monastery I am about to perform magnificent service for the redemption of humankind?" he thought.

The father, who had believed his son would become a lawyer, was disappointed. There is nothing surprising in that. Later, the son who believed he would obtain the Lord's grace in the monastery was disappointed too.

That is the surprise!

It took time for this monk to realize that the monastery was a house of cruelty that plunged people into ignorance and trapped them in the hands of the arrogant.

Once he understood this, he did not fear to proclaim it to the world.

Nor did he lack the courage to leave the monastery.

The prospective lawyer became a monk.

Then that monk renounced monasticism. He was Martin Luther. When the moment came to renounce the monastic life, Martin Luther remembered the fiery words his father had spoken on the day he became a "holy man," and tears welled in his eyes.

14 January 1956.

52. நமது பாதை

நாம் தினந்தோறும் மக்களைச் சந்திக்கிறோம்;
அவர்களும் நமது கூட்டங்களுக்குத் தவறாமல் வருகிறார்கள்.
இதன் உண்மைப் பொருள் என்ன?

திராவிட முன்னேற்றக் கழகத்தின் கருத்துக்கள்
மீண்டும் மீண்டும் வந்து கேட்குமாறு மக்களைத்
தூண்டுகின்றன; மக்களும் மீண்டும் மீண்டும் வந்து
கேட்கிறார்கள்.

“திராவிட நாடு திராவிடருக்கே!” என்ற மழலை
மொழியைத் தினம் தவறாமல் நாடு கேட்டுக் கொண்டே
இருக்கின்றது.

## English Translation

We meet the people every day, and they too come unfailingly to our meetings. What is the true meaning of this?

The ideas of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam impel people to return and listen repeatedly, and the people do return and listen again and again.

Day after day the country continues to hear the infant cry, "Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians!"

Some are distressed when they hear this voice we raise; others become angry. Yet, so far as I have examined the matter, neither Congressmen nor those without political affiliation deny that North India exercises domination over the South. They all say, "There certainly is domination", and then prolong the sentence with "But..."

That "but" must gradually turn into "if so", grow into "that is right", develop further into "what shall we do now?", pass through "can you accomplish it?", take form as "the two of us can join together", and finally give birth to the condition, "Here is Dravida Nadu." Our confidence that this condition will arise is what enables us to endure every slander hurled at us by opponents and others.

Those who show hatred towards us today are people who must come to us. We possess the confidence that they will join us.

We believe that all those who prolong their response with "but" will come, if not today then tomorrow, and if not tomorrow then on another day.

The basis of our confidence is that the truth we proclaim has begun to touch many hearts.

We may be ordinary people, but the task we have undertaken is the immense task of liberating a nation.

This great undertaking is not a question of the relationship between us and other parties. It concerns the relationship between us and the people of the country.

Kazhagam comrades must therefore understand this truth and work accordingly.

They must possess four essential qualities. First, the patience to endure anything; second, freedom from anger at anything; third, the ability not to grow weary for any reason; and fourth, the ability not to become distressed by anything.

I say that we need these qualities because, when members of opposing parties devote themselves to abusing us, we must not become enraged. We must instead labour with the thought that our Kazhagam should grow and work for its victory.

Our development will not decline merely because opposing parties insult us.

A movement cannot grow by attacking others. It grows only when it acquires the strength to withstand the attacks of others. Striking back at those who attack is not a sign of strength. Our friends should therefore not cultivate their minds in that fashion.

The task we have undertaken must succeed. We must not abandon it and descend into some other activity.

If ten people stand against us, our purpose should be to work so that two of them join our side the following year. In the year after that, we must bring another four to our side. This is the arithmetic that matters to us.

When we engage in the worthy work of liberating the country, we must be prepared to accept many defeats. The Kazhagam can grow only if it possesses a heart that does not collapse before defeat. No party can develop if, whenever defeat occurs, its members shake out the cloth, throw it over their shoulders, and depart elsewhere.

That is what the leaders of the Justice Party did. Unable to bear defeat, they changed their clothing and joined the Congress.

Today our Kazhagam contains fine young bulls capable of working with a sense of duty. We have neither those who dance in delirious joy over victory nor those who wilt in defeat.

There is not the slightest sign that should cause us disappointment. Members of opposing parties know in their hearts that we have grown well, but they are ashamed to say so openly. We must therefore continue the work that lies before us. Our ideas are certainly winning today.

At the first stage, they disregarded what we said. At the second stage, we said that authority should not remain in North India and that the country must therefore be divided. Once they recognized that our Kazhagam was growing, they began to say, "The country must not be divided."

We still have many stages to cross in the work of securing Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians. So long as we have abundant young Kazhagam workers who labour with a sense of duty, no one can destroy the path to our ideal.

*Mandram* Annual, 1962.

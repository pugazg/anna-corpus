## English Translation

For four or five days, hooliganism has prevailed in Bengal. Leaders have been attacked, shoes thrown at motorcars, the shirt of Rajan Babu, Congress's sudden leader, torn, and Pandit Jawaharlal, advertised as the “lion of socialism,” put in difficulty. Such reports appear in bold type in the nationalist newspapers. Whenever one reads them carefully, one wonders: so much hooliganism in Bengal? Is this not a meeting of the All India Congress Committee? Is Gandhiji not present? Are not heroic and mighty leaders moving about there? Could such disgrace, clowning and disorder occur where millions of devotees bound by love of Mother India have assembled? Alas, what are we to say of the shape of the times?

Division and quarrelling occur where selfish groups and office-seekers gather; from them come shouting and confusion, followed by indecencies such as stones and earth being thrown. Could the army of nationalist heroes, assembled to cut down the coming Federation through a “war without sword or blood,” attack one another and behave so as to make the country laugh? Anyone reading reports of “Calcutta hooliganism” would think along these lines.

Many truths can be seen here, and they are exceedingly amusing. Those who say hooliganism occurred are Congress members, and those accused of committing it are also Congress members. Is this not a spectacle? Whenever disorder occurs at a public meeting in our province, certain nationalist drums immediately call it “Self-Respect hooliganism.” What will those drums call the sport in Bengal? Is that too a Self-Respect whirlwind? Did the whirlwind from Tamil Nadu travel as far as Bengal and set even its supposedly flawless lions spinning?

We trust the Bengali turmoil will teach a useful lesson to those who boast that there are no people crazed for office and no hunt for positions within Congress. Two men contested the Congress presidency. Intense preparations were made and emissaries displayed their skill. After the election result came Gandhiji's lament, followed by the disorder at Tripuri. The elected president lay ill. Ignoring even his illness, those who had elected him came to distrust him and worked forcefully behind the scenes to strike him down.

National leaders who had exhausted both speech and writing by calling Subhas the “Bengal lion” and “embodiment of sacrifice” suddenly discovered faults in him. Subhas, once portrayed as a hero capable of making the British tremble, abruptly became a reckless extremist. Those who formerly found power in his speech now claimed it was useless talk. The same nationalist comrades who had seen him as a male lion suddenly saw a rough Babu. Subhas, who gathered Bengali youth as nationalist warriors and served as their commander, was now depicted as leader of an army of Bengali hooligans.

How did this altered appearance arise? Earlier they looked at Subhas with their own eyes; now they looked through Gandhi's spectacles, and so he appeared repellent. Nationalist mouths had worshipped our comrade Nariman as a speaker who held Bombay in his palm and a sacrificer ready to dedicate even his life to national service. Yet when the Patel-Nariman dispute ripened, did those same mouths not abuse Nariman? The same comrades who said the pulse of the Central Provinces had been entrusted to Dr Khare drove him away. They began blaming Subhas in exactly the same fashion. Could anyone answer Mahatma's word with another word? When he himself criticised Subhas, what else could the nationalist leaders living in his shadow do? They opened a dictionary of abuse and showered Subhas with its words. His patriotism, spirit of sacrifice, competence and administration were all questioned. A dependent group surrendered to an autocratic Gandhi began accusing Subhas of desiring dictatorship.

An agitation demanded that Subhas either resign or become Gandhi's instrument. Pandit Jawaharlal exerted himself to bend Subhas's course, even by invoking his own experience. The Bengali hero refused the net, flung away office and came out. Unlike many who collided with the stone pillars of the Congress party and shattered, Subhas Babu left with self-respect. Those crazed for office were relieved and breathed freely; plotters smiled. The nationalist drums that danced around Gandhi and gathered money immediately began writing in a tune of joyful celebration. But the Bengali public and heroic Bengali youth rose in fury.

“Have you come to dishonour the lion of Bengal?” they cried. Whenever they saw Congress leaders hunting office, their anger intensified. The moment Rajan Babu was elected president, they shouted, “Shame! Shame!” Truly, is it not shameful? After publicly electing a man known throughout the country, they tried to crush him. When he escaped their trap, they immediately crowned Rajan Babu, Gandhi's child in hand. Why would Bengali heroes not cry shame? Why would they not denounce as shameful those incapable of protecting the gold Bengal gave them? Bengali youth appeared as the very embodiment of that feeling.

Every event that followed ended in confusion. All that can happen when the public is enraged occurred in sequence. The dignitaries who had provoked the anger called the Bengali Babus hooligans, and their newspapers wrote wailing editorials. What can we say of this except, “Suffer, heart, suffer!”

Nationalists had applauded those who lost restraint and caused disorder in Justice Party circles, bestowed upon them the sacred name of nationalist, and incited them against opponents. In Calcutta they saw the fruit of that conduct. The Calcutta disturbances confused the sharp mind of *Dinamani*. Fortunately, the anger of Bengali heroes did not fall upon the Honourable Achariar, who at Tripuri condemned Subhas by telling the story of a leaky boat. Perhaps Achariar remained outside their sight. Nevertheless, *Dinamani* became angry: Bengali Babus were hooligans because they opposed Gandhians!

Yet if those same Babus denounced the ministry of the Honourable Fazlul Huq in Bengal, this nationalist drum would sing folk songs praising their nationalism. If Bengali Babus disturbed a Muslim League meeting, it would say their patriotism overflowed and that the public was naturally so angry with Congress's enemies. In truth, the events in Bengal reveal the hatred felt by the public and radicals toward Congress's wire-pullers.

The contest for office shows that obsession with position runs through Congress. The torment inflicted by Gandhians upon Subhas Babu, although he was elected by a large majority, shows that Gandhism is fascism. The disturbances show that public anger and hatred toward Congress leaders have grown. Subhas sought support precisely to defeat the Federation and won a great majority. But Gandhian intrigue crushed his intention at the outset. This alone proves entirely correct the statement of a northern political expert that Congress is prepared to accept the Federation.

More important than all this is a lesson visible in Calcutta upon which Tamils must fix their attention. Many frequently advise activists of the non-Brahmin movement that everyone seeking self-rule should unite and that, whatever differences of opinion exist, joining Congress will allow those complications to be resolved. Let them look at Subhas Babu's fate.

It was said that Subhas Babu and the Gandhians did not differ in their ultimate aims, but only over a few matters. Even after winning a large majority, Subhas could not make Congress accept his policy. Why? Had Congress been a democratic movement, Subhas could easily have succeeded. His aim failed because Congress is the web of an autocrat's intrigue.

If this was Subhas's fate, what could Tamils accomplish by entering Congress when they and the Gandhians have profound differences over fundamental matters, especially social reform? Nothing. The Subhas episode is therefore a good lesson for every Tamil.

Those who join Gandhism have only one path. Whatever their conscience says, they can survive only by remaining slaves to the Congress leader; otherwise they will be trapped and destroyed in the web of intrigue. Thus justice, honesty, integrity, order and democracy are absent from Congress. In their place, autocracy, intrigue and gambling perform their wild dance. These disorders will destroy that party from within, and Calcutta fever is their symptom.

Congress today is the dwelling place of jealousy, hostility, fury and hatred, and that is why Calcutta fever struck with such violence. We pity those caught in it and suffering. Preventing such a fever from reaching Tamil Nadu is the purpose of the Tamil movement.

*Viduthalai*, 4 May 1939

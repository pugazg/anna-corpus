## English Translation

“The cultivator must have land.

“Food production must increase.

“Those who do not work must bite the dust.

“Cloth must be woven and accumulated.

“The domination of mill-owners must be overthrown.

“Everyone must have a good house.

“Free education must be provided.

“Ignorance must be beaten and driven away.

“Wealth must grow and life become prosperous.

“Poverty must be expelled.

“Useless philosophies, devotional paths and scriptural rubbish that produce division must be burned. There must be unity, and the conviction that everyone is one must become firm.

“The folly that calls men superior and women inferior must disappear. The sound knowledge that all who are born are children of one mother must spread.

“The decayed doctrine of four castes must perish. Untouchability must vanish. We must live as good people in the country.”

Whoever speaks thus, works for these aims and desires to see such an elevated society deserves the people's admiration and praise, regardless of who they are or where they were born.

When the lament of deprivation rises everywhere from every mouth - no work, no food, no whole garment, no hut in which to live - those who make noise that caste must remain, Sanatana must flourish and the four Vedas be recited can only be judged blind to distress and foxes unconcerned with public welfare. Seeing and hearing them will further weaken the people, not create a proper condition.

They care nothing if the country becomes a cremation ground and propose no beneficial change. It is enough that they live, and best if everyone on earth praises them with hymns. They babble everywhere and always that this is the teaching of dharma shastra and following it humanity's duty. They are deceivers trying to restore the law of the jungle.

A head of a Kasi monastery has come to Madras. His name is Karpatri Swami Maharaj. Last week he met Madras reporters at the Sanskrit College in Mylapore and spoke on several subjects, not in Sanskrit but in its alternate edition, Hindi. A summary appeared in *The Hindu* on the eighteenth.

He is a monastic head. It is therefore unsurprising that policies through which people gain clarity, adopt ideas required by the age, and attempt to live like other nations appear hateful, painful, “unrighteous,” and contrary to scripture in his eyes.

But public life and measures taken to improve it need not be dance or bitter neem to these reporters, nor must they search a manuscript to see whether everything has scriptural authority. Anyone may reasonably ask whether reporters and their editors, unable to find a way to remedy the people's present injuries, went to this Karpatri to discover a new path.

India's prime minister is in England; Pakistan's prime minister will spend several days in Cairo while returning from England; American officials are in Paris; and Soviet representative Vyshinsky has resolved not to yield even a little on Berlin. News of this kind produces anxiety and interest in people's minds. Reporters in other countries seek interviews to understand it and publish leaders' inner views intelligibly. The future is contained in the acts those few announce. People must know them and adjust their course.

Why is there such a contradiction between those reporters and ours? A man once watched his son's misconduct and said with scriptural cleverness, “Though my son was ruined, he was ruined with ritual propriety.” Likewise, although journalism ought to stimulate thought, attachment to birth-group and inherited teaching drives these reporters into unnecessary and dangerous work.

The visiting monastic head did not come to Madras on foot or in a palanquin according to ancient custom. He arrived by aeroplane, made in America or England. No text among the dharma shastras attributed to Manu, Mandhata, Parasara or Yajnavalkya describes building an aircraft. Therefore those books cannot declare it righteous to board one and reach a destination rapidly. Yet without examining whether dharma shastra permits it, Karpatri Swami placed his sacred body in this modern machine and arrived in Madras to proclaim scripture's glory. The reporters use modern printing machinery to carry it to others.

Even after boarding aircraft and employing splendid presses, the quality of praising antiquity can never leave this one group.

The ancestors' texts and arrangements enable them to live comfortably without capital and without the compulsion to labour. Thus, although times change and intellectual revolutions occur worldwide, there is no reason to suppose humane character will arise in their hearts.

Karpatri condemns the Hindu Code Bill, which would prohibit polygamy and grant rights concerning marriage within kin groups, inter-caste marriage, divorce and women's property. He asks what right the government has to intervene in religion, as though dharma shastra and the bodies carrying it alone possess that right. He asks why reform is applied only to Hindus and not Christians, Parsis, Muslims and other faiths. He thunders that the *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana* teach our people justice. With this ruined intelligence, he boasts that “we are teachers of the world.”

If the aeroplane possessed a mouth, what would it say?

> Useless man! I was not created through your claim to be world teacher. Ungrateful man, you climb upon me and reach any desired place within minutes, yet say not one word about how I came into being. Have you no shame? Those who created me and other devices like me did not accept your teaching, are not prepared to accept it now, and actively oppose it.
>
> Had the world that produced me accepted your teaching, giant rats and Ganesas, boars and Bhairavas would inhabit all those lands. If you trust your antiquity and believe your ancestors' conduct good, get down from me. Swear never again, even in dreams, to desire the comfort I give. Will you?
>
> Embodiment of selfishness! I am the creation of a society that knew nothing of the Sanatana you exalt. Only by establishing such a new society in your country can machines like me be produced here. Understand this.
>
> Your doctrine was the foundation upon which the country fell to foreigners. Only because that foundation collapsed beyond repair did the crop of rights sprout in your people's minds, producing political freedom. Time and thought have changed. In keeping with time, you too ride upon me, yet try to denigrate intellectual change only when it occurs in others. You will fail. Abandon this conduct.

No woman today wishes to live as Draupadi did. The learned strive to create a society that will not even accept Kunti's sexual code.

The story says that a father married sixty-three thousand women, ruined their happiness, plunged them into grief, became infatuated with the youngest, fainted upon hearing her demand, and ended as an empty shell; while his son Rama of Ayodhya lived with one wife. The *Ramayana* presents Rama's marriage as though accusing his father's conduct of being unfit for the earth and capable of throwing women into distress.

Devotees rejoice that the son changed the father's morality and that Rama observed the vow of one wife. We ask them: if these Karpatris had so far preached that people should follow the same moral rule, would women still marry one man like Dasaratha, fail to receive what they expected, become disappointed, and enter degrading acts that cause others to condemn their birth and character?

Nor does the matter end there. The same road taken in search of pleasure ultimately tortures them, destroys their well-being and turns them into homes of many diseases. Preachers of righteousness may find it convenient to blame only those women. But that method merely ensures moral disorder continues and grows forever while those who condemn it continue living as preachers. Only preventing polygamy can correct this disorder.

With that understood purpose, the proposed law prevents men from marrying many women like Dasaratha and seeks public welfare by standing instead in Rama's position.

These Karpatris neither welcome ideas suited to the age nor follow the path of the Rama they praise.

We do not know whether Rama truly lived with only one wife. We say this from what the epic claims and its worshippers preach, particularly for those who believe the puranas, with the good intention that they reform.

Through his own conduct, knowingly or not, Rama condemns Dasaratha's. That much justice should suffice for minds respectful of purana.

Only people suffering sluggish digestion, stomach disorder or indigestion need a physician. The doctor begins with mild medicine and, if it fails, must administer stronger treatment. There is no need to medicate those able to digest stone. Patients do not ask why healthy people were not given the same medicine. If they do, the physician concludes they suffer not only stomach trouble but brain disorder and sends them to the appropriate hospital.

By asking why a similar law is not proposed for other religions, Karpatri demonstrates that he is fit for precisely that place.

Those who framed dharma shastra in former times approached the reigning king and obtained his command before establishing their harmful laws among the people. That age is gone. This is the age of popular government. There is nothing wrong today in people gathering, deciding and planning what they need, then asking their government to enact it as law.

If the Karpatri Swamis wish, let them stop spending monastic wealth upon Sanatana propaganda and personal pleasure. If permitted, let them establish a factory, increase production, and give workers a good life. Let them work as one among the workers and obtain only the means they need.

This elevated law of the world is directly opposed to the Sanatana conspiracy. Even so, this is the dharma of the present age, the dharma the world has accepted. Will these Karpatris accept it?

**31 October 1948**

## Translator's Notes

`Dharma` is retained in the title because the essay contrasts inherited religious law with a modern social ethic. Karpatri Swami (Hariharananda Saraswati) was a conservative Hindu religious leader. The Hindu Code Bill debates concerned reforms including monogamy, divorce and women's property rights.

`Sanatana` refers here to Sanatana Dharma as invoked by the essay's opponents. Manu, Parasara and Yajnavalkya are traditional lawgivers; Mandhata is an ancient king. Vyshinsky refers to Soviet diplomat Andrey Vyshinsky. The “four Vedas” and “four castes” preserve the source's phrasing.

The source OCR appears to state that Dasaratha married `sixty-three thousand` women; conventional Ramayana retellings often give a different number. The translation preserves the OCR reading and flags it rather than silently normalising it. The article's religious and historical claims are translated as polemical argument, not independently verified conclusions.

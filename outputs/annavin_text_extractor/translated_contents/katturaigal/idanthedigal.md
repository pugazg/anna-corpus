## English Translation

“Mudaliar is a very good man, exceedingly charitable, and capable in administration. I do not deny any of that. He is pure gold. But look here, the reputation of the Congress party must not be damaged. They have somehow selected me as the Congress candidate. Since responsibility for protecting Congress’s reputation has fallen upon me, I have to oppose Mudaliar; there is no other reason, sir. Do not look at me, and do not consider him. Look at Congress, sir. That is all I am saying.”

The Congress candidate.

“What can one do, man? Once you decide to stand against someone, you have to name some party. Those fellows hold meetings and keep saying ‘Congress, Congress.’ Must we not hold a meeting too? If we hold one, what other name can we use? It has to be Justice. That is why I must call myself a member of that party. Otherwise, what do I care for parties or brinjals? Am I saying Congress is a rotten party? Somehow it does not suit us, that is all. I have rendered so many services to these people. Will they go away without giving me one vote? No, I ask you myself. Let Congress be a great party. What sort of man is that fellow? How long have you known him? In what way is he better than me? Tell me, let us hear it. Look here, do not keep talking of parties and betray good people. That is all I say. If you think Congress alone is great, go on saying so. Just do not forget me; cast your vote for me. I have never failed to help you, and until now I have never opened my mouth to ask you for even one favour.”

The Justice candidate.

This is the sort of speech one commonly hears during an election.

The man standing for Congress places his party in front and speaks. He emphasises that the respect is not for him but for the party. He says he stands only for the party and labours only for its honour.

People may ask whether this is what matters and whether they should not instead examine the individual’s integrity, ability, and qualifications. That is a question for voters. Yet one fact cannot be denied: candidates claim to exist in service of the party that fields them. It may even be an electoral tactic, but the manner in which they display party loyalty deserves appreciation.

Those claiming to stand for other parties, however, speak by placing themselves in front. They do not speak about the party, show attachment to it, or even love it. Such people are mushrooms of election season. At the same time, whether they win or lose, they become termites consuming the party.

They possess none of the party loyalty with which a Congressman beats his chest and declares, “I exist for the party.” They form no bond with the party and feel no tenderness towards its workers. Like fireflies, they appear during elections and, before disappearing, conduct themselves so as to extinguish even the lamp of principle that party workers painfully lit.

That is why, once the number of genuine party workers increased, the Salem Conference resolved that we should no longer act as heralds for the electoral pageant of such people. Salem declared that we did not need the alliance of “notables” who felt proud merely to call themselves “enemies of Congress” and “friends of the government.”

The number must grow of those who regard an election as one among the services rendered to a party, one method of spreading its principles, and the task of reaching a place where those principles can be put into practice. We once had many people who were sudden lovers, friends created by crisis, companions of urgency, arrivals, and discoveries. The moment there was no election work, they vanished, and with them vanished the crowd without principle.

Congress now faces the difficulty that once confronted us. Black marketeers, friends of the Collector, people newly taught to wear khadi, and those who called satyagraha a riot are joining there for the election. Let them go freely. But genuine party workers there will not tolerate these “place-seekers” for long. A situation in which a loud voice steals the rights earned by a worker cannot endure. Before the khadi shirts of those who went from here to there become old, their romance will break, as it must. In this party, that party, or any party, those who live for definite principles, labour, and endure hardship and loss must be respected. Administrative responsibility must not be handed to glossy opportunists.

We have sometimes gained petty pleasure from hearing, “Yesterday the priest came to our zamindar’s house. The zamindar saw him coming and deliberately said, so that the fellow’s face would fall, ‘These Brahmins must never be trusted.’” We believed that zamindar would give his life for Tamils and utterly hated Brahminism. A little later we learned that he rebuked the priest merely to wipe our faces clean, not from genuine anger. Affection continued to oscillate until the Salem Conference. Young men dared to accept whatever blame might come, and those “heroes of speech” were driven out.

Congress youth who now delight in saying, “The president of our Town Congress Committee knew the Collector was coming, sir. Knowing it, he chose that very day to drive to see the Collector with a new khadi flag flying from his motorcar,” will soon realise that their party too needs a Salem.

You may ask, “Very well, sir. If they are driven away because they must remain neither here nor there, where are they to go?” Let them go anywhere, provided they live with sincere, firm, selfless attachment to some principle. Only then will public life become pure. Otherwise public life too will become like countless institutions of exploitation, an organisation through which selfish people run shops in the name of principle and exploit those devoted to it.

It will take time to learn how contemptuously the shopkeepers of principle within Congress speak about many “volunteers” who have obtained seats in legislatures under its name. On that day, Salem will occur there too.

For those who regard this as empty talk, we offer an example. During the salt satyagraha in Thanjavur district, where was the Vadapathimangalam mirasdar Thiagarajan? What was he doing during the August Movement? Why does he now come to join Congress? Did Congress lose strength because he was absent? Or has patriotism suddenly arisen in him? Why did he enter Congress?

Will genuine Congress workers fail to understand the subtlety? When the man from Vadapathimangalam goes seeking a leadership position in expectation of good returns from a new place, either the workers’ labour must be exploited or a “Salem” must come. What other way is there? That is why we say public life must be shown in practice to belong to those who work for principle, not those who lean wherever the wind blows.

If it becomes firmly understood that a person gains a place in public life only through steadfastness to principle, the “place-seekers” will either withdraw from public life or wholeheartedly begin cultivating whatever principle they truly favour. Only then will public life become sacred ground.

At Salem we conducted this acid test: are people in a party for its principles, or do they say, “Since we happen to be in the party, let us somehow talk about its principles”?

Those now remaining are not people who regard elections as the only party work. They exist for certain principles. They may not quickly capture the electoral machine or strengthen the propaganda apparatus, but they do not tire on that account.

Such friends have asked: “You said those people were not wanted for the legislature. What have you decided about municipalities and district boards?” *Viduthalai* wrote an editorial on the subject.

Local bodies such as district boards and municipalities are not arenas for party quarrels. They are places where people oversee administrative officials exercising certain powers granted under a particular law. It has long been our policy that parties should not compete there and turn them into arenas for unrelated disputes.

Congress thinks otherwise. It has attempted to conduct the elections by party method. Because it has strength and convenience, it wishes to strengthen its hand from Delhi to Kuppam. What will be the result? Congress becomes prey to the place-seekers described above. It will take time for the full result to appear, but one day it will certainly be seen.

What we desire and recommend first is that these institutions not be turned into party battlefields. People may ask whether there should then be no contest, whether the bodies should simply be left to those with influence or to the town’s wandering “big men.” No. They certainly cannot be handed to whoever happens along; that would be dangerous.

What other method is there? There is one.

A programme and policy must be created specifically for these bodies. Those wishing to sit in them must abandon the method of speaking to the public about party grandeur, Gandhi’s greatness, Jawaharlal’s courage, and Aruna’s revolution merely to win applause and prizes for empty slogans. They must explain, “If we are elected, we will perform these particular tasks; our administration will introduce these new methods.”

The people must examine the programmes presented by two or three separate groups and elect those supporting whichever programme appears sound. Only then will real work occur there; no other method will produce benefit, nor has any done so thus far.

To prevent “place-seekers,” many Congress youths in different areas have fought within their candidate-selection committees. Whether they won is not important. What matters is that the attitude is changing.

In one town, several people who declared during the August Movement that they would no longer remain in Congress, gave written apologies stating they would work with the “opposition” in municipal affairs, and entered a new pact of cooperation now come forward as Congress candidates. When opposed in committee, these place-seekers ask, “What does it matter?” We say such people should have no place not merely in municipal councils but anywhere in public life. The key lies with the earnest young people devoted to public service within every party.

We know notables who say, “The Congress committee president himself told me there would be no contest in my ward. Even if they field someone, they will merely catch hold of a token candidate. I will win anyway; there is no fear.” During a temple festival they are orthodox believers; when Periyar visits, Self-Respecters; when a Congress minister comes, patriots; when the Collector arrives, friends of British rule. They assume these “ten incarnations” for one purpose: selfishness, the pursuit of personal comfort.

Even in attending to municipal administration, the division between governing party and opposition ends in nothing but bargaining, rather than definite policy and a clear programme.

An opposition member may say, “Let Congress have the majority, man. The Commissioner will listen only to what I say.” Similarly, if another party has the council majority, a Congressman may say, “I will go higher up. Is not the Local Board officer the elder brother of our son-in-law’s relative? I will have our man say one word to him and get this very order cancelled. Just watch.” This is called government by representatives elected by the public. Why these impostors? Why this useless performance?

Before a council meeting, one may bargain with the majority party: “I will vote with you on items seven through nine. But item fourteen concerns our Balakrishna Mudaliar. He has apparently spoken to our Naidu too. Leave just that one item alone.”

After walking from house to house and obtaining votes, the majority party never explains its administration or programme to the public. Nor does the minority opposition explain, “The majority party’s programme or administration contains these harms; I said these things in council to prevent or avert them.”

Both sides merely go to the council chamber like various invited groups who attend a wedding house, enjoy the spectacle, eat, and return. Nothing occurs to make people believe that one side works according to a programme and the opposition points out defects in it. Most possess no such sense of responsibility.

Suddenly ask a candidate seeking your vote, “Sir, how much money is spent on roads? How much on primary education?” No answer will come. Instead a faint smile will spread across his face. He will ask, “What is this, Naidu? Why this new manner? These are very strong questions. What are you angry about?” The candidate assumes that a voter asking questions is showing anger.

Is the candidate alone to blame? The beloved voter’s conduct contains many faults too. He will not ask such questions. The garden-lease quarrel, the dispute over cattle in the shed, a fight among in-laws, a recommendation to the sub-inspector, and the story of being unable to obtain samba rice will all be discussed at every doorstep, with the candidate and his little band on one side and the voter with children and family on the other.

At the end, the candidate says, “By God, I generally rely upon you alone.” The voter concludes, “Whatever happens, I shall ask my elder brother one word and let you know.”

A task established to improve the urban life of many thousands of people using many thousands of rupees is thus entrusted to “place-seekers.” We say this is improper.

Candidates must arise who state the definite principles and administrative work they will pursue. Voters must forget who stands and to which party the person belongs, carefully examine the proposed programme, decide whether it is sound, and cast their votes accordingly. Will such candidates and voters arise? They certainly will. When and how? When public life is purified, and when genuine public workers protect their feeling and labour from being plundered by people who seek positions using party names.

Therefore we speak not from a party motive, but from the desire that urban administration function properly. Do not support place-seekers. Do not sacrifice parties and principles for them. Do not turn public life into a new institution of exploitation. This is our firm declaration.

(Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 3 November 1946.)

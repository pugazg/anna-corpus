## English Translation

“How much farther is it?”

“We are quite near now. It is only another five miles. We can get there quickly.”

“All right, all right. Drive faster. What is this area called?”

“Vyasarpadi.”

This was a conversation between a gentleman and his motor driver.

The gentleman, who had come from Nellore, set out by motor car intending to reach Madras. The car had approached the city. When it reached Vyasarpadi, he asked how many miles remained before they entered Madras.

Three hours later, the same gentleman was still travelling in the same motor car, but had not reached Madras!

“This has become a great nuisance. How far away is Madras now?” the gentleman asked.

“There must still be at least twenty miles to go,” replied the driver. The car was not moving backwards like some horses; it was certainly travelling forwards. Yet it did not reach Madras.

There would have been no such trouble if the motor car that reached Vyasarpadi had gone straight into Madras. But it could not do so. Unable to take the direct road, it had to make a long circuit, travel a great distance in another direction, and then enter Madras. The journey would have ended in five miles by the direct route; the detour turned it into a journey of 150 miles.

This is no story and no fantasy. It was the experience of the Honourable Gopala Reddy.

Comrade Gopala Reddy had this magnificent experience after becoming a minister.

On the day of the Buckingham and Carnatic Mills strike last week, the Honourable Gopala Reddy left Nellore. When he reached Vyasarpadi, an army officer reportedly told him, “Sir, there is disorder in the city. Do not proceed any farther.” Gopala Reddy, however, had studied at Santiniketan without losing his temper; and he was no lightweight, but an “Honourable” minister. He ordered, “Drive on!”

The motor car proceeded. Crowds of people stood all along the road and prevented it from advancing. Perhaps they thought that, when the whole city was participating in the strike, this one motor car had no reason to run into the city. The minister may have imagined that nobody could stop a car flying the khadi flag. Stones were reportedly thrown at it; the crowd surrounded it so that it could not move; and the khadi flag on it was torn down.

Seeing this, the minister ordered the driver to turn toward Red Hills. From there he explained the situation to the Chief Minister by telephone. The Chief Minister instructed the army to provide suitable protection. With military vehicles before and behind it, the car again headed toward Vyasarpadi. Again there was an obstruction, and again it returned to Red Hills. Once it was clear that the direct route was impossible, the minister travelled in a wide circle through many places, passed Avadi and other towns, and finally reached Madras after a journey of nearly 150 miles. To reach a city only five miles away, our friend Gopala Reddy had to make a 150-mile circuit!

It is not merely Gopala Reddy's motor car. The ministry itself travels in just this fashion. Unable to take the straight road, it has been compelled to twist, turn and proceed somehow or other. The Honourable Gopala Reddy ought to know a Telugu verse; perhaps the responsibilities of ministerial office have made him forget it, so we remind him:

> Chakkani raja margamu
> undaga sandulu
> duredi enduku?

Tamil has a proverb expressing the same thought: why take a crooked road when the good, straight road lies open?

But what can he do? He knows perfectly well that it is needless to travel 150 miles to reach Madras when it is only five miles away. Yet there is no other way, for he cannot travel by the direct road.

The people's anger obstructs the ministers' path. Therefore they must journey only through the crooked routes of prohibitory orders, baton charges and tear-gas bombs.

The mill workers have suffered for a month. Had the ministers boldly intervened to end their distress, the matter could have been settled as easily as travelling from Vyasarpadi to Madras. Mr Town does not keep an imperial army beside him. He has no party of his own in the legislature. He is one man, but he is a capitalist and, moreover, a white capitalist. This ministry has no need to fear him and no reason to favour him.

The workers, by contrast, shed their blood for the tricolour that now flies high. Their demand is not merely just; it could be settled easily. Out of a profit of ten and a half crores of rupees, they ask for half a crore as bonus. Nor are they striking because Communists incited them. They are unalloyed trade unionists, protected by Anthony Pillai's calmness and Thiru. Vi. Ka.'s conciliatory purpose.

Even so, the ministry displays to them the strength of its newly acquired authority, not affection. When it attacked the Communists, it offered an excuse: “Do these red-flag people care more for labourers than we do?” What has it given the workers today? It distributes tear-gas bombs generously even to mothers.

Every thoughtful person in Madras condemns the new ministry for continuing the old rule instead of finding a new method. Because it has become “Honourable,” criticism does not enter its ears. Even Kalyanasundaranar's counsel has had no effect. The intoxication of power has made it forget its heart remarkably quickly.

The ministers try to escape by blaming the public. They say the strike inconveniences the public. That may be so. But those who failed to prevent such strikes cause an even greater inconvenience. Why should the “party of the poor” hesitate to wipe away a poor man's tears? If it truly existed for the welfare of the poor, would it talk boastfully in defence of one capitalist's obstinacy while fourteen thousand families suffer hunger, starvation and fear?

What cosmic calamity would follow from releasing Anthony Pillai? Would the ministry's mighty strength be scorched to ashes by one solitary man's angry glance? Why such stubbornness over this ordinary matter?

When today's ministers were once in prison, the heroes of the pen wrote that they had to be released before reconciliation could become possible. Is it beautiful, intelligent or democratic for those same writers now to pretend not to see this event? Have they rented their pens to the group eager to destroy the labour movement?

From hundreds of platforms, Thiru. Vi. Ka. praised Congress in sweet Tamil and called Gandhi a visible god. Today his heart burns, and harsh but truthful words emerge from him. Has this softened the hearts of the Congress ministers? No. Why should they soften? Their position has become “weighty,” and the moisture in their hearts has consequently dried up.

The Congress that grew by receiving the labour of Tamil Nadu's venerable elder now seeks to injure him. Rumour around the town says that he too may be sent inside prison.

Thiru. Vi. Ka. will say, “We are subjects of none!” Not only that; he may sing a new hymn as well.

One may point out that our friend Periyar is here.

Periyar and his great force will certainly stand and work beside Thiru. Vi. Ka.; beside the worthy Tamils who trusted Congress and were deceived; beside the Congress Dravidians who grieve that, believing themselves to be labouring for national freedom, they merely helped enthrone a small group that rules by savage methods in the country's name; and beside the workers.

We trust that Thiru. Vi. Ka. will rejoice when he hears Periyar's cry: “Fear not! Do not worry!”

At the beginning of their public lives, the two stood together and won. Later they separated and suffered in spirit, while their opponents prospered through the division. Today an opportunity has arisen for them to unite. More than an opportunity, it has become a necessity that they stand and labour together in public life.

And not merely those two. This is the hour when everyone who believes that government must not injure the noble ideals of the people's welfare, democracy, socialism and rationalism must work together. If the Muslim Leaguers in the legislature undertake this task, it can easily be dismissed as a “Hindu-Muslim dispute.” They therefore cannot perform it. Others must do it and make true democracy flourish. Otherwise Tamil Nadu, which once declared that wrongdoing remains wrongdoing even when committed by the eye on the forehead, will itself have to be sacrificed to partiality.

We trust that Periyar's speech at the meeting condemning repressive legislation will lay the foundation for this rare undertaking. If he hears that Thiru. Vi. Ka. may have to go to prison, what will Periyar say? Surely, “The next cell is mine.” And beyond it, not merely a handful of cells but great halls will have to be built for Periyar's vast force.

Any public servant would say that, instead of leaving us outside while they govern as they please, boast that nobody can question them, fling repression at every issue, conduct lying propaganda through newspapers and make true public service impossible, it would be more honourable for them to issue one general order: “We must rule; therefore all of you must remain in prison until our rule ends,” and throw us all inside.

The Honourable Gopala Reddy said: “The people surrounded the car, threw stones at it and tore down the flag. They did not know I was a minister. Had they known, my position would have become extremely dangerous.”

Such is the intimacy between minister and people! The minister thinks that if they recognize him, his head itself may be in danger. That is the measure of their affection.

Congress Dravidian comrades! Friends who refuse to recognize that the times have changed, who hurt your own hands by throwing stones and shed the blood of your own people: heed the minister's considered words. He says those people did not know he was a minister and that, had they known, he would have been in danger.

The minister has drawn a picture of the people's state of mind. We ask thoughtful people to observe it. And this has happened before even one year has passed! Yet the government still feels moved to rage against the opposition. What are we to call this?

History is the best dictionary for learning the speed and character of the force born when the hearts of trusting people break. We ask our Congress friends to consult it in their leisure.

The farmer's heart seethes. The mill worker's suffering increases. The tears of public servants beaten, kicked and imprisoned continue to swell. The lack of clothing and the shortage of rice weigh heavily on the public mind. The arrangements made by rulers who see no way forward create still more trouble. All these forces now lie scattered and separate, but each is growing. Every baton charge, every prohibitory order and every tear-gas bomb is a fine “tonic” for that growth. Forgive us: our ministers dislike that term. It is a fine elixir of life, and they dispense it generously!

The suffering teachers, afflicted mill workers, anguished farmers, students without school facilities and distressed public must now gather and stand together as one body. The Dravidar Kazhagam will labour to unite all those scattered forces. If the ministers threaten to prevent this, let the prison fortress prepare to welcome the Dravidar Kazhagam.

“Who is in Cell Number Seven?”

“Anthony Pillai.”

“Who is in the next cell?”

“Thiru. Vi. Ka.”

“And next?”

“Periyar.”

“Who are those in that hall?”

“Members of the Dravidar Kazhagam!”

“What is that noise? Who is over there?”

“Elementary-school teachers!”

“And who are those visible at the far end?”

“Communists.”

Let Omandur Ramasamy Reddiar, famed as a man of peace and compassion for the poor, visit the prison asking such questions about its inmates. May Thiru. Vi. Ka. have the privilege of welcoming him from behind the bars! Periyar has enjoyed that privilege many times. Let Omandur earn the distinction of imprisoning Thiru. Vi. Ka., who laboured to smash the chains fastened around Mother India's feet.

Let Omandur imprison every progressive force in Tamil Nadu and then govern. We are not worried. His own conscience will tell him: “You are not governing. You are destroying your friends' goodwill.” May Murugan's grace reach him.

*13 April 1947*

## Translator's Notes

The title `ஜீவாமிருதம்` literally means an elixir or nectar that gives life. Anna uses it ironically: state repression nourishes and unites the very opposition it seeks to suppress.

The Telugu verse is transliterated conservatively from the OCR (`சக்கனி ராஜ மார்க்கமு / உண்டகா சந்துலு / தூரேதி எந்துகு?`). Its sense is supplied by the Tamil proverb immediately following it: why enter crooked lanes when a fine royal road is available?

`கனம்` was the Tamil honorific used for “Honourable”; Anna repeatedly plays on its literal sense of heaviness or weight. `கதர்` is khadi. `திரு.வி.க.` is Thiru. V. Kalyanasundaram (Thiru. Vi. Ka.), the Tamil scholar and labour leader. Anthony Pillai was associated with the mill workers' struggle. `நாமார்க்குங் குடியல்லோம்` is a classical devotional declaration meaning “We are subjects of none.” The reference to fault remaining fault even when committed by the “forehead-eye” invokes the Nakkirar legend.

Several source lines are visibly damaged, especially the description of the mill stoppage on Image 2, a sentence about partiality and the forehead-eye on Image 6, and fragments surrounding Gopala Reddy's quotation. Their recoverable contextual meaning has been translated conservatively; no speculative repair has been inserted into the preserved Tamil source.

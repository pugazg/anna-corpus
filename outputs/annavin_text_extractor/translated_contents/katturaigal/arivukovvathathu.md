## English Translation

The late comrade M. Singaravelar was also in the Congress for some time. His reason for joining it was that opposing imperialism meant opposing one among the many cruelties that could ruin the lives of poor people, breaking one among the many fetters fastened upon the humble, and removing one bar from the prison cage enclosing society. It was only in this sense that M. Singaravelar joined the Congress in opposing imperialism. He nevertheless continued to condemn the Congress, and Gandhism in particular, as a capitalist, "bourgeois" movement.

When Gandhi began a fast declaring that "the Untouchables must not have a separate electorate," Singaravelar condemned Gandhi's course with extraordinary force. One may say that the article he wrote then deserves to be memorised by everyone concerned with both national liberation and the liberation of the poor. Here it is:

> According to our socialist view, Gandhi's decision to undertake a fast concerning the Depressed Classes must be described as a great victory for British rule. We must understand that it has established the triumph of British political strategy. Gandhi has abandoned the struggle for swaraj that he had undertaken and agreed to kill himself over this useless question. This must be regarded as a great victory for British politics: the "Triumph of British Diplomacy." In the political strategy of the British, where does our Gandhi stand? "He is nowhere." We may say that he is nowhere at all.
>
> At one time, he witnessed the British provoke the Africans called Zulus into rebellion and then destroy them. At the end of the nineteenth century, he associated with the Boers but, in their war, assisted their enemies. In the Great European War, he raised an auxiliary force for the British; after that war ended, he began the Non-Cooperation Movement against those very British. Did he at least carry it through properly to its conclusion? No. Because someone or other had been killed, he stopped it at Bardoli while 50,000 people remained in prison. Is this what he accomplished? His contradiction did not end there.
>
> He began the useless Salt Satyagraha, sent 60,000 people to prison through it, signed the Delhi Pact, and abandoned every one of them. That too did not end matters. He then began the Civil Disobedience Movement and caused approximately sixty or seventy thousand people to be confined in prison. Claiming that Hindus and Panchamas were being divided, he displayed his fury over the separate electorate, undertook a fast, and proclaimed that he was going to die. We ask: what ghastly spectacle is this?
>
> The world must learn one thing from it: however world-famous Gandhi may be, no undertaking should be based upon trust in him. Rather than trust him, one might plant a post in mud, trust it, and attempt to climb ashore.
>
> His entire life has consisted of contradictory acts. One may say without objection, "His life is a series of contradictions." His fame began in South Africa. Did he at least remain there and accomplish the task he had undertaken? After stirring agitation there for five or six years, he left the Indian residents living there stranded in midstream and came to India. We cannot understand why the world still trusts a man who has suffered a succession of such defeats, a "Series of defeats."
>
> His agitation by fasting appears to us to be merely an empty shot, an empty clamour. We ask: what if a separate electorate is given to the Depressed Classes? Under the present British scheme, what is anyone actually going to receive? Under a plan that keeps saying, "Nothing for you; nothing for your father either," who is going to obtain any greater freedom? Such being the matter, we ask: why this empty clamour? Our conclusion is that all this noise is daylight deception.
>
> This separate electorate is said to divide Hindus into two classes, caste Hindus and casteless Hindus. The Panchamas, it is said, will become separate through the separate electorate. Hindus will supposedly regard the Panchamas as enemies because of it, and hostility will arise against the unity of the two. Hearing this tale brings a proverb to mind: "The wolf sits and weeps because the goat is getting wet."
>
> For the past five thousand years, Hindus have supposedly treated Pallars, Paraiyars, Panchamas, Thottis, Parangiyars, Thiyyas, Shanars, and other Untouchables—the Depressed—as their comrades. Now, it seems, the separate electorate has intervened to destroy that fellowship and has brought ruin upon universal unity. What a spectacle!
>
> Tens of millions of Depressed people lack houses, shelter, food, clothing, education, sanitation, medicine, and even the means to obtain the ritual handful of rice at death. We cannot understand why all that is supposed to be ruined by one or two votes. We ask: during the past fifty years, what have Hindus achieved for Panchamas through general constituencies in the Indian legislatures? Throughout this period, what have you Hindus done for fifty million Panchamas?
>
> How many wells have you built? How many houses have you caused to be built? How many hospitals have you built? How many schools have you established? How many colleges have you founded? How many public-health institutions have you created? How many free scholarships have you provided? How many libraries have you founded? How many facilities for clean water have you arranged? What have you done to eradicate the poverty, superstitious customs, and blind devotion of fifty million Depressed people? What are you doing? What are you going to do? These too are our questions.

Thus wrote comrade Singaravelar. Wherever he saw cruelty and injustice, deception and distortion, sham argument and falsehood—whether in a government palace, a Shankaracharya monastery, an ancient manuscript, or the publication of those staging the drama of national liberation—M. Singaravelar would condemn it without fear, pity, or deference, without even asking whether it lay within his power.

The reason he possessed such courage was his love for the oppressed, those subjected to cruelty, and the poor and humble. Through this article, therefore, we can clearly understand that the Congress course was not truly capable of safeguarding the rights of the people of this country and granting them liberation.

*Dravida Nadu*, 3 March 1948.

## Translator's Notes

The long central passage is an historical quotation from M. Singaravelar and retains its polemical judgments, period terminology, English parentheticals, and caste labels. These are not silently modernized or presented as translator assertions. `சமதர்மம்` is rendered as socialism in Singaravelar's political context. The proverb about the wolf and wet goat preserves the source's charge of hypocritical concern.

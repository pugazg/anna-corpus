## English Translation

“First, you must understand the structure of a man's body. You must also know the levels at which heat and cold are maintained in that body. It is essential to learn whether he consumes food suited to his physical constitution. Only then should you investigate what may have caused his illness, in accordance with Valluvar's words:

> Diagnose the disease; seek its root;
> seek the means that will cure it;
> and apply that means at the proper time.

“You law-college students, who are to become advocates in the future, must first learn to investigate in this way and administer medicine suited to curing the disease. Thereafter, step by step, you must learn about surgery and other forms of treatment. This is my first lecture to you on medicine.”

Thus a medical expert appointed to train students who are studying law and preparing to become advocates teaches his lesson.

“What is this, sir? How strange! Is a doctor serving as the teacher of law students? This too seems to be one of the world's novelties. Is it a joke, a marvel or an act of madness?”

If you ask me this, what answer can I give? I can say only one thing. The organisers of the law college ought to have known that the teacher training its students should be a legal expert, and they should have appointed someone proficient in that field. But they did not. In place of a legal expert, they brought in and installed a medical expert.

Nor did the doctor so appointed refuse the post by saying, “I am a doctor; what do I know of law?” He accepted it and began teaching: “You students of the law college who are to become future advocates, listen to my first lecture on medicine.” It never occurred to the teacher to ask whether medical practice should be taught to law students. Nor did the students ask, “Sir! We have come here to study law, but you lecture us on medicine. Are you a doctor or a legal expert?”

You will be surprised if I tell you that an incident like this recently occurred in our country. But it is true. This is precisely what happened. Here is the incident.

“Not everyone who learns Sanskrit can be called an Aryan. In ancient times Sanskrit may have been the common language; everyone wrote in that language,” says a certain man.

Do you ask who he was, where and when he spoke, and why? I shall tell you that too.

On 28 February 1945, the fifth anniversary of the Ramalingam Tamil Students' Association established at the Oriental College in Tirupati was held. Presiding over the celebration, comrade T. A. Ramalingam Chettiar, M.L.C., of Coimbatore delivered a speech and flung out the sentences I have quoted above without the least examination of what came before or after them.

I am confident that no one needs to wonder about the purpose for which a Tamil students' association would have been established at the Oriental College in Tirupati. Everyone can understand that an association founded by Tamil students would concern itself with Tamil and would aim to help its founders develop their knowledge and benefit from the many fields founded upon the Tamil language.

Yet what counsel did a man invited to preside over an association founded with such an excellent purpose give its student organisers? Like the teacher who explained medicine to law students, he expounded to the Tamil association's students the greatness of Sanskrit and the necessity of learning it.

“When one kind of seed is sown, another kind of gourd will not sprout” is one of our country's proverbs. But only here, in our country, does it happen otherwise. The anniversary of the Tirupati Tamil Students' Association demonstrates that when a tamarind seed is sown, it sprouts as a mango tree.

The seed called the Tamil Students' Association was planted with the good purpose of discovering the language, art and civilisation of the Tamil people, enjoying them ourselves and enabling others to enjoy them. Comrade Ramalingam, however, tried to make it sprout forth the language, art and civilisation of the Aryan people. We do not yet know what conclusion the association's students reached about the man who transformed their Tamil Students' Association into a Sanskrit Students' Association.

When we examine Ramalingam's method, we find that he cannot even be compared with the teacher who taught medicine at a law college. The latter had merely been placed in the wrong institution; his knowledge of the subject on which he spoke had not changed. Though he spoke about medicine at a law college, he explained medical methods correctly.

Ramalingam, however, who spoke about Sanskrit at a Tamil Students' Association, appears to be a crippled scholar who knows nothing about Sanskrit itself. He says Sanskrit was the common language in ancient times. There is no more difference between making that claim and saying that, in ancient times, horse horns were washed with hen's milk.

Many linguistic researchers, the author argues, have stated that Sanskrit, used by the Aryans, existed only in written form; that many Sanskrit scholars tried to bring it into spoken use but failed to obtain the desired result; that numerous alterations were therefore made to it on many occasions; that the very name “Sanskrit” consequently came to mean “a language that had been refined or corrected”; and that when it was later found unfit for spoken use, it was called a “dead language.” Seeing that the comrade who came to speak on the subject had not learned even this, we are obliged to pity him because he too is a Tamil.

The author next turns to works classified as *Shruti* in Sanskrit. He contends that, when one person reads them, others may hear them with their ears, but cannot retain their meaning in their minds or explain what they heard to anyone else. The reason, he says, is that the works called *Shruti* are merely sound forms made from Sanskrit letters and contain neither a wealth of meaning nor any explanation of ideas.

If this were not their nature, he asks, why would they have acquired the name *Shruti*? He glosses the Sanskrit word directly in Tamil as “that which is heard by the ear.” Any work written in any language can be read by someone who knows the language and heard by many others. Why, then, should only the category called *Shruti* be defined as something heard by the ear? Other works heard by the ear contain explanations that can be conveyed to others. Perhaps, he remarks ironically, this is why that “special” name was assigned to *Shruti* even in ancient times.

Likewise, all the Sanskrit works in the category called *Smriti*, he says, are fit to remain only in the memory of those who read them. *Shruti*, at least, releases meaningless empty sound: one person reads and another hears. But *Smriti* does not seem to possess even that qualification. Whoever reads it must keep its ideas within his own mind.

The author alleges that Aryan sages who composed those works filled them with so many irrational and repugnant accounts that they could not be repeated. He glosses the Sanskrit word *Smriti* in Tamil as “that which must be kept in memory.” Perhaps, he says, it was because the Vedas, Agamas, Shruti, Smriti and other works and the Sanskrit language contained such ignorant and repellent ideas that a rule was created in those days forbidding non-Brahmins from studying them.

Who, he asks, can deny that until the British assumed responsibility for governing the country, that rule and related customs such as widow immolation remained in practice? If a rule preventing non-Brahmins from studying Sanskrit or its works existed until the British arrived, what are we to call the Coimbatore comrade's assertion that Sanskrit had been the common language in ancient times?

It is the nature of a scholar to measure even a truth he knows before speaking it. But what medicine can be prescribed for the arrogant disease of scattering statements without knowledge or restraint?

Did this astonishing researcher stop after claiming that Sanskrit was the common language? No. “Everyone wrote in that language,” he declared, placing a vermilion mark upon a rubbish heap and displaying it proudly.

We do not know whom he includes within the word “everyone.” It would have helped greatly had he identified what was written in Sanskrit in Tamil Nadu, by whom, and in which periods. Did Tolkappiyar write in Sanskrit? Did Tiruvalluvar write in Sanskrit? Or did the poets of the Sangam write in Sanskrit? The only assertion still left unspoken is that the *Tolkappiyam*, the *Tirukkural* and Sangam literature were all written in Sanskrit.

If someone will say that too, we may bring this Aryan-Dravidian struggle to an end. Once the *Tolkappiyam*, the *Tirukkural* and Sangam literature have been declared Sanskrit works, what Dravidians remain? What Dravidian land remains? What struggle over them remains? Nothing would remain at all.

When every work in this country becomes Sanskrit, the people here too can only be Sanskrit-speaking people. That is to say, they can only be Aryans.

But do you ask about the people here who speak some language called Tamil and are called Tamils because they speak it? Who are they?

Why, they must be the people who came from Central Asia with their goats and cattle to escape famine, settled along the banks of the Ganges and gradually spread across this country!

Do not ask me, “This is a new piece of research! How can your statement that Tamils drove their livestock from Central Asia, came here to escape famine and settled in this country possibly make sense?” Go directly to Coimbatore. Comrade T. A. Ramalingam Chettiar, M.L.C., is there. Ask him. He will explain it.

Why do I introduce him to you as someone who can provide the explanation? Because he has now discovered a novel method of research and announced it for the first time at the Tamil Students' Association of the Tirupati college. I shall tell you what that research is: “It cannot be said that the Aryans came here from somewhere else.” That is the discovery now made by comrade Ramalingam.

The author presents Aryan migration from Central Asia and settlement here as a truth established beyond denial by hundreds of researchers. In such a situation, he asks, what surprise would there be if some person who serves as a pliable instrument in an obscure corner were to deny it by turning everything upside down: making the Dravidians, the country's ancient inhabitants, into Aryans; converting Dravidian works into Sanskrit works; and claiming that the Dravidians themselves came from Central Asia and settled here?

We do not fear that truth will disappear merely because such people speak this way. We must nevertheless lament that, even in this age of research, our country grants people who have reached this height of ignorance the privilege of presiding over events held in responsible institutions.

Readers with a spirit of inquiry may decide whether anyone other than a man who had reached the height of ignorance would venture to say that Sanskrit was once the common language of this country, that everyone wrote only in Sanskrit, and that it was wrong to say the Aryans came here from elsewhere.

I ask one further question. A Tamil Students' Association was established with the purpose that the Tamil language should grow, Tamil art should flourish, Tamil civilisation should attain distinction and Tamil culture should be protected. What name other than “one who has reached the height of ignorance” can be given to a man who stood in that association and delivered a speech praising the elevation and greatness of Sanskrit?

25 March 1945.

## Translator's Notes

The dates `28-2-45` and `25-3-1945`, the name and designation `T.A. இராமலிங்கஞ் செட்டியார், M.L.C.`, and the English parentheticals `Operation`, `Lecture`, `Doctor`, and `Dead Language` were checked directly against the page scans before correction in the Tamil source.

The article makes strong historical and linguistic assertions about Sanskrit, Shruti, Smriti, caste restrictions, widow immolation, and Aryan migration. These are translated as Anna's polemical claims and arguments, not presented as independently verified contemporary scholarship.

## English Translation

“The respected jurors, having examined this conspiracy case with legal precision and impartiality, have wholeheartedly delivered their verdict. All thirty conspirators, including Petrashevsky, are guilty. Accepting that verdict, I sentence all thirty people accused in this conspiracy case to death by hanging for conspiring to endanger the life of His Exalted Majesty the Tsar, to overthrow the Russian government, and for delivering socialist speeches that disturbed public peace.”

With these words, the judge finished.

The Petrashevsky conspiracy trial, which began in April, received its verdict eight months later: death by hanging.

The young men in the dock, prisoners and conspirators, seemed not to understand what it meant. The judge experienced the delight of a man who had untangled a complicated knot. The daily nuisance of standing in court was over. But the death sentence... Very well, it had to happen. Only one more day. Tomorrow morning. After that, time would have no work for them.

When judgment was delivered, the court adjourned. By the following morning, life would depart from thirty bodies.

They were taken to prison. Only one night remained. They must have gathered and dispersed on many nights before. After this final night they would disperse, but could never gather again. They paid no attention to that. They did not pass their final hours weeping, spitting fire or telling stories.

They slept peacefully as usual. The guard did not allow them to sleep through the night. Before dawn he struck and awakened them. They were loaded into carts. A clattering sound; at last, a sharp stop. The carts halted.

The prisoners inside could not rise. Their legs had gone numb. Cold pressed into their bones. Sleep still hung from their eyes.

A great gate opened. The prisoners were led inside. A tall gallows welcomed them.

They were made to stand in a row. Two rough prisoners were tied to posts near the gallows. Names were read: Petrashevsky... one name... another... another... thirty names of one kind or another. It sounded as though they heard them in a dream.

They still seemed not to understand what sort of punishment this was. They stood like schoolboys assembled for morning physical exercise. They were young men of that age: twenty, thirty, thirty-five at most. In the most vibrant season of life, they studied and attempted to criticise society. Here was the blow of the law.

The first prisoner was led to the gallows. When he stood upon the box, he raised his hand towards his comrades. The other prisoners waved back. The noose encircled his neck.

Suddenly there was a sound. The box was pushed away and the prisoner swung.

No, wake and look carefully. The prisoner's feet were still upon the box. He had not yet been hanged. Hoofbeats sounded from behind. A figure on horseback waved a white cloth and opened and closed his mouth.

What strange spectacle was this? The rope around the prisoner's neck was loosened. He was summoned down alive. Alive! Why? Nothing could be understood.

After a moment the guard's voice was heard:

“The Tsar has pardoned you.”

“The Tsar has pardoned us... the Tsar... has... par...doned us. Ha, ha!” Laughing, the first prisoner ran. When those tied to the posts were released, they did not stand in line. They ran around and around.

Receiving a death sentence and being released at the final moment produced a profound mental shock among the prisoners. The first prisoner, who lost control of himself, was shaken upon hearing of the release and went mad. He spent the rest of his life in madness. Those tied to the posts lost their sanity.

The scene at the gallows would remain deeply imprinted upon the mind. The Russian author Dostoevsky is said to have broken down whenever he described the scene above, for he was one of the prisoners made to stand beside that gallows.

Dostoevsky was enrolled in a military school so that he might hold a high post in the Russian army. He passed the military examination and worked for one year. Though he worked in military engineering, all his attention was upon literature. The intellect that should have been spent building bridges and constructing buildings went instead into constructing verse and filling pages.

After his father died a year later, he left the job and began studying literature. At that time a novel written by Dostoevsky came to the attention of his friend. The friend took it to Belinsky, the incomparable critic who weighed and judged Russian literature.

Belinsky turned its pages indifferently. Some passages appeared good. The two sat and read throughout the night.

Before dawn the next morning, a knock was heard at the door. Dostoevsky rose and opened his eyes. “A new writer has appeared in Russia,” Belinsky declared, embracing Dostoevsky and praising him.

That book was published in 1846 under the title *Poor Folk*. The author was then twenty-five. A poor young man loves another poor young woman. Though she loves him, she marries an older wealthy man. Broken-hearted, the poor man kills himself. That is the story in brief. In this work Dostoevsky's literary understanding and his manner of portraying suffering created by circumstances begin to emerge.

Some months later, one day before dawn, the door of his room was knocked again. Dostoevsky rose and opened it. This time fame did not stand before him smiling. The police arrested him on a charge of conspiracy.

Dostoevsky was one of the friends of the socialist Petrashevsky. Petrashevsky was an excellent speaker. Through the force of his intense speeches, he attracted all the students of St Petersburg. Dostoevsky and his elder brother were among those who went to him.

As stated earlier, the death sentence was imposed and then commuted. Because he was not inclined to speak about himself, Dostoevsky did not describe the sentence in detail. But in a note he says:

> “Even in mature age, after I had passed fifty, when I suddenly awoke at midnight, the scene of the gallows would appear before my eyes and my hair would stand on end. Even if I tried to sleep again, I could not.”

The death sentence was commuted to hard imprisonment in the frozen wastes of Siberia. It was while living in Siberia that he had the opportunity to associate with criminals and could begin to understand the minds of those who committed crimes.

After suffering for four years, he could not return to Russia. As a mark of good conduct, he was appointed a soldier in a Siberian town. There he married a woman who lived in that place.

Through the efforts of his friend, he returned to Russia in 1859. He published *The House of the Dead*, a novel depicting life in Siberia. He described life in that frozen wasteland as “a living man buried and strong nails hammered into the coffin above him.”

The novel produced a powerful shock in Russia. Once again, life seemed likely to become orderly. But responsibility for maintaining his elder brother's large family and his wife, who suffered from illness, fell upon Dostoevsky. His wife too died. Two years later he married again.

His life, crooked from the beginning, did not straighten; it became wholly distorted. His debts increased, and under cover of midnight he fled the country.

He wandered through Paris and many other cities. His celebrated books appeared: *Crime and Punishment*, *The Idiot*, *The Gambler*, *Demons* and many other novels of worldwide fame. Yet his poverty showed no sign of ending. He would wait longingly at banks, expecting money to arrive from Russia.

Though separated from his motherland, his patriotism did not diminish in the least. He would go to a cafe and read Russian newspapers. Even if he ordered nothing, the attendant took pity and said nothing.

As one biographer writes:

> “While his wife suffered childbirth in the next room; while creditors threatened him for repayment; while the landlady, aided by the police, threatened him over unpaid rent; while the doctor demanded payment for medicine already supplied; and while epilepsy prepared to torment him, amid all these circumstances of suffering were written the great novels that shook the world, such as *The Gambler* and *The Brothers Karamazov*.”

Somehow managing to endure, Dostoevsky returned to Russia at the age of forty-nine. Little by little his fame grew. His speech at the Pushkin memorial celebration placed him at the summit of literature.

Turgenev spoke first that day. Dostoevsky spoke second. His words fell like a storm of hail. His speech completely captivated the gathering. When he finished, no one scheduled after him came forward to speak. The celebration ended there.

He did not live long afterwards. He died on 9 February 1881 at the age of sixty. A multitude greater than Petrograd had ever seen paid homage to the literary author who had introduced human beings to one another.

His coffin travelled in a magnificent procession. Tributes were sent from Russia's small towns, great cities and every remote corner. In the procession, Petrograd students carried the shackles that had been fastened upon Dostoevsky in Siberia. The united Russia for which he had laboured in literature paid homage at his grave.

Critics unanimously declare that among novelists who construct and write the workings of the mind, Dostoevsky is the greatest of all. Nothing equals his novel *Crime and Punishment*.

The novel ends with these words:

> “A new life is blossoming. Suffering and degradation disappear, and a future filled with joys never before seen or discovered appears before our eyes.”

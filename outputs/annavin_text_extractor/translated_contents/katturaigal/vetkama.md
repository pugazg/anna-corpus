## English Translation

Newspapers have reported that, at a place near the bank of the Mahanadi, the Orissa provincial government has planned to establish a factory for producing petrol from coal, a factory for manufacturing machine tools, and a factory for making scientific agricultural equipment. The Orissa government is obtaining assistance and advice for establishing these factories from British, American, and German industrial experts.

At the same time, however, no project has been initiated to employ the Madras Presidency's natural resources scientifically and increase public wealth.

More than two years have passed since reports appeared that iron ore exists in Salem district and lignite deposits in South Arcot district.

Those deposits are said to be abundant and the material obtainable from them to be of good quality.

We are encouraged to believe that if this natural wealth were used to establish large new factories, the Presidency's prosperity would grow.

We are also encouraged with statements that a plan for this very purpose is ready.

Ministers repeatedly stir expectation by saying that Delhi has been informed, advice has been requested, and permission is awaited.

Advice is freely offered that the government can no longer meet its growing expenditure and perform work beneficial to the public merely by imposing still more taxes; a way must be found to create new wealth, living standards must rise, and industrial development must therefore expand.

Even the eminent men who call talk of "Dravida Nadu" and "North India" poisonous recognize and openly say that industrial development in the South is inadequate.

Experts in mechanical industry such as Sir M. Visvesvaraya have insisted that it is wrong for India's large factories to be concentrated in a single region; that factories must be distributed throughout the country if its economic condition is to improve; and therefore that a new industrial plan must be prepared for the South.

Nevertheless, the natural wealth has so far continued to sleep. No large new factories have been started.

The Madras government asked that an iron industry be established in this Presidency using its iron ore and coal deposits, but no favourable answer has yet arrived from Delhi. Why?

We ask the divine personages who consider it wicked to say "Dravida Nadu": is this condition just for the Madras Presidency? Will it enrich life?

What remedy do they intend to discover? Why have they closed their eyes and become silent sages?

Even small provinces such as Orissa possess the capacity to initiate major industrial schemes and obtain the Delhi government's permission and assistance, while Madras is left to decay. Why should it remain a child raised by a stepmother? What will they do to change this condition? They call only the Dravidar Kazhagam's plan evil. Very well; when understanding comes, it will come. But what path will they now find to remove this condition? Why will they not speak? Is it shame, sorrow, or fear?

Will they simply declare that the Madras Presidency may become a rural backwater containing nothing but agriculture and petty cottage industries?

Do they imagine that Madras possesses no facilities for new industrial development?

Are they afraid that new factories in the Madras Presidency could not withstand competition from northern industry?

Or are they terrified that, if they emphasize and explain this problem, Delhi will say there is not much difference between Dravidar Kazhagam propaganda and their own speech?

Are they ashamed that, when they distinguish between southern and northern industrial development, we may say, "Sir, this is precisely the Dravida Nadu question we have discussed for so long"?

Or do they believe Delhi's grip is now too strong, that speaking about this problem is dangerous, and that they must wait for a more favourable time?

Have they submitted? Are they hiding? Is it fear or shame? What is the reason for leaving iron and coal asleep here while allowing poverty to dance?

20 March 1949.

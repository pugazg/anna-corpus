உத்திரவுகள் வந்தபடி, இருந்தன. இந்தி ஆதிக்கக்காரரின்

எல்லாம் அரசியல் சட்டப்படிதான் நடைபெறுகிறது

அச்சம் வளர்ந்தது. சட்டப்படி நடந்தால், இந்திதான் ஆட்சி

ஒரு தனிக்கட்சி நடத்துவது என்ற நிலையிலிருந்தும்,
அனைவரின் கவனத்தையும், ஆதரவையும் ஈர்த்திடும் பெரிய
பிரச்சினையாகிட உதவிற்று. மாணவர் எழுச்சி பீறிட்டுக்

## English Translation

India's Prime Minister Lal Bahadur announced that a conference of Chief Ministers was being arranged to formulate a two-language policy and to amend the Official Languages Act enacted in 1963 so that the policy might acquire legal form.

In view of the situation that has now arisen in the South, Kamaraj, President of the All India Congress, declared emphatically that the two-language policy promised by the late Pandit Nehru must be given a form capable of dispelling everyone's fears, by amending the Official Languages Act accordingly.

State Chief Minister Bhaktavatsalam expressed confidence that success would be achieved in giving legal form to the two-language policy. He also said that he had discussed the matter with legal experts and university vice-chancellors and had prepared a plan.

Union Ministers Subramaniam and Alagesan announced that, since the circumstances they had insisted upon had come into being, they were withdrawing their resignations.

It was announced that the Working Committee of the All India Congress would meet before the conference of Chief Ministers. President Dr Radhakrishnan, too, has referred to this matter in his address.

The form in which the conference of Chief Ministers, convened with the support of all these announcements, will fashion the two-language policy, and the degree to which that plan will prove satisfactory, depend upon the wording of the proposed amendment and the manner in which that law is enacted.

Leaving aside for the moment how far and whom this "arrangement" will satisfy, we must understand that the reasons which made this two-language policy necessary are extremely important.

The Constitution ordained that Hindi alone would be the official language of the Indian Union; it also stipulated that English should continue to be used for fifteen years from the commencement of the Constitution.

In accordance with the Constitution and the order issued under it by the President, the use of Hindi was steadily expanded and the place of English steadily reduced.

The growth of Hindi domination and the displacement of English created fear among non-Hindi-speaking people. In the South, and particularly in Tamil Nadu, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam conducted a peaceful anti-Hindi agitation and gave expression to this state of mind. The manner in which Hindi was imposed, directly and indirectly, in government departments lent strength to the Kazhagam's protest.

As the fear of the people of non-Hindi regions was turning into protest, Pandit Nehru, then Prime Minister, and President Dr Rajendra Prasad gave several assurances on behalf of the Government. The most important among them was the assurance Pandit Nehru gave in Parliament on 4 September 1959. That assurance acknowledged that non-Hindi-speaking people were anxious about Hindi becoming the official language.

Had the Constitution been allowed to take effect exactly as written, after 26 January 1965 Hindi alone would have become India's official language and English would have been removed altogether.

As the constitutional deadline of 26 January 1965 drew nearer, the fears of non-Hindi-speaking people increased. Many reasons were advanced to insist that replacing English with Hindi was wrong. Opposition to Hindi grew to a point where it could no longer be disregarded.

There was another consideration. Though the Hindi enthusiasts had written with great fervour, at the time the Constitution was framed, that after fifteen years Hindi alone would be the official language, and though they refused to grant English even the honourable designation of an official language, they too came to realize that English could not be eliminated altogether. India was not ready to entrust the entire responsibility of official communication to Hindi; non-Hindi-speaking people were not ready; the Hindi language itself was not ready.

Thus there were two obstacles to Hindi becoming the sole official language: first, the direct opposition to Hindi; second, Hindi's own inadequacy for the task. For both reasons, therefore, it became necessary to continue using English. It was this necessity that produced the Official Languages Act.

The Government claimed that provision had been made for the continued use of English and that Nehru's assurance was thereby being fulfilled. The DMK and others opposed to Hindi believed that merely permitting English to be used "if desired" would steadily reduce its use and make room for Hindi domination. They pointed out, for this reason, that Nehru's assurance had not been incorporated into the Act.

As 26 January 1965 approached, the imposition of Hindi gained new momentum. Government orders favouring Hindi kept appearing. The jubilation and pride of the champions of Hindi domination were openly visible.

The Government said that everything was being done in accordance with the Constitution. That statement did not lessen the fears of non-Hindi-speaking people; on the contrary, it increased them. It was clear that, under the law, Hindi was to be the official language. It therefore became equally clear that Hindi domination could be prevented only through legal safeguards. This realization helped transform the anti-Hindi agitation from the campaign of a single party into a great issue attracting everyone's attention and support. The students' uprising burst forth. It demonstrated that the South, the non-Hindi-speaking region, would not accept the removal of English and the installation of Hindi as the official language.

It was only at this point, when Tamil Nadu was surging with opposition, that its Chief Minister announced that he would "argue for a two-language policy and obtain statutory provision for it."

Prime Minister Lal Bahadur had continued to speak, issue statements and deliver radio addresses saying that nothing had suddenly changed after 26 January 1965 and that Nehru's promise was being fully honoured. Against that background, Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Bhaktavatsalam's assertion that "the two-language policy must be established by law" was not only noteworthy because it implied that the Prime Minister's explanation was insufficient; it also revealed that his own mind had been changing.

Never before had he said what he now said: that the two-language policy required legal form.

Chief Minister Bhaktavatsalam had habitually maintained that Nehru's promise was sufficient, that it was being implemented, and that he had complete faith that Lal Bahadur's Government would act according to it. His sudden insistence that the two-language policy must acquire a definite form must have startled Delhi circles and shaken Congress circles as well. This very Chief Minister now says that the Official Languages Act must be amended at the proper time to secure fulfilment of Nehru's promise, that the two-language policy must find a place in the law, and that the existing Act is inadequate. Yet when many people pointed out the Act's defects in 1963, he refused to accept their criticism and welcomed it with complete satisfaction. Now even he recognizes that only an appropriate amendment of the Official Languages Act can make the two-language policy effective in practice and fulfil Nehru's promise.

If such a change can occur in his position, there is nothing surprising in other Congress leaders rapidly moving toward the view that the law must be amended for a two-language policy.

I appealed to the students: You have displayed a spirited mobilization; everyone has praised your awakening as justified; through it the Indian Government has understood the strength of opposition to Hindi domination; the Government has now reached a point where some solution must be found. We must wait and see what this new approach yields and who undertakes the effort. Others must now be given the opportunity to attend to the issue. The students should therefore abandon the agitation. There must be no direct action. As soon as direct action begins, malignant forces that delight in ruining a pure movement will enter it. For that reason, I asked the students not to engage in direct action.

That statement did not have an immediate effect. Even now my heart trembles when I think of what subsequently happened in Tamil Nadu. Everything that ought never to have happened, everything that Tamil culture would never accept, took place.

Writing about it, the weekly edition of *Swadesamitran* observed that no one could fail to feel profound shock and deep sorrow over the dreadful battlefield Tamil Nadu had witnessed during the previous week. But sorrow alone was of no use, nor was it enough merely to offer sympathy to those who had suffered. The most important thing was to ensure that nothing of that kind ever happened again.

Investigating the causes of what occurred and continuing to trade accusations will serve no useful purpose.

Even in the face of repression, a condition in which violence does not arise must be created and preserved. If that appears or proves impossible, then however pure its purpose and however spiritedly it is conducted, the agitation must be stopped at once. That alone is the course that does not depart from morality.

It may be argued that violence was merely the consequence of repression having exceeded all bounds. That does not justify it.

Once violence has occurred, the Government gains an opening to take various measures on the pretext of protecting law and order. In this connection, however much anguish we feel over the arrest of Kazhagam Treasurer Karunanidhi under the Defence of India Act and the arrest of many Kazhagam comrades, we must remember that neither we nor our Kazhagam should lose composure, and that we possess the fortitude to endure anything.

What the country needs most urgently now is peace. Feelings of hostility must subside. The moral path must be protected. Every act and every word capable of causing disorder and fear in society must be avoided. People of every field and every kind have sustained heartbreaking losses in every way, and Tamil Nadu has been left reeling. Tears and blood are everywhere. However this condition may have arisen, no one can regard it as acceptable. Least of all can our Kazhagam, which for many years has conducted numerous moral agitations without causing injury to peace or order, life or property, and has demonstrated through its conduct that agitation is a pure service undertaken for the triumph of an ideal, support agitations that create conditions in which evil deeds can run rampant. It has never supported them.

Even if the Government refuses to accept our position and arrests many of us, Kazhagam comrades must remain calm and stand upon the moral path, confident that justice will prevail. This is my appeal, born from a wounded heart that has suffered repeatedly in contemplating what happened. Many others in the country have expressed the same thought. Tamil Nadu's appeal today is that everyone preserve peace and remain steadfast in morality.

Within the very Congress circles that had been saying both that no Hindi imposition existed and that Hindi alone must be the official language, an awareness is now growing that imposition does exist, together with a determination that it must not occur and a conviction that a non-Hindi-speaking region will not, and should not, accept Hindi as the sole official language. Everyone knows that many Congress leaders and organizations have expressed this view.

Congressmen who had until now denied and opposed the demand that Nehru's promise be given legal form to confirm and implement it, insisting that the promise alone was enough, have now begun to demand a law. In some municipalities, resolutions supporting the proposition that English must not be removed have been adopted without party distinction and with the support of Congress members. Each of these is a sign of a new tendency; taken together, they prove that a new tendency is indeed emerging.

The issue of opposition to Hindi domination has ceased to be merely the activity or agitation of parties opposed to the Congress. It has become a contest between those attempting to impose Hindi domination and the determination of the non-Hindi-speaking regions. This is an important new turn.

It is at this stage that the conference of Chief Ministers is meeting. Only hereafter will the form and operation of the two-language policy become known; only after they are known can we offer a detailed opinion upon it.

Even if the two-language policy is well designed and implemented in good faith, it will not permanently solve India's language problem. Yet when we observe that a new tendency has emerged, there is room for hope.

The obstinate position that Hindi alone would be India's official language and that English would under no circumstances be allowed to remain even as an associate official language has been broken. Repeated assurances had to be given that English would not be removed unless non-Hindi-speaking regions desired it. When it was insisted that such assurances could not be trusted unless embodied in law, the Indian Government came forward to amend the Official Languages Act. This shows that a new approach has emerged.

The proposal that Hindi and English should both function by law as official languages is itself only temporary. It is nevertheless one step ahead of the previous arrangement. It demonstrates that the Indian Government has reached, indeed has been compelled to reach, a point at which the voice of those opposing Hindi must be respected. The old dogma that there can be only one official language has been relaxed, and it has been accepted, at least in principle, that two languages may function as official languages. This shows that the old obstinacy is crumbling.

Unable any longer to disregard the scale and emotional force of the anti-Hindi struggle, the Indian Government has come down as far as accepting this two-language policy.

Eight years ago, when Rajagopalachari advocated this two-language policy, the Government refused to accept it. Now it has concluded that such a policy may serve as a compromise between the Hindi-speaking and non-Hindi-speaking regions.

Even if the two-language policy is expressed clearly in the statute and administered honestly in practice, it will not give complete satisfaction to the other non-Hindi-speaking peoples. Until every developed national language is accepted as an official language of the Union, no permanent and satisfactory settlement can be said to have been reached. Nevertheless, the fact that the agitation against Hindi domination has produced at least this much benefit will strengthen our resolve.

It causes deep pain that the agitation could not be conducted with discipline, without disturbing peace and order, without violence, without hostility arising between the police and protesters, without cruelties and brutal incidents, without destruction of public property, and without disorder and fear in society. Everyone knows that repression will be unleashed as soon as agitations begin. Because repression was allowed to become a reason for violence to raise its head, the very mention of agitation has now come to arouse distress. This is a profoundly regrettable condition. No one who has seen antisocial forces infiltrate and corrupt even the purest agitation, thereby harming society, can remain free of anxiety and alarm.

Thus, although the two-language policy is only an interim arrangement, although complete satisfaction with it is impossible, and although the permanent solution lies in making all national languages official languages, we must continue to work peacefully, honourably and with discipline, along Gandhian and moral lines, so that the success already obtained may open the way to the victories still to be won and to that permanent settlement.

If an interim two-language policy is given a sound form and implemented honestly, it will for the present prevent us from being caught all at once in the grip of Hindi domination, deprived of the aid of English, and reduced to hardship, humiliation and decline in every respect. Though it does not resolve the language problem and its complications, the two-language policy helps halt and restrain the Hindi domination that was immediately bearing down upon us with threats.

Once the two-language policy has acquired legal form, there will be an opportunity to comment further. For now, the Indian Government's movement from an obstinate position to one seeking compromise must be regarded as a notable victory. Had even this not been secured, Hindi would very quickly have driven English out, imposed its supremacy, and left us confounded. For the present, the danger has receded: a law is to ensure that English, a world language, continues as an associate official language. Hindi has not been removed as an official language; but the benefit we may expect from the two-language policy is that Hindi imposition and Hindi domination have been checked.

Although it is true that this is not wholly satisfactory and that a permanent remedy must still be found, we affectionately tell the students that there is no longer any need for them to continue the agitation. They should leave the issue to those who will continue to oppose Hindi domination, devise and pursue the means of finding a permanent solution to the language problem, and create conditions in which the work can proceed with discipline, without violence, and along moral lines.

We humbly appeal to the students not to take part in further agitations. All the journals that welcomed, praised and supported the students' love of language and their awakening have made the same appeal. There is no one who has failed to welcome the student community's announcement that the agitation is over. Lawyers, leaders in contact with the public, editors and the public at large have all become concerned with this issue.

Those who closely examine the speeches in Parliament will recognize that a new turn has occurred. Though some spoke irritably because Hindi domination was being broken, many responsible persons declared, with profound significance, that India must not be allowed to break apart through the imposition of Hindi: India is important, not Hindi.

The old cry was that Hindi was necessary to unite India. The question that has now arisen is: India or Hindi, which do we need? The answer given by most is that India comes first. We see that all the Chief Ministers have assented to this new turn.

When our colleague Manoharan explained our Kazhagam's policy clearly and categorically, the interruptions that arose were the trumpet blasts of those angered by the realization that their grip was loosening. They did not represent the opinion of the parliamentary majority.

Many Congress members who had previously devoted all their concern and skill to denouncing opponents of Hindi, and had been captivated by the winks of Hindi supporters, this time argued with feeling, explained Tamil Nadu's position, advised that merely blaming the DMK would not help solve the problem, declared emphatically that all Tamils opposed Hindi imposition, and insisted that the law must be amended so as to leave no room for doubt or fear. It is possible to feel both happiness and hope on seeing this.

Even people from states that may be regarded as entirely within the Hindi region have now understood the situation and come forward to alter their former approach.

Dinkar, an eminent scholar and poet in Hindi, a former member of the Rajya Sabha and now Vice-Chancellor of Bhagalpur University, has said that if making Hindi the official language would disrupt India's unity, even the plan to make it the official language must be abandoned.

Two professors working in Bihar emphasized the same point when they met me on 25 February 1965. They said: "We thought Hindi would create a sense of unity. We now understand the situation. Many responsible people in Bihar have come to believe that India's unity must not be damaged by Hindi." They cited Dinkar's speech as evidence.

In speaking with them, I said: "We bear neither enmity nor quarrel toward those who argue that Hindi should be made the official language. It is the Government's course that has simultaneously created anguish among both Hindi speakers and non-Hindi speakers. To Hindi speakers, this Government repeatedly spoke with confidence of Hindi becoming the official language in 1965, thereby kindling enthusiasm in their minds. At the same time, it repeatedly assured those of us in non-Hindi-speaking regions that Hindi would not be imposed and that English would continue without any time limit, thereby creating confidence in our minds. Today's crisis is the consequence of this practice of speaking in two voices."

The Bihar friends expressed satisfaction and then asked what the DMK's language policy was. I told them: "All the national languages must be given a place in the law as official languages. Since that will take time, English must continue as the official language until then. That is the Kazhagam's policy."

They asked, "Even after that, will not a link language be necessary? Which language will it be?"

I replied: "Once all the national languages become official languages, no one is going to cast English aside. Even those who demand that Hindi become the official language say that English must be learned. Let that English remain the link language."

They were not wholly satisfied. They asked whether it would not be better for one of our own languages to be the link language.

I said: "Time alone must determine whether one of the national languages should become the link language. That position must arise naturally. A language must not acquire it with the Government's assistance; the people themselves must decide."

They were pleased with that answer.

I see many events proving that a new turn is taking place. The effort of the Chief Ministers is itself a result of this new turn. After examining what legal form it now receives, I shall express my further views upon it. For the present, I am happy merely to point out that a new turn has occurred. The rest, later.

Your elder brother,

Annadurai

## English Translation

"In what language are the letters written?"

"In English..."

"Send them back at once."

"Long live Egypt! Down with British imperialism!" During the past few weeks, these revolutionary waves have competed with the waves of the Mediterranean Sea. Trumpets sound everywhere; everywhere there is the cry of battle.

Egypt, a small country, confronts Britain, which proudly struts as one of the world's great powers, and slaps its shoulders in challenge. The places where the British stay are set ablaze. Stones rain upon their offices. Loud cries ring out: "Run! Drive them out!"

"In Egypt?" the whole world asks in wonder upon hearing the Egyptian people's war drum. Egypt's Prime Minister, Nahas Pasha, sings a heroic song: "The stubbornness of British imperialism cannot defeat us. We will not permit King Farouk's influence to be destroyed."

A few weeks ago, he boldly proclaimed that Sudan, a part of Egypt, was united with Egypt and that both lands came under the rule of Egypt's King Farouk. Since 1899, Sudan, on the banks of the Nile, had been a beautiful land through which the British lion strutted proudly. Egypt claimed it as its own because those living in both countries were one people, Islamic brethren.

Egypt raised the question, "What work has a foreigner here? What empire have the English here?" and resolved that it would no longer remain submissive. Parliament endorsed the decision and annulled the long-standing Anglo-Egyptian Treaty. These measures were undertaken with the object of removing British troops from the Suez Canal.

Egypt's song of freedom has thrown Britain into confusion. The British government is stunned by the sound of the Egyptian people's hearts. Its confusion increased, its anger grew, and it has now reached the stage of threats. Troop numbers increase. Orders have been issued to the forces. Large numbers of aircraft and commanders are being concentrated at the mouth of the Nile.

These demonstrations have not subdued the Egyptian government's song of freedom. They have been like oil poured upon a burning fire. The more Britain blusters, the more the Egyptian people rise. Consequently, the flame of freedom burns ever faster.

Seventy thousand harbour workers employed under the English on the Suez Canal have stayed away from work. The enraged Egyptian people have begun to attack. The Egyptian government too has begun adopting various measures against Britain. It has commenced its rule in Sudan and increased guards and restrictions upon the English. Orders have been issued that the government will not consider letters written in English; they must be written in Arabic.

The British possess the distinction of having accomplished through intrigue and strategy what guns and cannon could not achieve. In the nineteenth century, they won many victories through diplomacy without a rifle upon the shoulder or shedding even a little blood.

They came in the guise of commerce and conquered the Indian subcontinent. In the same way, through diplomacy, they caused their Union Jack to fly over the Mediterranean region.

The Mediterranean is the palanquin of many empires. It is not merely a sea crossed by the ships of Western powers; it is also a meeting point between the civilizations of two different worlds. The waves of time have floated innumerable historical events upon the waves of that sea.

In the sound of the Mediterranean's waves, one hears history's song of social development. That sea knows the growth of civilization and the clashes of many civilizations.

When one recalls the fall of the many countries that touch and kiss that sea, the flood of time rolls before the eyes. Mounds of ash from vanished empires lie here and there.

How few could be the memories related by Egypt's pyramids, which stand as monuments to the fame of an ancient age? There is the land of the Greeks kissing the water. There is the Roman Empire, where Alexander, hero among heroes, raised cries of victory, telling us how many twists and turns accompanied his campaigns. There roamed Julius Caesar like a lion.

There is Egypt, home of the sweet-voiced Cleopatra, silently saying, "We nurtured a beauty and through her lost our very greatness." There is Bethlehem, saying, "Here was born Jesus, who came to sustain the world through love." There is the mouth of the Nile, saying, "Here the great warrior Napoleon was defeated; the valiant commander Nelson conquered him." And there is the beautiful island of Elba, saying, "He fought and lost. His sighs struck against me. Napoleon lived upon me."

In a Mediterranean region possessing so many distinctions, the British easily spread the shadow of their empire.

The fall of the Roman Empire, which once made the world tremble, carried the crown called "authority" to Western countries. Yet Egypt, where the waves of three continents leap and dance, did not fall for a long time. Through treaties, the British made even it their battlefield and stage of power.

The last world war helped them. Through it, Britain became the leader of many countries, including Iraq, Persia, Syria, Jordan, Palestine, Egypt, and Sudan. It also became guardian of small islands such as Malta and Cyprus. Britain shone like the radiant jewel of the Mediterranean, before which Arab lands bowed their heads.

Now the time when that unjust flame will be extinguished draws near. A lamp needs oil if it is to burn forever. Oil-rich Persia has sung a heroic song and expelled British domination. Iraq too, formerly under Britain and now a friendly country, sings a song of freedom. Palestine became independent two years ago, splitting into two countries. In little Jordan, the same condition prevails. Now Egypt beats the war drum.

The flowering of freedom in the Mediterranean region has startled the imperial powers. America and France are alarmed to see British influence fade. They fear that Russia, a neighbour of the Mediterranean, may become the region's friend. If Russian influence takes root there, the imperial countries will be unable to preserve their influence in Europe.

Because of this fear, America watches the Mediterranean with the greatest attention. Under the name of Mediterranean defence, America, Britain, France, Turkey, and other countries have joined to form a front. Mountbatten has been appointed commander of this alliance's naval forces.

Egypt refused to join the alliance. It explained: "The alliance must not possess forces exceeding the troops Britain is required to provide. That would appear to threaten us. Let Britain act justly."

Britain did not accept this reasonable explanation. Enraged that its influence was diminishing, it roars like a lion. Churchill threatens to send troops and aircraft to the Suez Canal.

If this condition develops, Egypt and the other Arab lands may unite, and the waters of the Mediterranean may turn to blood. The sea's waves may once again tremble beneath the cry of war.

Should that happen, the Russian power may come forward to cooperate with the Arab countries. The result could create a third world war.

If, through its lust for domination, Britain creates a condition in which communism and capitalism clash in the Mediterranean, the whole world will curse Britain. The tears of the Arab people will take revenge upon it.

British diplomats hold in their hands the formula for preventing this terrible condition.

Those who desire peace will welcome the Churchill government's adoption of a peaceful path. Otherwise, storms and volcanoes alone will dance across the world. May Britain avoid receiving that blame as its reward and instead preserve its honour.

*Dravida Nadu*, 4 November 1951.

## English Translation

As my friend Bharathan requested in last week's issue of *Dravida Nadu*, beginning this week I have come forward to write, under the title “Hinduism and the Tamils,” my thoughts on whether Hinduism is suited to the Tamil people.

We know that many Tamil people around us proudly imagine that Hinduism is their own. We also know that many among us have still not understood that Hinduism is a channel dug by the Aryans to keep the ship of their livelihood moving. It is therefore essential that all Tamil people learn and understand the truths about this Hinduism.

The period in which Hinduism spread through Tamil Nadu marks the period when the Aryans settled in our country and began exercising their dominance. There is not the slightest evidence for concluding that Hinduism, or any other religion, existed before the Aryans came here. No one endowed with a researcher's understanding would therefore come forward to call this Aryan religion, Hinduism, the religion of the Tamil people. The purpose of this essay is to demonstrate that Hinduism, introduced by the Aryans at a later time, cannot in any way be acceptable to the Tamil people, who had pursued a highly civilised life and lived by a just order for many thousands of years before the Aryans arrived. *Dravida Nadu* will publish this essay serially in parts.

Many explanations are now offered for the name of the religion called Hinduism. Some say it is Hinduism because it is the religion of the people of India; this is the view of the Western scholar Max Müller. Others say that because the Aryans lived on the banks of the Indus, they came to be called Hindus and followers of Hinduism. An Upanishad, however, insists that both explanations are false and gives what it calls the true meaning:

> “Himsāyām dūya deyasaḥ  
> Sa Hindu ityabhidhīyate.”

Its meaning is said to be: one who remains in suffering and is afflicted is called a Hindu. Ancient histories emphatically record that the Aryans wandered from place to place in distress, without shelter, clothing, or food. We must therefore accept as true the Upanishadic view that they were called Hindus. If one asks whether it is then improper to call the non-Aryan Tamil people Hindus, we answer that it is indeed improper. No history records that the Tamil people wandered from village to village in misery, not knowing how to conduct their lives. There is, by contrast, much evidence of the Aryans wandering and suffering. For fear of excessive detail, let us present only one or two examples. In hymn 42 of the first mandala of the Rig Veda, the Aryans appeal to their leader Indra as follows:

1. “Enable us to obtain wealth easily.
2. Lead us to pasture lands thick with grass.
3. Give us enough food to fill our stomachs.
4. O young calf of Suvitrā, O Lord, you help us in the battle fought to obtain land for us among the houses of Tukira.”

Since these Rig Vedic statements clearly reveal the poverty-stricken life of the Aryans, it is evident that the Aryan people alone are entitled to be called Hindus.

The same Rig Veda also tells us that the Tamil people enjoyed a prosperous life even before the Rig Vedic age. Let us present one or two examples. Hymn 104 of its first mandala says:

1. “Adorned with gold ornaments and gems, they [the Tamils] spread a covering that concealed this land.
2. Though they [the Tamils] penetrated and spread to the furthest limit of this land, they could not defeat Indra with their magical power.
3. Indra split their [the Tamils'] forts with his thunderbolt and reduced them to dust.
4. Both wives of the potter immerse their heads in milk! May they be plunged into the depths of the Siba River.”

These Rig Vedic statements clearly portray the Tamil people's prosperous life. The statement that “the potter's wives immersed their heads in milk” also shows that all Tamil people, without superiority or inferiority based on differences of occupation, equally enjoyed the pleasures their wealth could provide. From everything said thus far, it may be concluded without doubt that Tamil people cannot, for any reason, be called Hindus or followers of Hinduism. It is therefore certain that those who have ignorantly called them Hindus until now will no longer do so.

The distance between Kedaram in the Himalayan foothills in the north and Tiruvaiyaru near Thanjavur in the south is approximately 2,500 miles. We do not know how Tirunavukkarasar travelled so far without his footprints touching the earth. If it is said that he came submerged in water, without setting foot on land, we may leave it to geographers to determine whether a continuous waterway exists between Kedaram and Tiruvaiyaru. Yet it is deeply regrettable that believers in such Puranic fabrications do not at least ask how anyone could travel beneath a continuous stretch of water for 2,500 miles. Even experienced swimmers cannot cross five or ten miles of water. That Tirunavukkarasar, with no training or practice in swimming or underwater travel, completed a distance beyond even the dreams of the most skilled swimmers is a wonder beyond wonders. Could such an enormous falsehood be found anywhere except in religious books like those of Hinduism? The Aryans devised such stories to cast the Tamil people into the abyss of Hinduism and make them their slaves. Tamil people must at least now examine them critically and resolve to live according to nature.

There is another feature in this story to which Tamil people should direct their attention. It is said that a person came to the place where Tirunavukkarasar lay exhausted and unable to move and asked him, “Why have you come in anguish into this cruel forest, when all the organs of your body have wasted away?” Tirunavukkarasar replied, “I came wishing to delight in the sight of our god Siva seated with Parvati on northern Kailasa.” The stranger then said, “Can human beings reach sacred Kailasa, which even the gods find difficult to attain? What have you done by coming into this cruel wilderness? It is best that you turn back.” Tirunavukkarasar refused, saying, “I will not return without seeing Siva seated upon Mount Kailasa.”

Seeing his firmness, persistence, and courage, that person, said to be Siva in human form, disappeared and called, “Navukkarasa, rise!” At once all Tirunavukkarasar's wasted limbs were restored as before, and he rose in a beautiful form. He prayed, “Lord, grant me the sight of your divine form seated upon Mount Kailasa.” Siva replied, “Immerse yourself in this pond, emerge in the tank at Tiruvaiyaru, and there behold and worship the vision of us as we sit upon Kailasa.” So says the *Periya Puranam*. This latter portion of Tirunavukkarasar's story especially demands investigation and careful attention from the Tamil people.

Saiva texts name four leaders of Saivism, a branch of Hinduism: Tirunavukkarasar, Tirugnanasambandar, Sundarar, and Manikkavasagar. Tamil people must examine the truths set out here while remembering that Tirunavukkarasar was a Tamil, whereas the other three were Brahmins. Research indicates that Tirunavukkarasar lived more than a thousand years ago, and scholars conclude that the Aryans had settled in Tamil Nadu and begun exercising complete dominance more than two thousand years ago. Tirunavukkarasar thus appeared after the Aryan religion of Hinduism had taken root in Tamil Nadu. Though this Tamil son initially followed the doctrines of Saivism, he had acquired learning and mature understanding in his youth and realised that there was no truth in that Aryan religion.

Recognising Aryan-created Saivism as a false religion established for Brahmin livelihood, Tirunavukkarasar rejected it and embraced Jainism. He became deeply learned in Jain texts and rose to prominence among the Jains under the distinguished name Dharmasena. Seeing his intellectual acuteness and his rejection of Saivism, the Hindu Brahmins thought that a Tamil had arisen to destroy their livelihood and that they must either bring him under their control or eliminate him. Through many stratagems they made him return to Saivism. Yet our Tamil hero Tirunavukkarasar only presented himself as a Saivite out of fear of Aryan cruelty; he did not acquire a sincere attachment to Saivism. He did so to please his sister Tilakavathiyar, who had been prompted by Brahmin intrigue. Since their parents were dead and he had no other relatives, he thought it improper suddenly to reject her and outwardly behaved, at her bidding, so that others would take him for a Saivite.

The Brahmins, skilled in intrigue, somehow perceived his inner disposition. Seeking to instil in him genuine attachment and faith in Saivism, they extravagantly praised Tirugnanasambandar, a Brahmin child of their own lineage at Sirkazhi. They said that at the age of three he had seen Siva face to face, drunk the breast milk of Siva's wife Uma, and was performing divine wonders. Tirunavukkarasar wished to see this child, went to Sirkazhi, and met him.

As the saying goes, the future crop is known from its sprout. Though still a child, the Brahmin Tirugnanasambandar immediately distinguished himself and his community from the Tamil people by telling Tirunavukkarasar, “Appar, sing of your god.” Tamil people, behold this Brahmin mischief! Does a young fish need to be taught to swim? Though Sambandar was a child, he knew that he was a Brahmin and, accordingly, that the god he worshipped was different; hence he asked Navukkarasar to sing of “your god.” Tamil people must consider carefully why this division between “your god” and “our god” arose there.

Even today, as a sign that Siva is a Brahmin god, worship and acclamations at Siva temples are conducted in Sanskrit during festivals. The *Tevaram* and *Tiruvasagam*, proclaimed the Tamil Veda by certain sham Tamils, possess not even a grain of honour or influence in those temples. Only after Brahmins have worshipped with Sanskrit mantras, received the sacred ash, and departed is the Tamil Veda, the *Tevaram*, recited, like one waiting to eat another's leftovers. Yet some Tamils shamelessly claim that Siva himself supplied the opening lines for all the *Tevaram* and *Tiruvasagam* hymns. If Siva were truly a Tamil god and had himself initiated these works, would he give first place to Sanskrit mantras that neither he nor his Tamil worshippers understand? Tamil people must at least now reflect, escape this Hindu snare, and strive to live with self-respect.

Tirunavukkarasar remained with Tirugnanasambandar for many days, accompanying him wherever he went and trying to discover whether Saivism held any truth. He gained nothing except the task of carrying Sambandar's palanquin. What else could Tirunavukkarasar, a Tamil who joined a Brahmin group, expect but the labour they assigned him? What true Tamil's blood would not boil on reading a Purana which says that an educated and elderly Tamil carried the palanquin of a Brahmin child not yet three years old?

After visiting Tirugnanasambandar at the Brahmins' urging, Tirunavukkarasar left him and went alone to many temples to observe what was being done in the name of Hinduism. In Siva temples run by Brahmins, whose aim in establishing Hindu doctrines throughout Tamil Nadu was to degrade Tamil people, he witnessed cruelties and attempted reforms to protect Tamil dignity. What did he gain? At the Siva temple called Tiruthunganaimadam he saw Tamil people treated with great contempt. Grieving over the Brahmins' ignorance, he tried to reform them with the light of his understanding. Enraged, the temple Brahmins cried, “Is a Tamil Sudra fellow coming to teach us wisdom?” They took a trident from the temple, heated it red-hot, and branded Tirunavukkarasar's shoulder. This incident appears in the *Periya Puranam*.

But the customary strategy of Puranic fabricators entered this account too. To conceal the humiliation inflicted on Tirunavukkarasar by the Brahmins and invest it with divinity, they skilfully inserted the fiction that Siva himself marked Tirunavukkarasar's shoulder with his trident to remove the pollution acquired through his conversion to Jainism. They made everyone believe it, and many irrational people believe the story even today.

If that great god called Siva branded Tirunavukkarasar's shoulder with a trident to remove his pollution, can we believe he possessed even a little of the divinity religious fanatics attribute to him? Religious texts say that God can remove everyone's impurities with a single sacred glance, and can accomplish anything merely by willing it, without the aid of anyone or anything. Would any reader who uses reason accept that Siva nevertheless had to take his trident, heat it in a fire, and brand Tirunavukkarasar's shoulder to remove his pollution?

Moreover, how can changing from one religion to another create the indignity called *tittu*, or pollution? The monthly flow of blood experienced by women is also called pollution. Does anything comparable occur in people who leave one religion and enter another? Evidently not. Many Saivites in places such as Jaffna and Tirunelveli have embraced Christianity. To remove a pollution supposedly caused by conversion, do Jesus, the Christian god, or his Father said to reside in heaven brand them with crosses or other religious symbols? They do not. Just as the Aryans pretend to purify Hindus through *diksha*, Christian priests perform something under the name of baptism and rejoice that religious pollution has thereby disappeared. What folly! The embellishments called initiation and baptism are strategies by which religious Aryans conduct their religious commerce.

The cruel scheme called pollution is a meaningless system brought by the Aryans and implanted in Tamil Nadu. Before a people whose fundamental belief admits no superiority or inferiority by birth, it is laughable to claim that leaving one religion and entering another causes pollution and that rituals such as initiation, branding, and baptism are needed to remove it. The Tamil word *tittu* means uncleanliness; it can never properly be applied, as religious teachers do, to people who change religions or undertake similar acts. Dirt or uncleanliness may be removed by bathing, immersion, or washing, but not by branding someone with a trident heated in fire.

Tirunavukkarasar understood that one cannot learn the truth of anything beyond doubt without adopting patience as one's instrument. Enduring what he had suffered in the name of Hinduism and wishing to observe further Brahmin cruelty, he visited more Siva temples. At the temple called Tiruchathimutram he saw the Brahmins committing many improper acts and tried to reform them. The local Brahmins wished to teach him a lesson but, perhaps because they lacked influence there or for some other reason, did nothing beyond telling him, “Come to Nallur; we shall deal with you there.” When he reached Tirunallur, they beat him severely, kicked him on the head and elsewhere, and drove him away. Unable to bear these cruelties in silence, Navukkarasar expressed them in a *Tevaram* containing the lines, “He caused devotees who melt in contemplation to be beaten ... He placed the rain-drenched sacred feet upon my head ...”

The meaning Saivite devotees assign to “He caused devotees who melt in contemplation to be beaten” does not accord with reason. They say: “It is Siva's nature to subject devotees who contemplate and worship God with melting hearts to suffering.” Yet religious texts describing God's nature say that God has eight qualities, one of which is the power to know everything by himself. If a god must inflict suffering upon his devotees and only afterwards grant them bliss in order to learn their inner purity and test whether they remain steadfast in pain and pleasure, we cannot see how that god is superior even to people said to possess little understanding. That a god credited with natural knowledge and omnipotence must inflict ever more suffering on sincere devotees merely to discover their true condition is a farce.

Some may object that God does not test devotees for his own knowledge, but performs this divine play to show the world the abundance and firmness of their love and guide others along the same path. There is an answer for them too. Human beings, deemed inferior to God in every respect, punish an offender before others so as to reform him and deter others from committing the same offence. If the supreme intelligence called God employs this same method, what difference remains between him and other living beings? We do not know. Those who claim to know God, or who believe unsupported arguments for God's existence, must resolve these doubts. They must distinguish divine nature from human nature and explain the difference, the superiority and inferiority, between God and living beings.

Instead, devotees become enraged at a rationalist who asks why God is superior if he merely performs the little deeds human beings perform. They offer the evasive answer that God may do anything and that mortals must not interfere with his deeds. Those deceptions can no longer preserve the blind convention called divinity before thoughtful people. Those bewildered by the spectre of religion should at least now recognise this and strive to live humanely.

There is no ignorance greater than claiming that one may follow the course prescribed by religious books to reach a beginningless and endless First Cause and obtain the bliss it grants. It resembles a story in which the son of a barren woman uses a hare's horn as a ladder and plucks a flower from the sky. God resembles the barren woman's son; the path described by religious books resembles the hare's horn; and the bliss God grants resembles the flower in the sky. What has been said thus far is a brief explanation concerning God. For those still unconvinced, a fuller explanation may be given if desired. Let those who doubt ask.

Among the many wonders recounted by the *Periya Puranam* is this: when the Brahmin child Tirugnanasambandar was three years old, Siva arrived with his dear wife Uma, mounted on the bull, and stood in the sky. Pointing to Sambandar crying beside a tank at Sirkazhi, he told Uma, “Look there! Our dear child stands crying in hunger. Express your breast milk into a golden bowl, take it to him, and satisfy his hunger.” Uma did so.

Suppose a rationalist asks a devotee who accepts this event literally: “Dear man, devotee who never forgets Hara's feet, virtuous worshipper of the ancient and perfect Siva, did you personally see Uma obey Siva's command, express her milk into a golden bowl, and give it to Tirugnanasambandar? Did Sekkizhar, author of the *Periya Puranam*, see it? Did Nambiyandar Nambi, author of the *Kalitturai Tiruvantati* that served as Sekkizhar's source, see it? Or did the stone image Pollap Pillaiyar of Tirunaraiyur, said to have told Nambiyandar Nambi the Puranic stories, see it?” The devotee replies, “You are an atheist. One should not even look upon your face. You belong to the group that says there is no God. You appear to read *Kudi Arasu*, *Viduthalai*, and *Pagutharivu*. Your question is insolent. I shall not answer you. Go away!”

It is true, as he says, that I am an atheist, belong to the group that says there is no God, and read *Kudi Arasu*, *Viduthalai*, and *Pagutharivu*. But I gladly accept anything consistent with reason and nature, whoever says it, whichever newspaper publishes it, and whichever book contains it. I do not share the narrow mentality that says, “Ah, is he an atheist? One must not look at his face. Does he belong to the group that denies God? One must not even speak with him. Does he read *Kudi Arasu*? Associate with him and the whole household will be ruined.”

Some days ago I travelled from Kanchipuram to Tiruvarur, where Siva is worshipped as Tyagaraja. The *Periya Puranam* says that he appeared before the Chola king who killed his own son beneath the wheel of a chariot because that chariot had run over a calf, and restored the calf, the prince, and the minister to life. I saw Tyagaraja's chariot lying in the street with a broken axle and the god himself suffering within it. I asked the people there: “What a reversal of the times! Why does the three-eyed great Siva, who removes the afflictions of everyone in the world and grants them welfare, lie helpless in the street unable to remove his own distress?”

They replied, “The wrath that seized our omnipotent Siva caused the chariot to break apart.” I respectfully asked: “How did Siva become angry? Why? At whom? At the people of this town? At the Japanese trying to invade India? At the British who refuse India self-rule? At our friend Rajagopalachari, who supports the Muslims demanding partition? At our leader Ramasamy Periyar, conducting a great agitation for a separate Dravidian country? Or perhaps at people like us who say there is no God? At whom, and why? How did you learn that Siva was angry? Did you see God in the chariot? Did God come and say, ‘I myself broke the chariot axle because of my anger,’ or inform you in writing?”

The devotees said, “Oh! We thought he was somebody, but he is an absolute atheist. If Siva learns that we spoke to him, he will break the remaining chariot axles too. We must not even look upon his face,” and slowly slipped away one by one. A few remained, glaring at me. I addressed them with a humble preface: “Gentlemen! Companies of devotees who worship Hara's feet! Cast off your anger. Use a little reason and hear what I say. You call me an atheist merely for speaking this way. These questions were not asked by me or raised by *Kudi Arasu*. Your own *Dinamani* asked them on 21 May 1942. What do you say to that?”

A Congress supporter stepped forward and angrily declared, “*Dinamani* speaking atheism? What you say is a complete lie.” I handed him my copy and asked him to read it himself. He read:

> “A strange reason is offered for one country ruling another. They are said to govern that country as trustees on God's behalf. No one knows where they met God, or whether God gave them authority orally or in writing. This is the deception now being practised.”

The Congress supporter then left without a word. Some bystanders walked away remarking that this too was a change brought by the times; others wondered aloud whether even *Dinamani* could lose its bearings so badly.

Friends! Although *Dinamani* had worked with Aryan dominance as its sole objective, its editor was a Tamil. The force of the Tamil people's rationalist awakening will rouse every Tamil; one day Tamils will unite and become comrades of the Self-Respect Movement. I inserted this incident under the title “Hinduism and the Tamils” to make that truth known.

Tirunavukkarasar began a *Tevaram* with “He caused devotees who melt in contemplation to be beaten” and ended it, “Our Lord of Nallur placed his sacred feet upon my head—how good indeed!” I have already explained that a god said to possess boundless love and compassion would contradict the very definition of divinity by beating sincere devotees. The direct meaning of “He placed his sacred feet upon my head” is that God kicked Tirunavukkarasar on the head.

Religious leaders' claim that the “sacred foot was placed upon his head,” instead of openly saying that God kicked Tirunavukkarasar, is one of their stratagems. Those who support their livelihood by claiming that nonexistent things exist commonly use such deceitful language. The difference between saying that God kicked Tirunavukkarasar and that God placed his sacred foot on Tirunavukkarasar's head is like the difference between saying that a man resembles an ox and saying he resembles a cow. Just as *pasu* and *madu* denote the same thing, placing the sacred foot on the head and kicking denote the same act.

Why did Tirunavukkarasar conceal the suffering inflicted by Brahmins beneath an indirect meaning? It is natural to Tamil character to endure harm and return good, and, when it becomes necessary to tell others of the harm, to describe it gracefully in tranquil words that do not inflame the listener while still conveying its essential meaning. Being a true Tamil, Tirunavukkarasar knew that the suffering Brahmins inflicted was not his alone but tormented Tamil people like him. Rather than treating it as a private matter and silently enduring it, he deemed it essential that all Tamil people understand Brahmin cruelty, and therefore expressed it through concealed meaning.

“Behold the Aryan; behold the Tamil,” our Navukkarasar said a thousand years ago. Though the distinction between Aryan and Tamil arose a thousand years ago, our people still do not grasp its truth. Who can deny that the reason is this Hindu religion that binds us and the Aryans together? Were it not the Puranas and epics written in Hinduism's name that made Aryans “gods on earth” and enslaved Tamil people to them? Did not law created in Hinduism's name prescribe one justice for each caste: shaving a Brahmin's head as sufficient punishment if he killed someone, but death for a Tamil who killed someone?

Was it not Hinduism that placed Tirunavukkarasar, a Tamil, in the position of a hired labourer and made him carry a weeding implement? Was it not Hinduism that placed the Brahmin Tirugnanasambandar in the position of a minor king and enabled him to ride in a palanquin? We cannot avoid saying that the mature and learned Navukkarasar was kicked by Brahmins because he had embraced Saivism, the Hindu religion. Even today, many intellectually distinguished Tamils remain Aryan slaves and accept the Aryan claim that they are *Sat-Sudras*.

There was another reason Tirunavukkarasar could not speak openly about the suffering Brahmins inflicted upon him. At that time, the Aryans used their intrigues to destroy Jainism and Buddhism, religions that cultivated love and knowledge, and spread a Hinduism filled with killing and cruelty through Tamil Nadu. They made the Tamil kings of the time follow it too. As the ruler goes, so go the subjects. With royal support and influence, Aryan Brahmins possessed complete authority to punish those who acted contrary to Hindu doctrine. It is therefore no wonder that the Brahmins could kick and beat Tirunavukkarasar as they pleased when he tried to reform them after witnessing their cruelties.

To make people believe that Tirunavukkarasar's sufferings were not caused by Brahmins, but that Siva placed his sacred foot upon Tirunavukkarasar's head to purify him, they altered the story and wrote it in this form. Those Brahmins even made Tirunavukkarasar himself sing a hymn about it. Yet Navukkarasar, believing it improper for a true Tamil to conceal what had actually happened, inserted the Brahmins' cruelties into the *Tevaram* hymn like a fruit hidden among leaves. Though a Tamil, fearing royal command and Aryan cruelty, was forced to act against his convictions, we cannot but praise Navukkarasar for conducting himself in a way that prevented the truth from being destroyed.

(Published on 5 March, 26 April, 3 May, 10 May, 17 May, 31 May, and 7 June 1942 under Anna's pen name “Nakkiran.”)

The Sanskrit verse and the Rig Vedic proper names “Suvitrā,” “Tukira,” and “Siba” are transliterated conservatively from the corrected Tamil source; the cited editions have not been independently identified.

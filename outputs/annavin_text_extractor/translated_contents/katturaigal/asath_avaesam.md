## English Translation

Is it really he who speaks like this? Is he not the very embodiment of calm, a man capable of behaving agreeably towards everyone? How angrily he speaks! Sparks fly from his eyes. He cries out. Tears well in his eyes. What harsh words he hurls!

Looking at the Congressmen, he declares bitterly, “It was because people like you existed that Pakistan separated!” He is not merely speaking; he is roaring. Where had he suppressed so much anger all these years? The speech makes one start as though a sleeping tiger had slapped its thigh and sprung up.

“I determined the course of my political life forty-six years ago. I have lived accordingly. Only a short time remains. I await death. I am a spent corpse! I have no need to desire anything,” he cries, tears flowing.

Ah, what a fine speech! A proper whip for the Hindi fanatics! “Azad, achchha hai!”—“Azad, well said!” In a manner that filled members with wonder and delight, India's Education Minister Abul Kalam Azad thundered in the Delhi chamber on the 29th.

Even while reading his speech, one sees sparks and a volcano. Such anger—and from Azad! Against whom? Against the opposition? No, no. Against members of his own party, the Congress.

He asked them, in substance: “Gentlemen! It was because of men like you that India was partitioned. Do you know that? You displayed a narrow disposition and a perverse mind. You possessed no generosity. India was divided. Why has your thinking still not changed?”

Azad is ordinarily a calm man. Those who provoked such irritation and anguish in him were former All-India Congress president Purushottam Das Tandon and Seth Govind Das.

Both are said to have levelled an accusation against Azad:

“Azad pays no attention to Hindi. He spends government money and his own time only on developing Hindustani.”

It was upon hearing this that Azad roared like a lion. He also used a Hindi expression that the Tamil source prints uncertainly as “பர்பாரேப் - தக்காயுள்.” The article explains it as meaning that they were carrying public opinion along a false path. Azad reportedly flung such harsh Hindi words in Parliament while answering them. Later, by order of the House, he withdrew the expression. The accusation had caused him that degree of mental pain and fury.

You may ask what offence there is in the charge, “You do not spread Hindi; you spread Hindustani.” Many among us still do not know the difference between Hindi and Hindustani. Yet Azad's anger shows that the distinction, and the mistaken impressions arising from it, are enormous. Otherwise, would he lose his usual composure? Would he hurl a severe expression and then withdraw it?

Had they merely accused him of failing to spend money on Hindi, Azad might have answered calmly. But that was not the accusation. They charged him with spending money on Hindustani.

How could Tandon and his companions accept that? Who is Azad? To you, he is Gandhi's disciple, a commander of the freedom movement, the man who was Congress president when the country gained freedom from British rule, and a lion who went to prison. But to Tandon, despite all those sacrifices, despite labouring for the Congress for forty-six years without leaving its camp, and despite disregarding Muslim reproaches in order to remain faithful to his chosen policy, Azad is a Muslim.

You may say that both belong to the same party and are Congressmen. What if they do? Azad is a Muslim. Tandon will not surrender his prestige. That is why he pounced. One party, yet such a division! That is the beauty of “that place.”

Azad is a Muslim, Tandon argues, and therefore shows attachment to Hindustani. Hindustani can be written in letters like those of Urdu. Consequently, granting money to develop Hindustani amounts to assisting Urdu. How can this be accepted?

Hindi uses the Devanagari script. This, Tandon says, is the Indian language. Instead of developing it and spending abundant sums upon Hindi associations, giving money for Hindustani means helping Urdu. How can that be accepted? Thus Tandon roars.

Is this an ordinary accusation? Consider Azad's sacrifices. Even today the Muslim world mocks him when it hears his name, yet he remains within the Congress and performs his duty. Tandon smears this stain upon him. Could Azad bear it? Who could? That is why, with tears flowing, anger bubbling and anguish rising, he used that harsh expression. “Is this charge levelled against me? What remains for me now?” he roared.

Azad is no weakling; he has endured hardship and loss. Yet this accusation was made against him by a Congress member. This crooked judgement and capricious mind are the private property of the northern Indian Congress leaders. We do not wish to explain that here. We ask instead that readers see what this quarrel reveals.

We do not readily understand the difference between Hindustani and Hindi. There, however, it has become a dangerous controversy. Even so, those seated upon India's throne employ every conceivable method to spread that language throughout the country. Tandon's anger arises from his belief that those efforts have not been pursued intensely enough.

At Wardha, where Gandhi had his residence, there was reportedly a Hindustani association founded by Gandhi himself. Azad gave it a grant of Rs. 60,000. Another association, praised by Pandit Nehru and blessed by Gandhi, published valuable books in Urdu. Azad gave it Rs. 40,000. “Why?” Tandon asks, framing the question as an accusation.

“What does it matter whether Gandhi or Nehru approved? Why give financial assistance to those languages?” asks Tandon.

Such is the fanaticism ruling them. On seeing it, Azad flared up:

“You wander about seeking to suppress and crush other languages. It was because you behaved this way that Pakistan separated. You ask why Urdu and Hindustani should receive financial assistance. There are forty-five million Muslims on Indian soil who speak those languages. Is it your intention that their language should be neglected?”

While emitting this fire of anger, Azad also revealed a truth concerning us.

Is not an encyclopaedia being prepared in Tamil? The people of Madras had applied for financial aid for it in 1942. Northern Indian members reportedly raised an outcry against the application.

> The government had decided to give a grant of Rs. 80,000
> for its completion. But a lot of noise was created against it. The
> sentiment was the same, namely, why give money for anything
> that did not relate to Hindi.

“The government had even decided to grant Rs. 80,000. But a great outcry was raised against it. The reason was the feeling that money should not be given to any language unconnected with Hindi,” says Azad.

A tremendous protest was raised. Where? In the north. Azad condemns that mentality and points to such a disposition as the cause of Pakistan's separation. We are glad that Azad disclosed the truth.

People with this mentality exist in northern India, holding that no language other than Hindi should be allowed to develop. Azad condemns and rebukes their conduct. He sheds tears upon seeing that their fanaticism has grown to the point of casting blame upon him.

At the same time, however, he declares that within another twelve years Hindi will somehow come into force in the records and procedures of the Delhi government.

Hindi is to reign in twelve years. Where? As the official language of the Indian subcontinent, where Hindustani and Urdu contend on one side, Bengali and Sindhi stand on another, Tamil and Telugu on yet another, and many languages are in living use.

Our friend Azad says so. Having replied to Tandon by pointing out that linguistic fanaticism caused Pakistan's separation, Azad finally makes this declaration too.

The Constitution of India laid down a decision to spread Hindi over fifteen years. Three years have now elapsed. In another twelve years, it is to become the language of the palace.

What astonishes us is that the very man who spoke the truth that India was divided because of linguistic fanaticism also announces this.

Tandon's desire to suppress every other language and seat Hindi, his own language, in a position of rule over the land is called linguistic fanaticism. Azad condemns it. But what kind of passion is it to declare that the same language will become the official language in another twelve years?

Some may dislike the word “fanaticism.”

For Pandit Nehru too has been condemning this fanaticism. Yet he says precisely what Azad says.

These men proclaim the lesson that Pakistan emerged because linguistic domination was imposed. Why, then, do they show such obstinacy only in the matter of Hindi?

The master and the disciple condemn linguistic fanaticism, but remain deeply committed to making that very language the official language. They call their own path an attempt to do it with goodwill.

We are told that once this goodwill arises, everyone in the Indian subcontinent will accept Hindi.

Fanatics stand on one side. Those with subtle intelligence stand on the other.

Thus both camps labour for Hindi. Both desire somehow to make it the official language.

But truths race past them. One such spectacle is Azad's fury: the stage at which his own colleagues accuse him. Azad is a former Congress president. So is Tandon. The two clash over the language question and attack one another bitterly. Even then, Azad ultimately advocates Hindi to this extent. How long can that position last?

Today, the quarrel is over a small matter.

Tomorrow...!

*Dravida Nadu*, 4 April 1954.

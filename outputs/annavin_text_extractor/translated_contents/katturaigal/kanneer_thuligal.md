அலங்கார விளக்குகள், அழகிய முகப்பு - கூடியுள்ள
லட்சக்கணக்கான மக்கள் - இவை தெளிவாக விளக்கும்.
இங்கு குழுமியுள்ள உறுப்பினர்கள் என்னிடத்திலும், கட்சி
கருத்துகளிடத்தும் காட்டும் ஆர்வத்தைக் காண மகிழ்கிறேன்.
அவர்களுக்கு நன்றி செலுத்துகிறேன்.

என்னைப்பற்றிப் பலர் புகழ்ந்துரைத்தார்கள்.

அருமைத் தம்பிமார்கள், அண்ணா கட்டளையிடுங்கள்,
கடமையைச் செய்கிறோம், என்று அடிக்கடி
வற்புறுத்தினார்கள். நான் கட்டளையிடுபவனல்ல, அத்தகைய
நிலையிலே உள்ளவனுமல்ல! சிலர், என்னை வழிகாட்டுங்கள்,
வாகைசூடி வருகிறோம் பாருங்கள் என்றார்கள். நான்

## English Translation

If anyone had any doubt or suspicion whether the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam would grow or perish midway, this conference, the enormous pavilion erected for it, the decorative lights, the handsome frontage and the hundreds of thousands assembled here will give them a clear answer. I rejoice to see the enthusiasm that the members gathered here show toward me and toward the party's principles. I offer them my thanks.

Many have spoken in my praise. I regard all those remarks merely as loving words of affection cast in beautiful Tamil, for I am not worthy of such tributes. My dear younger brothers repeatedly urged me, "Anna, give us your command; we shall perform our duty." I reminded them that I am not one who commands, nor am I in such a position. Some said, "Guide us and see us return crowned with victory." I remind them that I am no guide either. "Guide" is a word that came through religion and carries an orthodox odour.

During the crisis two years ago everyone expected that the Dravidian movement would certainly be scattered and smashed into a hundred pieces. But your faith has triumphed. The unity you maintained has grown into this great force today. I am elated to see your attachment and affection for me. I am bound by that affection, but I am neither a commander nor a guide. Until now I have conducted myself without betraying the trust you placed in me. I pledge that I shall do so in the future too.

I believe in democracy and desire dignity in politics; I am not one who issues commands. In this age when democracy has developed, commanding leaders cannot and must not arise. Without democracy, politics has no benefit. I do nothing by myself, nor will I. I listen to the views of many among you, gather and consider them, and then reach a conclusion accordingly. It is only by trusting your tremendous strength that I have accepted and carried the great responsibility entrusted to me. I work with immense patience.

Hundreds of thousands of us are assembled in this vast pavilion. I would say that all this feeling, impetus and awakening is alms bestowed upon us by our former leader Sir P. Theagaraya. Had that man, born into a wealthy family, not roused the declining Dravidian community, we could not have grown so greatly. Had he not raised his stormy voice not merely in India but in England too, we could not have grown. Many leaders fought then for the Dravidian community. At this moment I offer them all my heartfelt homage.

At this moment I also remember leader Periyar. Our comrade Jeeva is on this very platform. Today we work in different movements. But I believe that a day will come when Periyar, Jeevanandam and I will work from one place. I am not a shopkeeper in a marketplace of principles, and neither are they. I will not abandon my principle, and I must not. Yet a common camp where all three meet is bound to arise. Let it be a communist camp or be named a socialist camp; I do not mind. But the qualifier "Dravidian" must be added to it. Call it the Dravidian Communist Party and I will be its first member. Call it the Dravidian Socialist Party and I will be its first member. Let it be the Party of Dravidian Radicals, or the Dravidian Revolutionary Movement; I have no objection. I will be the first member of each. But if the word "Dravidian" is absent, I will not be there.

I look around this pavilion. I see the radiant lamps and you who give them light: your eyes, and the fire that surges within those eyes. I rejoice and stand astonished for a moment. Is all this our doing? Did we accomplish all this? The forecasts of those who prophesied about us have proved false. Those political astrologers will surely be heartsore when they see it. From this we learn that what they possess is a dictionary of haste, not a dictionary of experience.

Around the pavilion fly the flags of our branch organizations. Our Kazhagam exists in Anaimalai and at its foothills; in mountain country, valleys, passes and plains; amid flower gardens; in the wet fields of Thanjavur; in famine-haunted Ramanathapuram; and throughout every other district. The flags of our branches have come from all these places. They have also come from territories across the sea, from Ceylon, Burma and Indochina. We are thrilled by the sight of them flying. But some are amazed, some afraid, and others suspicious. They are tormented by uncertainty over what this movement may do and what it may lead to. I tell them clearly: understand that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is a companion of dignity, a friend of principle and the camp of the Dravidian people.

I say that communism needs a boundary. I say that common ownership can be spread within the boundaries of Dravida Nadu. We shall certainly attain that boundary: one that troubles no one, that our ancestors drew with their blood, that we later lost through foolishness, that deceivers seized from us, and that our awakening must regain. "A Home, Land for Our Nation," they say. Attaining the Dravida Nadu belonging to the Dravidian people is the fundamental objective of the D.M.K.

We walk along the path of liberation toward that objective. Problems arising during the journey are like friends met on the road. We will speak with them as we walk and then part from them, but we will not leave the path. If rain falls, we will take shelter for a little while; we will not retire. We will continue walking. Snow, rain, heat, cold, night or day will neither halt the journey nor change the path.

Corpses may lie on the road and skeletons may be there. Vultures may circle around them and the howling of jackals may be heard. But we will not lose our path. We shall walk on, regarding the corpses and all the rest merely as signs that heroes travelled before us. We will not lose the fundamental objective for which we came into being and continue to labour merely because problems arise on the way. One who forgets the separation of Dravida Nadu is no Dravidian.

I do not belong to the company of wealthy men. I am not elevated by money. I possess less wealth and less ability than you, and less courage by far than all of these. Yet I work by trusting the courage of the younger brothers around me. We shall certainly triumph in this journey toward our objective. That is certain.

At the next state conference, a delegation sent by North Indian imperialism to understand the question of Dravida Nadu will be seated here. Let us work in the coming years in a manner that brings this about. If only we acquire the courage to drive away the temptations that arise among us, we shall certainly win.

The Legislative Assembly is ornamental. The post of M.L.A. is profitable, and a ministerial post can intoxicate. But today they would cause us to lose our objective and destroy our integrity. We do not assemble in government halls; we gather beneath the banyan tree. Our branches exist in every direction across Dravidian land and beyond the sea, in Ceylon, Burma and Indochina. The comrades there support us.

Jeeva, who met me last week, said, "Whomever you support in this election, I shall strive for us to remain united afterwards." I say the same. If we speak in January, I will say, "Sign the agreement." We shall speak in March and work for the people with united minds.

So far more than 150 people have signed the D.M.K. agreement. There are capitalists among them and even an Iyer. When I see them, a little suspicion arises: is this a political Virata Parva? Are they coming in disguise? Do they intend to come and trouble us? Amid these doubts, each must protect the principles of his own party. That is why we shall weigh them, sift them, test them on the touchstone, examine them carefully, and sympathetically set aside whatever is not good.

We shall work to prevent Congress rule from returning. Kamaraj, the Rashtrapati of Tamil Nadu, repeatedly says, "Independence is a four-year-old child; people expect too much of it." He sings the refrain that whatever it does must be tolerated, provided one casts a vote for it. It was this four-year-old child that shot down innocent security prisoners in Salem prison, tormented the weavers, starved the people, beat scholar Appadurai in the streets of Madras, attacked even mothers with batons, imprisoned Periyar and me, and staged a ten-day political pageant. That four-year-old child committed still more unbearable cruelties. If it can do all this in four years, are we not afraid to imagine what will happen when its moustache sprouts and it comes carrying a staff?

In the Bharata story, Krishna stole butter as a child. Because Mother Yashoda did not beat and restrain him but kissed his crown and said, "My darling, if it is butter you want, why go outside? We have it at home," he began stealing women's garments when he grew older. In the same way, unable to tolerate the deeds of four years of Congress rule, the people now intend to strike it on the head and drive it out.

What has Congress achieved in four years? The vessel that held rice now holds millet; the vessel that held millet now holds tamarind seeds; the one that held tamarind seeds now holds cottonseed; then the cottonseed too is gone, leaving empty vessels, a collapsed roof, dying people, hunger and starvation. These alone can be shown.

I openly accuse the Nehru government of starving people to death. I say that Nehru's government runs behind the capitalists. Some ask, "You accuse Nehru? Where is Nehru and where are you?" I know Nehru is a very great man. I also know how greatly the newspapers of this country have made him great. Yes, I am an ordinary man. I also realize how thoroughly those newspapers have made me ordinary. Even so, I accuse him.

Nehru's government promised to nationalize industries, but it did not do so. It said it could not, revealing its lack of administrative ability. Why should this government not employ people such as Sir R. K. Shanmukham and G. D. Naidu as government servants? I mention these two rather than others because they are my friends and will not misunderstand what I say.

Invite Comrade G. D. Naidu to run motor transport in the Madras Presidency as its general manager. If it makes no profit, ask how it yielded profit while he operated it privately. If they behaved wrongly, summon them before the people's forum, judge them traitors and ask the people what should be done with them. Stories of the French Revolution roll through my mind. Workers scorched by capitalist cruelty stand ready to bring them in.

Nehru said he would put black marketeers on the impaling stake. He travelled throughout the country repeating it. Then he went to America. Before leaving, he pleaded with the black marketeers. He did not even call them that. He said: "O secret profiteers, do not show your accounts. Pay a portion of your profit as tax. Do not tell us how the profit was obtained or in what manner it reached you."

You heard younger brother Sampath speak about the rise and fall of great powers. Today the rise and fall of Nehru unfolds before our eyes. The Allahabad Pandit who studied socialism, spoke of the Russian plan at the Faizpur Congress, stood in the front rank of the freedom struggle, was hailed as the hero of liberty, the uncrowned king of India, the flawless jewel, the light of Asia and a scholar known throughout the world, has now lost the people's confidence.

Rather than leave rule in the hands of a Nehru who has lost confidence, it could be entrusted to Tata: even if there were greater villainy, administration would at least have ability. It could be given to Birla: even if there were greed, governance would at least not invite the mockery of foreign countries. Returning power to Nehru's hands is like entrusting a calf to a wolf. Therefore, in this election the D.M.K. will undertake every kind of action, pardon me, every lawful action, pardon me, every civilized action, to defeat Congress.

They went to prison and then entered the legislature. They said that once they came to power the C.I.D. would have no work. Yet now C.I.D. officers of every kind arrive at every hour, watching for where we err so that they may file a case and throw us in prison. Many of those C.I.D. officers are my friends. I do not name them because their jobs must remain secure. They may send the C.I.D. after me. I suspect Congress rule, so it may suspect me. But why send them after Kripalani, who only yesterday parted from them?

India is the size of an elephant and Ceylon only the size of a cat, yet the elephant begs from the cat. Ceylon sends rice to India. How is that possible? That is the disgrace. Black marketeers take rice grown in India through Vedaranyam and Point Calimere to the coast of Ceylon. That same rice then returns to India. That is why Senanayake is arrogant. What further evidence is needed that these Congress rulers do not know how to govern?

The ruling Congress travels today along a fascist path, unleashing every instrument of repression and watching the spectacle.

People speak of Nehru spending one month in England, staying at Mountbatten's mansion, and going to America the next month to be welcomed by Truman. History says that when Roman warriors lost influence at home, they travelled abroad, gained a military victory, returned in procession through Rome and restored their declining influence. Nehru follows the same method. Today he is like Julius Caesar.

Even if such a Nehru is renowned across the world, does he at least possess a strong foreign policy? No. They dance as America dances. Why should they take part in the Korean War? They say no troops were sent; why then send medical units? Nehru thundered that he would condemn any crossing of the 38th Parallel. But he himself merely kept crossing from Allahabad to Amritsar, Amritsar to Poona and Poona to Bombay; he issued no condemnation. The aged Iranian leader Mossadegh struggles over oil. Why should Nehru not speak a good word to foster goodwill? Why not speak about the Suez Canal? Has he said one good word on these matters? Will he at least do so now?

The late hero Liaquat Ali Khan said that a country standing abroad with a begging bowl cannot move with an independent foreign policy. How can India, begging wheat from America, hold its head high? The Prime Minister of Pakistan stated the truth. Because he had realized that truth, he could raise his fist even at the moment of death.

Therefore we say Dravidian land must not remain in the grip of these people who lack the ability to govern. They will oppose until Dravida Nadu separates. They will curse until the final moment when they sign the plan of partition. They called Pakistan "Paathakastan," a land of sin. They asked whether the child should be cut apart, whether cow-killing should be committed, and warned that woman-killing was evil. When Pakistan demanded partition, Achariyar opposed it with this talk of child-killing, woman-killing and cow-killing, for he came from a tradition practised in speaking of such things. Yet the same man later said that when a hole forms in an upper cloth, the wise course is to tear it into two: neither piece of cloth is ruined and two pieces result. Thus he offered a story in support of Pakistan's partition.

We therefore know that those who oppose today will support tomorrow. Even the Communists did not accept Pakistan until the end; after it arose, they said it had become proper on the basis of self-determination. Do not grow weary because they oppose us today. Work with confidence that tomorrow they will support us. Recognize that this is human nature and human disposition, do not abandon the effort, and work with still greater enthusiasm.

They said even Pakistan was a small country. Within it the Pathans demand a separate Pakhtunistan. They are people who do not sheathe their swords, and so *Kalki* prints pictures and lectures us that their demand is just. The Karens live in one corner of Burma and demand a separate country. There is agitation even in Scotland. Palestine is a desert, yet even there exists a sense of separate nationhood, and the international forum intervened and divided it. Does Albania not live separately? Spain has a small territory; between Germany and France there is no sea, only a boundary line, yet are they not two countries?

Norway and Sweden even share the name Scandinavia, yet remain separate. There are many such tiny countries. We speak of Swiss watches, Czech torches, French perfume and German medicine: each country excels in a particular field. Why does Nehru, when he travels there, not say, "Europe for the Europeans"? Why does he not say that European nationality will break if they remain divided? Which of these countries is larger than Dravida Nadu? Let them measure before they speak.

Look at North Indian history. We repeatedly read that someone came, but never that he went south and conquered. Rama came to the South, but with Sita. Pulakesi attempted conquest and failed. Neither Kanishka, Harsha, Akbar nor Aurangzeb conquered the South and established lasting rule. Even the ten incarnations taken by God occurred only beyond the Vindhyas. Is it not where sinners live that God must incarnate?

That is why we ask again for Dravidian land, which no one conquered by the sword but which fell through intrigue. We ask for Dravidian land, fortified by sea and mountain and by the strength of its people. We ask for the country our ancestors lost, the joyous Dravidian land that is the song of our objective. We ask for the country whose merchants travelled to Yavana lands and Kadaram to trade, and where great poets lived who turned love and valour into poetry and epic. Why offer so many arguments of right and justice? We ask for the country where we were born, the country that belongs to us. Yes, if we attain anything, it shall be Dravidian land; if we do not, we shall die in the very attempt to attain it. Possess such heroic feeling.

Across the path of our objective comes the election, that enchanting thief which bewilders the mind. At this moment we must guard our objective from theft. This is extremely important. By nature I am somewhat short, so I cannot easily be deceived. Whoever comes now and however they speak, whether their voice trembles with emotion, whether they cast loving words, kiss my crown or kiss me, I shall say: sign the agreement.

By justice and by right, it is we who ought to contest the election and enter the Legislative Assembly and Parliament. Yet we did not go, in the manner that strategy indicated. How can those we send to the places we should occupy refuse even to listen to what we say? How could we send such people?

More than 150 people have signed our agreement. Even if they do not accept the Dravida Nadu question in ordinary times, the list shows how many support it in a moment of crisis. Some ask what we will do if they sign now and later change. What would we do if they signed and then died? We regard a change in the same way. Will they leave us after winning? Will we simply let them go? Are we helpless? Once I needed a recommendation to become a member of the Justice Party executive committee. But now we have grown enough to make those wealthy men tremble. How was that possible? Having achieved such a feat, can we not make these people remain on our side?

Where no one signs the agreement, the organization will give no one its official support. There the organization's comrades will act only as voters. Among those opposing Congress, they will give their votes to a progressive, honourable and honest candidate, even if that candidate is a Communist.

At this moment I cannot avoid expressing my regret that the Communist comrades were unable to sign the agreement. I firmly believed that they would sign and was ready to cooperate wholeheartedly. But like us, they are a separate party and may have difficulties; hence they could not sign. In the same way, our policy restrains my hand. Communist comrades are worthy of our respect and affection; they are good people. But just as difficulties arising from their being a separate party prevented them from signing, we too were compelled to demand an agreement. Just as they have the right to decide not to sign, we have the right to decide to support only those who signed.

I say that in Dravidian land the D.M.K. is the proper and genuine Communist Party. If a foreign committee came to investigate, I would prove it with thousands of pieces of evidence. Here are countless dry heads, stomachs clinging to the spine and sunken eyes. Many comrades worn down by labour work among us, and many labourers are members.

The socialist programme others speak of is like a gramophone record. How can the record sing before the gramophone is ready? We are preparing the social gramophone itself. We possess the socialist record, the social gramophone and the political needle.

I know and recognize that Comrade Jeeva is a child born of the Self-Respect Movement. He belongs here. But if he comes at election time, I shall say, "Sign the agreement." He may come here and change anything contrary to socialist principles; he has the right to rebuke me. Yes, I am confident that within another five years a genuine united front will certainly arise, and I submit that confidence now.

Dravida Nadu possesses the boundaries a country requires. Pakistan did not even possess contiguous boundaries. If that Pakistan could be obtained, why can we not obtain ours? People ask whether, after attaining Dravida Nadu, we will drive out the Marwaris from the North. We will not. They must obtain government permission, and only then may they remain here. They may live here as foreign residents with government permission, like the French and the Dutch.

In 1954 we shall place the separation of Dravida Nadu before the people and conduct a referendum. We shall then show the people's verdict to the central government and make our demand. If it remains obstinate, we shall go to the United Nations. The central government will refuse, and may imprison us if we resist. We shall then contest the election, turn the same demand into a resolution in the legislature and fight in the legislatures. The North Indian government will dismiss our ministry. We shall contest again on the same question and win again and again. The struggle will proceed in this manner.

Do not wonder what the people will do without a ministry. The administrative machinery established by the British will function even without ministers. In 1960 there will certainly be a political upheaval, and then we may have to leap into a struggle. Its plans may include refusing taxes to the North Indian government and boarding trains run by it without tickets. Comrades, work now in preparation for that.

Comrades, at least two lakh members must join this year. The garlands you placed on me are not important, nor is the great pavilion you have erected. Membership must increase. That number will raise me, raise your General Secretary and raise you yourselves.

Comrades, I look upon you with enthusiasm, but at the same time with fear. The enthusiasm that has grown must be channelled; otherwise it will kill us. We have developed enthusiasm without limit and must create ways of work suited to it. Regard government prohibitory orders as the roof tiles of your own homes. Let every repressive measure be a plaything. You must cease to know any difference between home and prison. Yes, only then can we regain the Dravidian land we lost. Prepare to make such heroic sacrifices. O drops of tears, jewels of the nation's eyes, give me that offering. Greetings.

(*Dravida Nadu*, 23 December 1951)

## Translator's Notes

This is a political conference address from December 1951. Its claims, forecasts and rhetoric are translated as historical source material without silently updating them.

"Virata Parva" refers to the *Mahabharata* episode in which the Pandavas live in disguise. "Paathakastan" is a polemical pun on Pakistan using a Sanskrit-derived word for sin. The linked expressions about cow-, woman- and child-killing reproduce the speech's account of contemporary partition rhetoric.

"S.M. Movement" in the source is expanded as the Self-Respect Movement. "Faizpur Congress," "Pakhtunistan," and the printed English slogan "A Home - Land For Our Nation" follow the readings visible in the scans.

The scan clearly gives 150 agreement signatories on both relevant pages and dates publication to 23 December 1951; both readings replace corrupted OCR.

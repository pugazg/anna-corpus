## English Translation

“You speak in condemnation of the *Ramayana*. You say Rama is not a god. Why, then, have you kept the name Ramasamy?”

This is a question familiar to us all. Friends know that Aryans and their followers release it when they reel under the arrows of the father of rationalism, Periyar Ramasamy. Innocent people imagine it overflows with brilliance and humour. Those unable to answer his arguments think this utterance will rescue them. Questions of this kind have arisen at many meetings.

But the question I cite was not one somebody asked Periyar Ramasamy.

You may ask at whom this arrow was aimed. Before asking me, reflect for a moment. You will realise that the person questioned was also named Ramasamy and, like our Periyar, possessed the quality of denouncing antiquity.

Do you know his answer?

“It is true that I condemn the *Ramayana*. It is also true that I describe Rama as an Aryan. My name too is Ramasamy. Where is the error?” Ramasamy asked.

“Can one who condemns Rama bear Rama’s own name?” the questioner pressed.

Ramasamy replied at once: “Sir, does not *samy* also mean master? My name is Ramasamy: I am Rama’s master!”

Angered by the answer, the questioner cursed Ramasamy further, within his own mind.

Though orthodox believers, lovers of antiquity, Brahmins, and religious fanatics condemned him endlessly, Ramasamy remained wholly unshaken. Until the end he lived as a heroic and vigorous Dravidian. This commander of our movement died a year ago. A memorial association was established for him, and Periyar Ramasamy participated in its opening ceremony and spoke on the fourth and fifth at Nagatla, a village in Andhra.

Tamil Nadu has not yet known the fame and effectiveness of Barrister Ramasamy Chowdary, who fearlessly performed the work of self-respect in Andhra. That his force and Periyar’s could not stand together as a united army in the field was a great loss to the Dravidian cause. Madras lies less than three hundred miles from Tenali, where Barrister Ramasamy was born, raised, and performed many services. Yet Aryan newspapers kept the two forces divided and prevented their union. Dravidian society therefore could not receive the full benefit of his work.

This is the Aryan method of war: skilfully create divisions in the Dravidian ranks and then ensure that the divided forces never reunite. The countless divisions in Dravidian society are a result of that method, while the Aryan force remains linked like a chain. As Periyar humorously says, if a Brahmin at Kanyakumari is stung by a scorpion, a Brahmin in the Himalayas develops a swelling. Yet we could not obtain the cooperation of Ramasamy Chowdary of nearby Tenali, who laboured painfully for the same principles as we did.

Born into an influential family, he went abroad, qualified as a barrister, returned home, and lived without applying for employment. He fought a rare battle to make the fallen Dravidian rise in vigour and to destroy Aryan domination. Antiquity must disappear. Brahminism must be pulverised. The cruelty by which an immigrant group seizes and torments the indigenous people must fall. The habit of accepting afflictions and suffering beneath them must end. Modernity must blossom. The arrogance of the priestly group must be restrained. The ignorance that treats as religion a cowardice capable of depriving one of wife and children must die. Divisions must be removed. Rituals resembling the acts of a madman must be abolished. The mentality of kneeling before an adversary who knows nothing but wrongdoing must change. An awakening must arise that rights must be won even through struggle. There must be courage.

These are the thoughts we hold for the resurgence of the Dravidian people. Barrister Ramasamy thought likewise, published those thoughts in sweet and simple Telugu verse, and was affectionately called *Kaviraj* by the people. He wrote books exposing the frauds of Aryan puranas and epics that enchant the popular mind. He declared that neither Aryan priests nor the Aryan language should enter marriage ceremonies. He lived fearlessly resisting Aryan domination.

He was not like the Salem barrister named Ramasamy who issued a declaration that “the Brahmin plague is spreading” and then submerged himself in the Aryan pool some years later. This barrister remained a Dravidian until the end. “Whatever hardship comes, I will not bow to the Vedic horde,” he proclaimed. Its echo was heard that day at Nagatla.

The journey felt like being roasted in a pan of fire. Crossing mile after mile of open land swept by dusty winds, the train carried us to Chirala through blasts of scorching air, intent only on performing its duty. The name Chirala must awaken in you the same thought it awakened in me. During the hunt for votes, Achariyar promised to labour for workers’ rule, overthrow repressive laws, and abolish repression itself. Once in power, it was at this very Chirala that he fired bullets upon fellow workers and conducted a government that taught the people the meaning of Congress honour.

Seeing Chirala, I involuntarily sighed. I sighed at the thought that the country still trusts a group trained to utter lies knowingly and deceive people ignorant of the facts.

Nagatla lies twenty miles beyond Chirala. There, under the name Kaviraj Ashram, stands an association commemorating the late Barrister Ramasamy. Seetharama Ammal donated a building worth five thousand rupees and a reading room. On the morning of the fourth, Kuppusami Chowdary of Karachedu opened the building and explained:

“Until now people regarded as charity the construction of temples and tanks that enabled one caste to live. That old custom has died. Donations are now increasingly given to associations that impart sound knowledge to the people and our society. This is a sign of wisdom.”

That evening villagers gathered in great numbers. Women attended in numbers many times greater than we see in our part of Tamil Nadu. After Venkatachari welcomed those present and described Barrister Chowdary’s rare ability, Periyar Ramasamy explained the principles of the Self-Respect Movement in Telugu for about an hour. That night he discussed the question of Dravida Nadu with comrades from many towns.

On the morning of the fifth, Pandit Venkata Ratnam and Narasimhiah spoke about the barrister’s cast of mind and the obscenity of the puranas. I spoke in English that evening, and Janardhanam spoke in Telugu. After Sathyanarayana, Telugu teacher at Chirala High School, spoke, Periyar delivered a rare two-hour concluding address in Telugu. He explained the necessity of consigning the *Ramayana*, *Periya Puranam*, *Manusmriti*, and similar works to the fire, and spoke with a flood of emotion that impassioned young people with a sense of racial resurgence must come forward to conduct a social revolution.

A young man named Gopinath rose to offer thanks but instead attacked the *Ramayana* canto by canto. He showed the cruelty of Sambuka’s killing and thunderously exposed the nature of Rama Rajya. Demanding that the *Ramayana* be burned, he declared with sparks flying from his words, “Even fire will be polluted when this book of evil ideas is placed in it.” The ceremony concluded pleasantly with cries of “Down with Brahminism! Long live non-Brahmin society!”

Some say that a Dravidian consciousness cannot take root in Andhra. They say so only because we have had no means of recognising the Dravidian consciousness already present there. We possess no English journal through which to understand feeling in Andhra and communicate ours to them. The existing *Sunday Observer* and *Liberator* have other work. This is the reason the Self-Respect Movement has encountered an obstacle.

These ideas have indeed spread through Telugu country, but lie scattered here and there. Barrister Ramasamy Chowdary died before he could gather and shape them. Yet one cannot say that the young people who follow his principles will remain silent.

Our comrades know how the revolutionary poet Kanakasubburathinam, in his drama *Hiranyan, or the Peerless Hero*, explained the wonder of Narayana emerging from a pillar. An Aryan wearing a lion mask hides behind the pillar and kills Hiranyan, the peerless hero. How remarkable that Barrister Ramasamy spoke and wrote the same idea there as our unique poet of self-respect did here. An Aryan, assisting Prahlada who betrayed his own father, hid behind the pillar and killed Hiranyan; our people have been made to believe this was the Narasimha incarnation, Chowdary wrote.

The puranic storyteller boasts that Bhagiratha brought the Ganges from the sky. Do not believe it, the barrister said. Bhagiratha may have created a channel for the river from the Himalayan slopes; the puranic author turned this into a fraud. Believing it enables the Brahmin to live while our people lose their wealth.

He expressed another still more important thought, but died before it could take root in that land.

For a long time, Tamils alone repeatedly opposed Aryan domination and fought without submitting. Storms of revolution have blown again and again. Though kneeling kings, witless ministers, thoughtless people, and some poets without self-respect became Aryan followers, many repeatedly rebelled. That revolution continues to this day. Tamil alone has periodically given the people the feeling needed for it.

Even after receiving an Aryan embrace, Tamil has remained a spring of self-respect without surrendering its ability to live independently. Sanskrit is mixed into Tamil today, but not to the degree found in Telugu and Malayalam. Even now Tamil can remove the Sanskrit within it, raise its head, and function independently without losing a trace of richness or beauty, indeed with greater splendour. Unlike the Kerala maiden who wilts at a touch or the Andhra woman destroyed by an embrace, Mother Tamil merely allowed Sanskrit and other languages to remain beside her, like a mother drawing children playing in the courtyard close, giving them a treat, and sending them away. She did not become their slave.

One may call this independent Tamil a bulwark of self-respect even today. It is doubtful whether Telugu or Malayalam could function independently if their present Sanskrit vocabulary were removed. It is no exaggeration to say that people who lose their language lose their path and sight.

Recognising this defect, Barrister Ramasamy launched an agitation to remove Sanskrit words from Telugu and write independent Telugu. He said the very name *Andhra* signified slavery to Aryanism, while calling oneself *Telugu* awakened a language-based consciousness. When Andhra University was established, he served in its Senate and argued that its name should be changed to “Telugu University.” Because believers in Aryanism formed the majority, his effort failed. He expressed his protest by saying he could not participate in the proceedings and walked out.

Today many young people believe Telugu must function independently and can do so. They wish to gather pure Telugu words from village speech and song, from a mother’s cradle-song endearments and a ploughman’s *elelo*, and enable Telugu to live on its own. That enthusiasm cannot fail eventually to open the way to communal resurgence.

Barrister Ramasamy condemned the ruin caused by dividing Telugu people into Kamma, Kapu, Velama, Reddy, and many other groups. He said these were not caste divisions. Different kinds of land produce corresponding occupations and ways of life; similarly, names arose among people in Andhra’s different regions according to the character of the land and occupation. They did not indicate caste. Caste distinction belonged to Aryans, not to us. His explanation resembled the ancient Tamil division of land into kurinji, mullai, marutham, paalai, and neithal.

Like our movement, the barrister drove Aryanism from marriages. He conducted many weddings without Sanskrit and without Aryans. He also laboured for Dravidian resurgence.

At a Justice conference held in Nellore ten years earlier, Barrister Ramasamy reportedly met Periyar Ramasamy and read to him from his work *Sambuka Vadham*, which exposed the disorder of Rama Rajya. Besides tirelessly serving the Self-Respect Movement, he wrote many books explaining the fraud of Aryan mythology. In *Suta Puranam* he subjected those puranas to searching examination.

Many there now wish to carry forward rapidly and vigorously the movement he began. Telugu books have appeared with titles such as *The Brahmin Who Commits Eighteen Sins*, *Clan Custom Is an Invention*, *Slave of the Shastras*, and *Social Revolution*. An association called Suta Ashram exists at Tenali.

One story in the Telugu book *Slave of the Shastras* runs as follows.

“Very well! From now on I shall live strictly according to the shastras. Can the ancient greatness of Bharata Varsha be allowed to suffer? Hereafter I shall follow the Agamas and the customs of primordial times,” resolved a Brahmin schoolteacher.

While the Ganges still slept, before the crow called and when sunlight appeared only as a speck, he went according to scripture to a well, completed his morning duties, and bathed. As he poured the cold water over himself, his delight in scriptural conduct seemed to seize and shake his body, and he trembled. Smiling, he chanted “Sambho! Mahadeva!” while pouring pot after pot upon his head.

The man who regularly cleaned the backyard arrived and stood there, unaware of the teacher’s resolve. The Aryan saw him. “Fate! Ruin!” he cried, striking his head. “Great sinner, where have you come from? Seeing a chandala immediately after bathing is a terrible sin. Go away!” he cursed.

After the man left, he began his ritual bath again. The washerman happened to be in a hurry and arrived just as the Iyer completed his second bath. That too violated scripture, compelling another bath. After bathing three or four times, he finished worship and came to sit. His wife had placed the usual wooden plank. A scriptural injunction entered his mind: Manu, Parasara, or one of their descendants had said sitting upon a wooden seat violated observance.

“Remove the seat! Remove the leaf too! Sweep, plaster, and purify the place!” he told his lawful wife. As school time approached, he became frightened and agitated and sat before the newly plastered floor had dried.

As he prepared to eat, he heard someone wailing loudly. They did not know the Iyer intended to live by scripture. But he recalled having read some command that hearing lamentation at mealtime led to an evil fate. He did not eat and went straight to school.

It was grammar class. Teaching Vedic grammar to anyone other than a Vedic Brahmin violated scripture. The school contained non-Brahmin children. Teaching is the ornament of a teacher, but following the Agamas is the ornament of an Aryan. He therefore refused that very day to teach the Sudra children and immediately lost his job.

The next day was a death-anniversary rite. Thinking it most scriptural to perform it on the bank of the Ganges, he went to Kashi. There he was distressed to see people of many castes bathing. Performing the rite where low people bathed would violate observance. He therefore went to Karachi, intending to reach the sea. There he saw not only many castes but white people near the shore and grieved.

An idea arose. Water and land had both become impure, so performing the rite in either place violated scripture. The pure place was the sky. Deciding that the sky must therefore be scripturally proper, he went to an aerodrome and hired a plane. Accompanied by a priest, he circled in the air, intending to complete the rite while the plane remained aloft. The ceremony began. At that very moment the priest became dizzy and nauseated, vomited, and polluted the place. Thus even an aeroplane was unfit for the rite.

What was the Iyer to do? Should he follow scripture and leap into the Arabian Sea, sacrificing his life? He reflected, and reason dawned upon him.

“Fool! How greatly time has changed and the world changed. In a universe that has changed, continues changing, and possesses change as its very nature, you are one tiny creature. Can you stop this transformation? Can you swim against the flood called change? The Agamas and shastras were arrangements made by people of that age according to their knowledge and for the convenience of their world. Do they suit present knowledge? Does today’s world need them? Can they assist present convenience? Do not cling like a monkey.”

The Brahmin who had resolved to conduct life by scripture acquired wisdom. Realising that scriptural conduct was impossible for anyone, he ordered the plane to descend and returned home. The slave of the shastras became a man of intelligence.

This story appears in the Telugu book *Slave of the Shastras*. It contains not only the story but forceful criticism of many puranas. The seed of the Self-Respect Movement has therefore been sown there. If watered with labour, it will sprout and flourish. But is knowing the method of cultivation enough? Workers are needed.

That is the truth I saw by going to Nagatla. Effort brings victory. When I think that effort brings victory, a smile appears. When I think that we remain unable to make that effort, a sigh follows. The two come alternately. What can be done?

11 June 1944

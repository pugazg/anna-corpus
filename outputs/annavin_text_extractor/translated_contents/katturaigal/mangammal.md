## English Translation

One may be tempted to ask: is Mangammal becoming Thangammal merely a story about a changed name? It is not only the name that has changed; her status has changed with it. Therein lie both the subtlety and the flavour.

Mangammal, who had been reviled and denounced as a great sinner, a destroyer of her household, a traitor to her community and a woman who brought disgrace, has now been made Thangammal. Is that an ordinary matter? Mangammal hurt her mother's heart, committed a great sin and left her home.

Every effort to find her proved futile.

Wherever they searched, the rogue could not be caught. Just when they thought that, once caught, she should be dragged back by the hair, placed in the public rest house, tried for her offence and severely punished, the audacious woman would slip away and disappear. Warriors of the “one stroke, two pieces” variety were sent in all eight directions to find and drag her back. Detectives went to work. “Find the traitor and bring her to me; I shall hang her from the gallows,” thundered the guardian of the town.

They searched every nook and corner for Mangammal, but she remained in hiding. “Can you see someone moving in that grove? Could it be Mangammal? It must be! Run, run—do not let her go, catch her!” some would cry. Others would run to seize her, but the graceful woman would escape.

“Do not make a sound! Do you hear the bangles? Those are Mangammal's bangles. Come quietly; let us look in the flower garden. She will surely be caught.” They would set out, but the sound of bangles would cease and the elusive woman would not be found.

There was nowhere they did not seek her; they suffered endlessly trying to catch her. Reports would frequently arrive that she had roamed there, spoken here, sung in one place and danced in another, but she herself would not be caught.

The guardian's anger grew, and so did the townspeople's. Mangammal's offence was capable of bringing disgrace upon the entire town.

The people's hearts boiled. Mangammal's conduct was strongly condemned in public assemblies. Many grieved that this great sinner was bringing ruin upon the whole region. She nevertheless remained hidden.

One year passed, then two. Mangammal was not caught.

Only afterward did a strange but painful event occur.

The very guardian who had condemned Mangammal as a destroyer of her household, a traitor and a great sinner, and who had roared, “Drag her here and I shall hang her,” began calling that same Mangammal “Thangammal”—the precious woman.

“Even if you did wrong, dear Thangamma, never mind. You have remained hidden long enough. Do not make us suffer any further. Return to town. Our hearts cannot bear being parted from you.” He had tender petitions prepared in this fashion and ordered them hung at monasteries, rest houses and charitable inns. The people were bewildered.

“It is enough if Thangammal returns to her town. I shall not even inquire where she was hiding or what she was doing. It is enough if she returns home. She need not fear that anyone will condemn or ridicule her. Mangammal is the old name; I shall forget it. I shall also forgive her former conduct. Mangammal is now Thangammal! Please return to town.”

The guardian composed this moving appeal and sent his servants throughout the town to sing it.

The people were stunned by this new approach. Why was the town guardian now tenderly addressing the great sinner Mangammal as Thangammal?

Was she not a murderous woman whose conduct had made the people's hearts tremble? Why did he now call her “gold” in a quivering voice? The people thought and thought, unable to understand.

Had evidence been found that she was innocent? No. She remained guilty. Yet the man who had crowed that he would throw her in a cage and impale her suddenly called, “My precious one, come!”

She had deceived the town and gone into hiding.

Everyone had hated and condemned even the name Mangammal. The leader of the town had now transformed her into Thangammal. Great pain filled the townspeople's hearts.

From somewhere she speaks exultantly:

“You ignorant people! How you crowed before! How you shouted, ‘Bring us that destroyer of the household; we shall tear out her entrails!’ You called me ruinous and deceitful. You abused me as a dog and a demon. You sent fierce heroes to catch me and prepared a gallows upon which to hang me. What happened in the end? Who won? You had no ability to find me. What could your kind do against my daring? You thrashed and trembled. What will you do now?

“How fiercely you shouted, ‘Mangammal, the great sinner!’ But I am no longer Mangammal, my lords. Your leader calls me Thangammal! Shame and pain are yours; victory and joy are mine! I have become Thangammal. The town leader calls to me, ‘Please come, dear mother, my precious one!’ If I return, there will be no inquiry. No one may twist their tongue against me. He says merely returning is enough. What do you say now? How loudly you beat your mouths before! Who has won? Mangammal has become Thangammal!”

The townspeople hang their heads in shame. The woman who ruined the household has risen in status. Even now she says, “Whether I return is my choice.”

The town guardian formerly cried, “Catch the rogue and drag her here!”

Now he begs, “You have made us suffer enough, Thangamma. Please come home!”

She is the one who ruined the town.

Yet her status and name have changed.

Mangammal has become Thangammal!

Where did this happen? Could such a transformation occur? How did the people tolerate it? Questions come one after another. Do not be alarmed, friends. I have been speaking about our own town. Are you angry? Very well—hear the rest of the news.

You know what has been happening in the country for the past four or five years: prices rising like poison; people suffering because goods cannot be found; the government intervening to fix prices, yet goods remaining unavailable at those prices; goods becoming available on the “black market” at higher prices; people paying those prices because they have no choice; some thereby reaping plunderous profits through the black market while the public suffers; and enormous amounts of unaccounted money accumulating in a few hands.

Earlier, several hundred thousand people died in Bengal after falling victim to the cruelty of black-marketeers. Now tens of millions are wasting away, lamenting and bewildered because of them. The black-marketeers' plunder is said to amount to many hundreds of crores of rupees. As the poor person's stomach burns and life is ruined, these people gather sacks of money.

Their cunning has plundered the lives of an unsuspecting public. People's misery increased. The name people gave to black marketing, illicit trade and plunderous profit was “Mangammal.” They would say: “What is the price of yarn in the shop? What does it cost through Mangammal?” “We need four sacks of rice; see whether Mangammal can provide them.” “Somasundaram is building a new two-storey house—Mangamma's grace!”

Thus “Mangammal” meant black-market goods and the plunderous profit obtained from the black market.

This “Mangammal”—black-market money—ruined the town, tormented the poor and amassed plunder. Tens of millions suffered because of it.

The people denounced “Mangammal” as a great sinner and destroyer of households. Leaders such as Nehru and Patel condemned it strongly and thundered that they would abolish the black-market system. They vowed to find Mangammal and punish her severely. Nehru said those who deceived the poor and amassed plunderous black-market profits were traitors to society and sinful souls who ought to be hanged.

The government made efforts to find Mangammal and established a special department. Mangammal, however, became an illusion; neither black marketing nor plunderous profit disappeared. These black-marketeers brought disgrace even upon India's name.

Leaders declared that their property should be confiscated and that allowing them to roam free was a disgrace to the town.

Mangammal had an evil reputation. People spat at the very mention of the black market; such was their hatred.

Nehru and Patel had promised to trace and confiscate money plundered through this hated black market and punish the great sinners who had betrayed the people. For some days now, however, they have been pleading: no accounts need be shown for that plundered black-market money; it is enough to pay the government at least some tax upon it and invest it in new industry. They openly say and issue statements promising, “We shall not even ask how the money was earned or why you concealed it for so long. It is enough to bring the money into circulation.” What a shameful and painful event! Mangammal has been made Thangammal!

The leaders once condemned these very black-marketeers as vultures tearing at the lives of the poor and poisonous germs corroding society. Now they coax them and plead, “There will be no inquiry; we shall not regard you with contempt; we shall not ask how you obtained this plunderous profit; paying a little tax is enough.” How greatly Mangammal's arrogance will swell upon hearing these endearments!

Hearing from the mouth of Pandit Nehru, who had thundered “I shall hang you,” that no accounts need be shown, there will be no condemnation and paying tax is enough, will Mangammal not regard the people as mere dirt?

Those who said black-market money was filled with sin—the blood of the poor and the sweat of the gullible—and would destroy the country now say, “However the money was obtained, even if it is black-market money, we shall not accuse you; give a little of it to the government as tax.” Must the people tolerate political morality sinking to this level?

Would one establish a new police station at a festival to catch thieves, fail to catch any of them, and after the festival paste up a notice saying, “We request everyone to place in this collection box, according to their means, at least enough from the stolen goods to meet police expenses. Though they were thieves at the festival, a thousand million salutations to them if they perform at least this charitable act”?

Should Pandit Nehru and Sardar Patel surrender to black-marketeers in this fashion? Should they turn Mangammal into Thangammal? Patel apparently regards the tens of millions who praise him as “Sardar” as mere simpletons. Last week he pleaded: “New factories require money and capital. Capitalists, invest your capital! Do not hesitate. When you invest, the government will not pounce upon you and threaten, ‘Where did you get this enormous sum, and how did you hide it without accounting for it?’ If you fear questions will arise when investing in your own name, invest under another name. There will be no condemnation and no punishment.”

The Sardar, celebrated as the hero who subdued kings and emperors, turns Mangammal into Thangammal and begs, “Please show us kindness, dear woman.”

These leaders now avoid even the name “black-market money,” calling it “hidden money” or “secret earnings.” Perhaps police stations will soon call thieves “midnight workers”! Mangammal is being made Thangammal. Great leaders kneel before a great sinner. They treat the people's feelings and honour as worthless. This is our kingdom!

Mangammal continued her sinful acts even while Pandit Nehru thundered that he would hang her. The heart trembles to consider how boldly she will continue her evil work now that she has been made Thangammal.

Why this new approach? Why submit to traitors to society? Is it impossible to find and restrain them and end their atrocities? Are these leaders powerless? Have those who fought the rule of armed British masters lost their strength? What policy do the national leaders—who spoke honeyed words about assuming office to give the poor a life—follow today? Why do they show tenderness to those who amassed plunderous black-market profits? How will the hearts of ordinary people, who expected these leaders to find and severely punish social traitors, ache upon seeing this new course?

Believing their leaders powerful enough to destroy these devourers of people, the public boldly heaped abuse upon black-marketeers. Today those black-marketeers strike back at the public with ridicule.

Some argue that production must increase, that capitalists and industrialists must come forward to make it happen, that they presently lack interest in industry, and that leaders are therefore trying to encourage them. Production must indeed grow—but by what method? By making black-marketeers fat? Is not the purpose of increased production to enable the community to thrive? If that purpose is to be achieved, should not the black-market system that destroys the community be abolished?

If the leaders governing the country stand heralding scoundrels who amass black-market profits, what will be produced? Goods? No, no! How can public welfare grow? Corruption will be produced. Famine will be produced. Evil will flourish. They have come forward to proclaim honours for the sinner! Will he fear or feel shame any longer?

Sardar Patel is praised as the man who intimidated the Nizam of Hyderabad, the Nawab of Junagadh, the Maharaja of Kolhapur and the ruler of Rampur with a fierce glare. Yet he assembles industrialists and sings their praises. He asks them to show kindness, assuring them, “I shall see that no difficulty arises in your path. I shall reduce your tax burden.” He does not merely speak coaxingly to those capitalists; he speaks almost fearfully, saying, “I humbly submit to you.” Why? Is it concern, affection or fear? Can this be so?

When rebuking workers, this Sardar becomes an explosive bomb. When condemning Communists, he becomes an atom bomb. But when he meets capitalists, he plays the flute. Why this humility?

Nor is Patel alone in this submission. Pandit Nehru condemns all “isms” wholesale and mocks socialism and communism. What arrogance his conduct will produce in the minds of capitalists swollen with pride!

27 November 1949

கருதப்படுகிறது, இப்போது! ஆரம்பத்திலே அதிர்ச்சி - பின்னர் அதிசயம்.

கவிதைகள் தீட்டினான் முதலில் ஷேக்ஸ்பியர் நூல்களை மொழி
பெயர்த்தான், பின்னர் - படைத்தலைவனாக ஆனான், அதற்குப் பிறகு!

ஆட்சியை எதிர்த்தான் என்பதற்காகச் சிறையிலடப்பட்டான் 1958ல்
- ஆயினும் எப்படியோ தப்பினான், சிற்சில மாதங்களுக்குள்ளாகவே!

தப்பியவன், வெனிசூலா நாட்டில், லா கைரா துறைமுகத்தருகில்
வாழ்க்கை நடத்தி வந்தான்.

அந்தத் துறைமுகத்துக்கு சான்டா மேரியா அடிக்கடி வருமாம்!
அப்போது அவனுடைய உள்ளத்திலே முளைவிட்டது, இத்தகைய திட்டம்!

இந்தத் திட்டத்தோடு, கப்பல் அதிகாரிகளின் அனுமதி பெற்றுப் பல
தடவை கப்பலுக்குள் சென்று பார்த்திருக்கின்றான் அவன்!

அப்போது அவனுக்குப் புரிந்தது, அந்தக் கப்பல் பிரயாணிகளை
மட்டுமே ஏற்றிச் செல்லுகிற கப்பலென்றும், அதில் போர்க்கருவிகள்

## English Translation

For three days there was a single commotion throughout the world: a storm of questions about what might have happened to that ship, many different conjectures, suspicion that piracy might be taking place, and fear that it could have sunk somewhere at sea.

The newspapers continuously published descriptions of the ship.

Britain and America threw their respective navies and aircraft energetically into the work of searching for the missing vessel.

The Portuguese Navy prepared itself.

Appeals went out to every ship intending to travel through the South Atlantic: if the missing vessel was sighted anywhere, the news must be reported immediately.

At last, on the third day after the ship disappeared, a message came from a Danish vessel. The missing ship was sailing south-east through the Atlantic Ocean!

Within the next few hours, an American military aircraft flew above it.

On seeing the aircraft, the passengers jumped and shouted with boundless joy.

The event is now regarded as one of the wonders of this century: shock at first, followed by astonishment.

It is said that Columbus, who discovered America, named the ship in which he travelled *Santa Maria*. The Portuguese had given the missing ship that same name, with immense joy and pride.

The vessel, weighing approximately 22,000 tons, left the Portuguese port of Lisbon on 9 January 1961.

For the next thirteen days, until 22 January, the journey proceeded peacefully and without any disturbance.

There were approximately 600 passengers aboard.

On January 21, the ship reached La Guaira, a port in Venezuela, the northernmost country of South America. Twenty-three passengers boarded there.

They were supporters of General Humberto Delgado, who had rebelled against Portuguese ruler Salazar and been exiled.

Their plan was first to seize the ship, disembark its passengers at some port, and then use the vessel as a base for a revolt against the ruler of Portugal.

Their revolutionary leader, Henrique Galvão, was over sixty-five years old. He had a thin frame but a resolute heart. Without that, could he have entered such a terrifying and hair-raising undertaking?

He first wrote poetry, later translated Shakespeare's works, and after that became a military officer.

He was imprisoned in 1958 for opposing the government, but somehow escaped within a few months.

After escaping, he lived near the port of La Guaira in Venezuela.

The *Santa Maria* called frequently at that port. It was then that such a plan sprouted in his mind.

With this plan, he obtained the ship officers' permission and went aboard several times to inspect it.

He learned that it was solely a passenger ship and carried no weapons of war.

He therefore turned the circumstances to his advantage. All his associates boarded at La Guaira on January 21.

He alone had one doubt: what if the plan were exposed?

He therefore did not board at that port.

He knew that the ship would reach Curaçao the following day. He flew there by aircraft and bought his ticket there.

Thus the revolutionary leader too boarded on January 22. All that remained was to execute the plan successfully.

At 11:55 that night, two shots rang out aboard the ship. Nascimento Costa, who served as an assistant to the ship's captain, was struck and lay gravely wounded, struggling for life.

On hearing the shots, de Sousa, who was undergoing navigation training, left his cabin.

There was a second of bewilderment. “Go to the captain!” Costa said even as his life hung in the balance.

Thinking that someone must first be arranged to care for Costa, de Sousa ran to the radio operator's room.

When he opened the door, however, astonishment and shock awaited him. Where the radio operator should have been, a man stood holding a gun.

His bullets struck de Sousa, who staggered and fell. Yet with blood pouring from him, he dragged himself forward upon his chest towards the captain's room to report what had happened and explain the situation.

He reached the captain's room, but had no strength to cry out or knock upon the door. He lost consciousness.

Meanwhile, a passenger ran to the captain and tremblingly described the terror that had occurred.

The ship's captain hurriedly telephoned the radio operator's room.

From there he heard a voice he had never heard before:

“This is Captain Galvão speaking. In the name of General Delgado, I have seized this ship by force. You must not obstruct my action in any manner. If you do, you will have to face terrible consequences. Surrender; it will be to your advantage.”

What could the ship's captain do? He let events take their course.

Within approximately half an hour, everything was complete. The ship had changed hands.

The passengers could not believe what had happened. Many kinds of thoughts and confusion arose among them, along with rumours about what the outcome might be.

Costa and de Sousa were sent to the ship's hospital. Despite extensive treatment, Costa embraced death there. Only four days earlier he had received a telegram from his wife saying that a daughter had been born to him.

De Sousa survived and was put ashore near the port of Saint Lucia the following morning.

Then, at about nine in the morning on January 23, all first- and second-class passengers were summoned by the ship's captain.

Captain Mário Simões Maia addressed them in Portuguese.

“The ship has been seized by force. Those who seized it are directing it at gunpoint,” he said.

Galvão spoke next, explained his objective, and assured the passengers that no harm would be done to them.

Even so, indescribable terror prevailed among them: uncertainty and fear about what might happen next, an atmosphere like the silence of a cremation ground.

That condition lasted only a few hours. Afterwards, everything proceeded as usual.

People swam in the pool, played ball, danced and took part in entertainments.

Even a birth took place aboard during this difficult time.

A Dutch man and a Spanish woman met, became attached to one another, and exchanged promises of marriage.

Three days passed. The ship continued south-east, five hundred miles east of Trinidad.

It was then that an American aircraft spotted it.

The pilot and Galvão held discussions over wireless radio.

Galvão said categorically: “My aim is not piracy. I did this to liberate Portugal from Salazar's grip.”

The news flew throughout the world.

The ship continued peacefully.

Galvão nevertheless faced many difficulties. The food aboard was not sufficient for many days. Third-class passengers suffered severely after the air-conditioning machinery broke down.

The American naval forces repeatedly appealed: “Somehow disembark the passengers safely. We can discuss everything else afterwards.”

On January 31, while the ship was heading towards the Venezuelan port printed in the source as “Risaip,” Rear Admiral Allen Smith of the United States Navy held discussions with Galvão. Those too produced no result.

Galvão began to realise ever more clearly that his plans were faltering.

As a last effort, he stopped the ship outside the port and negotiated with the Brazilian government. Jânio Quadros had only just been elected President of Brazil, and he and Galvão had a close association.

Even so, Quadros, in his position as the head of a country, was unable to do anything further.

Galvão's plans were therefore reduced to dust. When the *Santa Maria* was brought into Recife harbour, all the passengers ran ashore, leaping with joy. The ship was returned to its owner. Galvão took asylum in Brazil. The chapter that shook the world came to an end.

(*Dravida Nadu*, Pongal special issue, 1963)

## Translator's Notes

The article recounts the January-February 1961 seizure of the Portuguese liner *Santa Maria*, also known as Operation Dulcinea. OCR-damaged Roman-script names were normalised in English using historical accounts: Lisbon, Venezuela, La Guaira, Humberto Delgado, Henrique Galvão, Curaçao, Mário Simões Maia, Saint Lucia, Trinidad, Recife, Jânio Quadros and Rear Admiral Allen Smith. The source calls Recife a Venezuelan port in one sentence, but it is in Brazil; the translation flags the printed form rather than silently reproducing that geographical contradiction. The text appears to compress or distort some operational details and names of wounded officers; its narrative has been translated as written, while the Tamil block remains verbatim.

## English Translation

A beautiful river flows along. On its bank a crane sits with a look of dejection. Its whole attention is fixed upon the moment when a good fish will be caught.

When a poet paints this scene, what comparison does he offer for the drooping crane? He compares it to Brahmins seated on the bank of a tank in the evening as though engaged in “prayer.” The crane sits there in apparent sorrow only because it wishes to seize, kill and eat a fish. In the same manner, the article says, Brahmins sit beside the tank in a posture resembling meditation upon Maheswara while pursuing self-interest.

Their character, the author argues, could not be explained with greater beauty or intelligence. Yet this was said neither by Self-Respecters accused of “hatred towards Brahmins” nor by members of the Justice Party accused of “labouring for office.” It was said many centuries ago, when Achariyar's ancestors wandered along riverbanks as priests while our ancestors ruled kingdoms. Long before Periyar Ramasamy was born, ancient Tamils had perceived, the author says, that the guise of prayer and austerity was a path for the pursuit of self-interest.

The *Kalithogai*, one of the Eight Anthologies praised by the learned, contains a verse describing the evening scene. In its damaged OCR form, it compares a heron settling upon the sandbank of the shining cool coast to a three-staff-bearing Brahmin recalling ancient teaching.

We believe this poem acts as a mirror showing what Tamils of former times thought about Brahmins.

One may ask why it is necessary to call the *Kalithogai* as a witness. When Madras Mayor comrade Satyamurti spoke at Erode on the 28th, he reportedly said, “I too am a Tamil.” He is also said to have called it foolish to divide people into Tamils and non-Tamils. Today we wish to say a few things about the nature of the sugary words of this Brahmin comrade, who claims Tamil kinship like a frog within a well.

Tamils acquired self-awareness. They began to identify their language, their art and their land, and to act upon the desire to live free of the binding grip of others' domination. Immediately, the article says, Aryans became alarmed and asked: “What is this strange development? What Aryan and what Dravidian? Were we not born and raised in Tamil Nadu? Do we not speak Tamil? Are we not Tamils?” They came forward to claim kinship. It was in this manner that the Mayor said with his tongue, “I too am a Tamil.” His mind, the author suggests, would have said something else.

There was a time when Brahmins proudly declared themselves Aryans. Now that the people have begun to understand that Aryan domination harmed the country's inhabitants in many ways, they have stopped that language and say instead, “We too are Tamils.” The article calls this a mere inversion and deception.

We did not create the distinction between Tamils and Brahmins, nor do we say it must remain forever. We would gladly welcome the unity of the entire human community. Ancient Tamils said, “Every place is our town; everyone is our kin.”

Yet, the author argues, from that age until today Aryans have lowered us and elevated themselves, created distinctions of every kind between themselves and us, and kept us suppressed. Aryans cannot deny it, and a Tamil with honour cannot forget it.

We put our question directly to the Mayor who now speaks evasively. By what right does he call himself a Tamil? In appearance, clothing, food, speech, feeling, employment and every other respect, where is he Tamil?

The article then caricatures the Mayor's Tamil in Brahmin dialect: “I know very well, don't I? The wife has gone to her elder sister's husband's house; the sweet rice is being made divinely.” His topknot, sacred thread, five-fold dhoti, *sandhyavandanam*, ancestral rites and death observances are described as Aryan marks continuing from the time of his forefathers. Which of these, the author asks, has the Mayor abandoned before calling himself Tamil?

The article next advances racial and bodily generalisations characteristic of its period. It claims that Brahmins came from a cold northern land with pale skin that turned reddish under the sun, while the bodies of Dravidian Tamils were formed through labour. It contrasts this with a caricature of an idle Aryan body. These assertions are part of the source's polemic and are not neutral biological description.

The Brahmin topknot, it continues, is not a recent possession but has existed for many centuries; it does not belong to the Dravidian.

The article quotes poem 202 of the Kurinji section of the *Ainkurunuru*. A friend sees the hero riding through the street. As the horse shakes its head, the tuft of hair upon its crown sways. She tells the heroine: “Mother, the horse comes dancing along the street; its crown looks like the swaying topknot of the young Brahmin in our town.”

The Brahmin topknot was thus, the author says, already an object of playful comparison and amusement in the age of the ancient Tamils, and remains so. He offers the example to the Mayor who has come forward to declare himself Tamil.

**Dress and Conduct**

The Brahmin's garment is the five-fold dhoti. Brahmin women traditionally wear their sari in a distinctive cross-draped style, and wearing the sacred thread is their custom. Some Tamils mistakenly thought Aryans superior and adopted the thread in a desire to resemble them, but it never naturally hung across the broad, powerful chest of the Dravidian, the article asserts.

Even Brahmin conduct, it says, had been described in those days. A classical verse portrays “the aged Brahmin,” living by alms and wandering from house to house throughout the town. The Dravidian, by contrast, is presented as one who places honour first and suffering later, and work before its consequences.

The author asks readers also to remember how ancient Tamils described Brahmin “character” and “occupation,” centuries before the appearance of those now branded “communalists.”

He quotes a classical list assigning Brahmins the tasks of speaking of the condition of love, describing the state of the chariot, conveying the elder's intent, interpreting omens involving cattle, and delivering messages concerning departure and its prevention.

Thus, the article concludes, from appearance through occupation Brahmins and Tamils have remained distinct until the present. In this condition, Brahmins call themselves a higher caste in many fields and lower us, yet the Mayor declares, “I too am a Tamil.”

“In what respect, sir, are you Tamil?” it asks. “From the crown of your head to the end of your feelings, are you not different from the Tamil? Are you the Tamil?”

Mayor, if there is even a little sincerity in your statement that you are Tamil, why did you bind yourself by a vow and work to prevent a Tamil named Sivasailam Pillai from becoming Education Officer in the Madras Corporation?

Why did you order the Tamil name “Thanikachalam Chettiar Road” removed and replaced with “Rangasami Iyer Road”?

Do you or your community treat us as equals? How do you treat us when associating with us? Why is there a separate *agraharam* in the village? Why a separate place at the bathing ghat? Why are all rights in the temples reserved for you? Why, even in 1940, is there a separate place for you in ordinary hotels? In which field have you behaved fraternally towards Tamils? Fold down a finger and count; let us see.

What difference is there, the article asks, between Aryans who occupy the highest place everywhere and in everything now sweetly claiming that they too are Tamils, and a Nazi parachutist landing in a country, pretending to be one of its people and doing harm?

Let the Mayor and the comrades thrilled by his speech answer.

The Mayor reportedly offered another blossom of opinion. He said that the people serving on the war committees now constituted by the government could accomplish nothing and that the committees were useless.

The article remarks that the committees contain no people who merely apply a surface coating and no one capable of hiding a whole pumpkin in a plate of rice.

The government must certainly take note of the Mayor's statement. Satyamurti's burning words strike the government itself.

The Governor and his government created the war committees to seek public support for Britain in the war. While such committees go among the public and labour to secure assistance for Britain, a Mayor declares without a tremor that they are useless. What opinion will ordinary people form of those committees after hearing him? If false statements about the committees lead the public to refuse the support they request, whose fault will it be?

The article tells the government: just as campaigning against assistance to Britain in the war is treated as an offence under the Defence of India Act, and the government arrests and punishes such campaigners, those who publicly abuse the people setting out to seek war support should likewise be liable under that Act.

Had the Mayor said, “Do not support Britain in the war,” he could have planted one particular idea in the people. By ridiculing the Governor's war-support committees, he has planted the very same idea.

It is improper to constitute committees seeking war support and then allow the Mayor and others to speak in a manner that destroys their standing.

The government must do one of two things.

It must arrest under the Defence of India Act anyone who maligns those seeking support for the war, whether that person is the Mayor or anyone else. Otherwise, it must dissolve the war committees.

To the Mayor who called himself Tamil, we have said: “Hero of the sacred grass! You are not Tamil. Here is the evidence.”

After the Mayor spoke maliciously about the government's war-support committee, it is the government's responsibility to tell him to “shut his mouth.” What is the government going to do?

*Viduthalai*, 29 June 1940.

## Translator's Notes

The printed title `அவரும் தமிழர்தானாம்!` was checked directly against Image 1. Straightforward OCR errors in the prose and Satyamurti's name were corrected in the authoritative Tamil source.

The article contains demeaning caste stereotypes, bodily generalisations, dialect mockery, and collective claims about Brahmins and Aryans. They are preserved as Anna's 1940 polemical rhetoric for archival fidelity; the translation does not endorse them as factual descriptions.

The classical Tamil quotations from *Kalithogai*, *Ainkurunuru*, and another cited verse contain spacing and OCR uncertainties. Their surrounding explanations are translated fully, while the quotations themselves are summarised conservatively rather than silently reconstructed into a critical text.

The final address `தர்ப்பர் சூரரே` is scan- and lexically uncertain, though it appears to be a satirical Brahminical epithet. “Hero of the sacred grass” is provisional and should not be reused as a definitive lexical translation without checking a better source edition.

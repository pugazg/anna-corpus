## English Translation

Before the Revolution in Russia, epidemics killed peasants by the thousands. Like Indian peasants, Russian peasants were simple people sunk in ignorance. Not knowing what to do when an epidemic arrived, they would attempt to drive out demons. One method frequently employed by the Russians was to harness widows to ploughs and drag them around the village. They believed that doing this would prevent demons from crossing the furrow and entering the village. Their belief was that ghosts and demons produced epidemics of this kind.

Before Soviet rule, Russian cultivators lived in indescribable poverty and unimaginable ignorance. Entire families were hereditary slaves of the nobles. Seven hundred nobles monopolised, ruled and enjoyed the land of Russia.

“The Russian cultivator lies in huts more foul-smelling than pigsties, in places with a stench worse than that of a match factory. In the rainy season he goes to bed by five in the evening because he cannot afford oil for a lamp. He has no meat, eggs, butter or even varieties of milk. He lives on dried bread and potatoes. Does he truly live? Even these do not fill his stomach, and he suffers,” writes E. J. Dillon.

Did the Tsar ask, “Alas! Why are my subjects, the very backbone of the country, in this condition?” No. He did not know.

When the full form of their suffering was finally shown to him, what did he do? Did he spring up in alarm and seek a remedy? No. He looked at the nobles and asked, “Why are these fellows such paupers?” The nobles summoned the priest and asked him to answer. “It is the consequence of his own deeds,” said the priest. The peasant continued to suffer, while the noble's authority suffered no diminution.

Did the cultivators ask, “We labour and are reduced to this condition; we change our very form so that the inhabitants of palaces of pleasure may live. Is there no deliverance for us?” They did ask. What answer did they receive while standing before places of worship? The ringing of bells!

* * *

“Farmers continue to face many hardships. Town dwellers do not know of them.

“Though villagers labour for eight or even ten hours, they do not obtain enough food to fill their stomachs. They have neither clothes to wear nor a house in which to shelter from rain and sun.

“Farmers do not ask for mansions or abundant luxuries. Food to eat, clothes to wear, a house to live in, medical facilities when ill and educational facilities for intellectual development are all they seek. Educational progress is essential. Many villages have no schools. Because of poverty, even those with ability and aptitude cannot pursue higher education. This condition must change.

“The distinction between rich and poor must be abolished in this country and an egalitarian state established. With the present revenue of forty-eight crore rupees, adequate facilities cannot be provided to farmers. The revenue is insufficient to give them electricity and other amenities.

“The Government must therefore raise a great deal of money. It will collect it from the wealthy through various taxes and spend it for the welfare of the poor.”

Comrade Kumarasami Raja, the Minister for Agriculture, spoke in these terms on 16 December at the Tirumangalam Taluk Farmers' Conference.

* * *

Unlike the Tsar's eyes of that earlier day, the Minister's eyes were not covered by arrogance. Knowing the hardships and losses of village people well, he spoke with concern and affection about the need to remedy their grievances. But it was only a speech. What action followed? What measures did he take?

“Every task cannot be accomplished immediately. Time is needed. Farmers must be patient,” preaches the Minister.

What answer is given to the farmer who asks, “Have all the years for which we have endured not been enough?” At that very time the Law Minister speaks. The Agriculture Minister says his heart melts on seeing the cultivator's distress; the Law Minister says that the farmer's agitation disturbs the peace and enforces the law.

In many parts of the country, particularly in Thanjavur district, farmers struggled for higher wages and a greater share of the crop. In some places, after suffering, they won. The countryside is experiencing an upheaval never seen before: cultivators refusing to till estate lands; mirasidars struggling because nobody will come for the harvest; estate owners inspecting fields under police guard; farmers gathering in crowds; cultivators going on their own to till fallow land; officials arriving to stop them. We see scenes such as these. Awakening today from deep sleep, the countryside is fighting hunger.

Our leaders carried the begging yoke to many countries of the world in search of food. Fear arose that famine might come. For the present, the seasons somehow helped and confidence arose that famine would not seize us in its terrible grip. Yet the farmer's condition in the village remains wretched.

This is the time for fundamental change in both land ownership and cultivation. The farmer has set out to cut away the chains of hereditary slavery. The village comrade is the very abode of patience. But when swelling anger takes hold, he will not retreat, be frightened or tire of fighting. Today he has acquired both the power to fight and a firm resolve.

Seeing this, the mirasidars grow anxious and call the law to their aid. What can the law do to reap ripened grain, make a withering field fertile or channel flowing water for irrigation? It can give a little more suffering to one accustomed to toil. It can try feeding gruel to one who drank porridge from an earthen pot. It can offer a secure place—a prison—to one who sleeps where snakes and scorpions crawl. What else can it do? Calling the law for assistance is useless; humanity must be called.

Estate owners may also tell one poor person to beat another. But neither the striking hand nor the beaten back can produce green crops in a field that is drying up.

Those who heard the heroic voice and heartfelt resolve of the awakened farmer in the play *One Night*, performed in Thanjavur for more than a hundred nights, know the truth that the cultivator is no longer asleep.

The cultivator shown with the title of this article truly comes from a farming family and is now an actor. It is the farmer's role played by our friend, the actor Sivasuriyan. That actor's speech is the speech of the farmers of the country today.

Look there at the cultivator! Have you noticed his body, like a log from which all oil has drained? Is he not the one who provides food for you and for the city of pleasure? Look at his feet. How much mud! Look at the pot of gruel: sour porridge is inside it. There is his wife, carrying a load of grass. His children graze buffaloes. The temple priest is within calling distance. Unmindful of the crooked course of his own life, he drives the plough and removes weeds.

At harvest time he will tell you. If you wish, you will go; otherwise you will send someone on your behalf. That man will exercise authority and ask why the yield is so low. Does the farmer ask, “What do you know of the suffering I endured? Do you know how many nights I lay awake in anxiety? I looked up at the sky a thousand times. I prayed to every god. How much hardship!” No. Without pulling a face, he says that he received what fate allotted him.

Though he has committed no crime, he stands before you like a criminal. Why? Because generation after generation of labouring for others and living only half a life has loaded burden upon burden until his very mind has bent. He cannot stand upright and ask why.

But understand one thing, sir. If he dares to stand erect and ask, “What manner of system is this? I labour throughout my life, yet poverty torments me. You intimidate the village and live in comfort, with no shortage of pleasure. Is this justice? How long will this continue?” you will be unable to answer. Nor can those who rule the village answer. Nor can the forces they bring together. That question is the first shot of revolution.

Today a rumbling can be heard. Tomorrow...?

## Translator's Notes

The colloquial title “எடக்கு பண்ணிக்கிட்டு...!” describes wilfully obstructive or contrary conduct; “Making Trouble...!” retains its spoken, accusatory tone. The website supplies two separately scanned images, 04 and 05, containing substantially duplicated text; both remain in the source block, while the translation avoids repeating the passage. Period terms such as `மிராசுதார்` are retained as `mirasidar` because they refer specifically to landed proprietors under the historical revenue system.

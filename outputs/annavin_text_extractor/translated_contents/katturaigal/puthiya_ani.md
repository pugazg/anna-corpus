## English Translation

The lady, like the Acharya, is truly fortunate.

Otherwise, would Periyar say, "This Maniammai possesses the qualification to conduct the movement properly for as long as I live and, as far as possible, even after my lifetime"?

It seems Periyar told the second delegation, "She has studied up to the tenth standard."

This lady possesses true attachment to and concern for his welfare and the welfare of the movement, qualities possessed by none of those who have laboured since the time when the movement consisted of only twenty or thirty people and endured beatings and kicks from its enemies. Our leader discovered this subtle truth during the five or six years in which he associated with the lady.

Even if it means forgetting the principles and contracting an incompatible marriage, he is making that lady our "leader." It seems the movement must have an heir!

Why the leader of a rationalist movement should experience the anxiety appropriate to the Nizam of Hyderabad or the heads of religious monasteries, we do not know!

Why an hereditary succession? Who is arranging it? To what age does this practice belong?

Is appointing an "heir" to a movement compatible with democracy? Or can it succeed in practice?

Is the Dravidar Kazhagam, along with the property said to belong to it, a jungle kingdom that must pass to another person by hereditary succession?

Was it for this that *Viduthalai* cried, "Throw in your silver coins and watch the work proceed"?

If the present leader of a movement believes that someone within it is a traitor or a wrongdoer, and can prove it, the proper course is to expel such people and allow those with genuine devotion to the movement and public influence to carry it forward after him. Is it proper instead to speak of hereditary title by declaring, "I entrust it to my heir"? Must a new dynasty be sought for that purpose?

The explanation issued by the leader on the seventeenth shows how deeply conceived his arrangement is.

In it he says, "I am greatly concerned that I must appoint someone as my heir and make arrangements through that person before I depart."

This explains what the "heir" appointed by him is for. Maniammai alone possesses the qualification to be this "heir." To make that lady his "heir," the leader evidently considers marrying her more suitable than any other arrangement.

A statement arrives in the spirit of, "Accept this; otherwise I shall destroy you." More may follow.

"I have no confidence in anyone in the movement. I see no one worthy of my trust. I do not believe that anyone else possesses the qualification and ability to conduct the movement."

Although Periyar had expressed these views many times before, comrades in the movement assumed that he spoke out of anger against this person or that, or merely to intimidate someone. Now he has made it clear.

He states firmly: "I have no confidence in any of you. More than that, I have confidence in Maniammai alone. I therefore make her my 'heir' by registering her as my wife!"

With his powers of propaganda, Periyar may henceforth even begin explaining this as "the revolutionary plan introduced under the name of marriage to make the party permanent and defeat traitors."

Yet we are compelled to reflect.

What purpose is served by our working under the leadership of one whose confidence we cannot deserve?

How can we trust someone who does not trust us? Why should we trust him?

If the leader declares that we cannot be trusted, how can we trust Maniammai enough to accept her as leader merely because he alone has confidence in her? Why should we?

Even the confidence we had until now concerning the lady has now been destroyed.

Until now we believed that she was a daughter serving the Father of the Dravidian people. No, the registered marriage now tells us that she cannot legally be called a daughter and must be called a wife. In these circumstances, Periyar's statement has brought fresh anguish to those already weeping over the incompatible marriage.

## The God Loki

“A troublemaker! A violent bully! A quarrelsome rogue with evil intentions! He lets no one live in peace. His crimes are countless!”

“Whom are you condemning so fiercely?”

“That unjust fellow walking there.”

“Him? You just spoke to him affectionately and behaved so meekly, addressing him with great respect.”

“Yes. What else could I do? I showed respect and affection to his face only as a pretence, to avoid trouble from that villain.”

“Then admit that you fear him.”

“Of course. He is a terrible sinner. How could I not?”

You may have heard such conversations about a local bully. People with a little caution and cowardice show him respect merely to avoid falling into his hands. Many consider such conduct toward a human dishonourable and ridiculous. Yet devotees adopted precisely this attitude toward certain gods.

The Teutons had a god who was cruel, unafraid of murder, and devoted to provoking conflict. Just as our land has Sani and Narada, theirs had a deity whose “sacred virtues” were betrayal, complicity in wrongdoing, and stealing whatever he found. He too had a temple. As one escapes a bully by speaking with a smile, Teutonic devotees worshipped this wicked god to escape his wrath.

The philosophies that God is the fountain and embodiment of love arose only after “civilization” entered the idea of divinity. Ancient doctrine recognized good and bad gods, and devotees had to worship both. That was theism and religion. Anyone who said gods should not be like this was an atheist and enemy of religion.

Even now villagers say that a certain deity’s anger will make blood flow from nose and mouth, strike a person down like a tree, blind the eyes, or seal the mouth. Teutons once lived in such a condition, though they do not now. They left that dark chamber for the broad world lit by the sun of reason.

While they remained there, they worshipped Odin, the primal god, and his heroic son Thor as guardians of the cosmos, but also worshipped harmful gods. Loki was one such god, Odin’s brother and Thor’s uncle. His pastime was tying the knot of conflict. He filled human minds with filth and stirred quarrels even among gods.

According to mythology, jealousy found among tribal chiefs also existed in heaven. Loki wondered why Odin’s fame and Thor’s influence should not be his. While outwardly socializing with them, he plotted, failed, was punished, muttered, and resumed the same wickedness. Such a god received devotion, worship, hymns, chariots, and festivals.

The Jotuns, hereditary enemies of the gods, decided to use Loki’s destructive nature for revenge. They sent three giant women into heaven. By magic the three assumed one form and entered as a maidservant. Loki became infatuated with this enchantress, who magnified the evil already smouldering in him and kept it rising like ceaseless waves.

The Jotun leader desired a beautiful goddess of heaven. The enchantress was commanded to ensnare her. The goddess was Freyja. Alas, even divine women faced this danger.

The gods wished to build a mighty wall to protect their realm from enemies. Today one wonders why gods needed such a plan, but then the thought was pure theism. Even here, “wise” people call anyone who rejects tales of Hanuman’s fortress of tails or Rama stroking the squirrel’s back an atheist. Teutons believed similarly before gaining knowledge.

A Jotun came disguised as a dwarf to build the wall. His terms angered the gods, who wished to drive him away. Loki, who treated mischief as a feast, cunningly persuaded them to agree. The builder demanded beautiful Freyja, and also the sun and moon. Loki assured the gods that the magician could not finish by their deadline, so they could punish him without losing Freyja. One god deceived and the others were deceived: deceiver and dupes alike were gods. Belief in such mythology was called religion.

The giant rapidly built the wall until only one day’s work remained. Heaven panicked and blamed Loki. Realizing his life was in danger, Loki learned that the builder’s magic horse supplied his power. He sent a mare to entice it. The horses ran into the forest in amorous play, work stopped, and the giant was killed for missing the deadline. Loki pinched the child and rocked the cradle too; creating such troubles was his occupation.

Once Loki’s beloved asked for the ornamental hair of the lovely goddess Sif. While Sif slept, Loki cut it off. The enraged gods caught and beat him. To appease them, he restored the hair and commissioned magical smiths to make splendid weapons as gifts.

The gods praised those smiths. Loki reported this to others and provoked rivalry, leading them to make still finer weapons. One was Thor’s hammer. While the divine smith Brok forged it, Loki took the form of an insect and stung him. Brok endured the pain, completed the hammer, gave it to Thor, and demanded Loki’s head as payment. Loki cunningly replied, “Take my head, but mind that you do nothing to my neck.” The gods agreed, frustrating Brok. Saying it was enough if Loki’s quarrelsome speech no longer escaped, Brok sewed his mouth shut. Such was the mythology.

Despite all these vices, Thor supposedly took Loki as companion on his divine exploits. On one journey they encountered a magical realm of ice-built forts, towers, and palaces inhabited by gigantic monsters.

On their way to fight the Jotuns, Thor and Loki stood before its king, who mocked them: “Gods, are you? Why have you come, and what can you do?” Loki boasted that he could eat any amount of meat, but lost an eating contest to a monster. He then claimed no one could equal his speed, yet a dwarf defeated him in a race. Thor boasted that he could drink without limit but lost. The king asked him to lift a cat, and he could not. An old woman defeated Thor in wrestling. Gods suffered defeat upon defeat. Reason rejects such tales as unfitting, but theism of that age demanded belief, and Teutons obeyed.

The magic city suddenly vanished. Laughing, the giant explained that all they had seen was illusion: Fire defeated Loki at eating; Thought defeated him in running; Thor had been given the sea to drink; the cat was really Midgard, the serpent that terrified the cosmos; and Old Age won the wrestling match. He offered this philosophical interpretation and disappeared.

Some here take pride in supplying philosophy for our myths. The Teutons too possessed that stratagem. Yet when clarity arose, they rejected these stories as a disgrace to the very idea of God and sought true knowledge.

After this experience, Thor and Loki went to Jotunland, won their battle, and returned. On the way Thor discovered his marvellous hammer was missing. Without it, the gods’ world could not survive. Loki became a bird, flew to Jotunland, and learned that King Thrym had stolen and buried it nine miles underground. He would return it only if beautiful Freyja were given to him in marriage.

Following Loki’s advice, Thor disguised himself as Freyja and Loki acted as her companion. They attended the wedding feast. When the hammer was presented as a wedding gift, Thor cast off the disguise and pulverized the giant. Just as our mythologists invented Vishnu’s Mohini incarnation, the Teutonic mythologist spun this yarn. Today we casually call it a yarn, but saying so then could cost one’s head, so deep were devotion and frenzy.

Loki even murdered a god. Odin had sons besides Thor, just as Siva has Murugan besides Vinayaka. As Murugan means beauty in religious and literary imagination, Odin too had a “Murugan,” named Balder. Loki plotted to kill him. Religion ordered devotees to read without alarm and continue worshipping, and people submitted. Loki’s sole reason for murdering his nephew was that all the other gods loved Balder. Such was this god’s character.

One day Balder became terrified of death. Odin tried many things but could not console him. His grieving mother appealed to every object and living being in every world and made each swear not to harm him. Thinking it harmless, she neglected only mistletoe.

The gods rejoiced that beautiful Balder was now safe. Mountain, flower, iron, cotton, water, or fire could be thrown at him and he merely smiled. Jealous Loki approached a blind god and asked why he alone did not join the game. He handed him mistletoe; the innocent blind deity threw it, and Balder instantly died. The gods wailed and Odin rolled upon the ground in grief.

Everyone suspected Loki and ostracized him. He fled to a cave, but Thor captured and bound him to a great rock. By Odin’s order, deadly venom dripped upon Loki’s face and made him writhe as though burned by fire. This villain nevertheless had a devoted wife. Like Savitri, she sat constantly beside him catching the poison in a bowl. Whenever she left to empty it, a few drops fell upon Loki and his convulsions caused earthquakes. The Teutons once believed this.

A god who provoked conflict, assumed a woman’s form, died by mistletoe, could not lift a cat, committed murder, and cut off hair: before gaining clarity, Teutons fashioned such stories and called them theism. Later they saw how deranged the mind must be to accept fraudsters’ rotten inventions as divine history. Disgust arose, questions and condemnation followed, religious advocates roared, and crowned rulers inflicted cruelty. Reason did not submit. After great struggle it defeated these unworthy ideas, and Loki became a former god.

## The Babylonian Creation of the Universe

“Uncle, what is that twinkling thing?”

“A star.”

“What is a star?”

“A star is something that shines.”

“I can see it shines. What is it, why is it like that, where is it, and why is it there?”

“Be quiet. You keep chattering. It is a star, twinkling above.”

“Go away! You get angry because you cannot answer. I’ll ask this other uncle; he is good and will tell me. Big uncle, what is it?”

“What are you asking about, child? Oh, the star?”

“Yes. What is a star?”

“The gods are up above, aren’t they?”

“Are the gods above? Aren’t they here in the temple?”

“The real gods are above. When they smoke cigars, their fire flashes, just as cigars and cigarettes do here.”

“So a star is the fire of a god’s cigar! This uncle is a complete fool; he did not know and became angry when I asked.”

The boy rejoiced and repeated the storytelling uncle’s answer to children his age. Many came to believe stars were the embers of divine cigars. It was false, but a truthful answer would not help a child too young to understand the details. The cunning uncle therefore calmed his curiosity with a comprehensible lie, and the boy imagined he understood stars.

How long could that lie remain after his mind matured enough to grasp truth? There have been many such “uncle’s stories,” some told with good intentions and others solely to deceive.

When humanity was in childhood, natural sights and events raised questions that forced people to invent stories. In time fraudsters used them for private profit. As false stories became a livelihood, storytellers and stories multiplied. Every unexplained object and event acquired a tale. In the hands of deceivers, these stories became like a burglar’s crowbar.

As knowledge grew, people gained the maturity to reject stories and recognize them as the deceiver’s means of survival. The tales became unwanted, not here, but in other countries that enriched their minds. Here there are many “intellectuals” who condemn those who laugh at invented stories.

“Do you refuse belief, atheist? Can you not believe, great sage? You do not understand its glory, dull-witted sinner! These are stories, yes, but not mere stories. Great ascetics graciously supplied them, laden with special philosophy, to explain rare divine secrets. Do not mock them!”

They continue: “Taken literally, asking what each means and whether it could happen will certainly confuse the mind. But these stories supply noble lessons and morals for human advancement. Reject the tale if you must, but would losing its morals not be base?”

Defenders of antiquity have many such stages of argument, all serving refusal to abandon old stories.

Most people here have not developed the clarity and courage to reject as mythological merchandise tales once assigned to phenomena that lacked explanations. Thus they still believe meaningless old stories about cosmic creation and development, call that belief theism, and organize worship, ritual, and festivals upon it, wasting wealth, intellect, and time.

An explanation of cosmic origins was not easily obtained. Human intelligence and research developed step by step, and science now supplies one. Before it did, every country invented tales like the crafty uncle’s answer about the star, and priestly groups used them to control people.

The Teutons believed one story of creation and devised worship accordingly. Babylonia invented another. Like the Teutonic tale, it began with nothingness: no sky, earth, god, human, sun, or moon. Everywhere was sea, its bounds unknown. At its bottom lived the primordial god Apsu.

Though nothing else existed and he dwelt in the deep, Apsu had the goddess Tiamat as companion. For a long age there was nothing more. Then the great sea churned. Why? A divine secret: asking was sinful, belief was theism. From the turbulent deep emerged two gods, Lahmu and Lahamu, male and female. The abyss slept again for ages.

Another pair arose: Anshar and Kishar. After another sleep came Anu and his goddess. Finally mighty Ea appeared, accompanied by the goddess Damkina. Their son Bel began producing humanity, roughly like Brahma in our mythology.

Apsu disliked Bel’s conduct and grew jealous of Ea’s rising strength and influence. Yes, a god became jealous. Taking his son Mummu, Apsu went to complain to Tiamat. You laugh because this is Babylonian mythology; any thoughtful person would. Babylonians themselves laughed once knowledge arose. Wherever knowledge prevails, no one now believes such tales. There is no fault in laughing, but after doing so please remember our own mythology.

Even today our temple mythologist reads that Indra, unable to bear demonic torment, led the gods to Hari reclining upon Adisesha in the Milk Ocean, fell like an uprooted palm, and implored: “Sriman Narayana, consort of Lakshmi, bearer of Panchajanya! I prostrate before you. I cannot endure the sinners’ cruelty. Only you, wielder of the discus, can destroy them. Protector in danger, guardian of the helpless, show me grace, annihilate the demons, and save heaven.” This is not Babylon but our local bhajan temple. Do you laugh? Is that not sin? Before Hari rises to relieve Indra, the mythologist who narrated it melodiously drinks milk donated by Parthasarathi Mudaliar’s wife.

Babylonia dismissed its ancient story as childish madness centuries ago. Here mythology is still recited and reason treated as sin. Old mythology tastes like panchamritam. Brows furrow at questions; whoever seeks explanation is called a sophist, and whoever refuses belief or mocks it is branded atheist even by his own family.

Babylon once stood where we stand today. When knowledge arose, its people flung away the priestly creation tale they had believed before reason dawned.

Tiamat replied to the gods who appealed to her: “Yes, Ea’s actions disturb the peace of the abyss. He must be destroyed. Tell me what shall be done.” Ea had secretly overheard the whole council and used his strength to imprison Apsu and Mummu.

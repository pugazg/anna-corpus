13. ராஜா சர் அண்ணாமலைச் செட்டியார்
அவர்களுக்குப் பகிரங்கக் கடிதம்!

ராஜா சர். அண்ணாமலைச் செட்டியார் அவர்களே!

பலகாலமாகப் பஞ்சமும் பிணியும் மிகுந்து, ஏழைகள்
புழுப்போல் துடித்துப் பரிதவிக்கும் இந்த நாட்டிலே, அதே
நேரத்திலே மதத்தின் பெயரால், மக்கள் பணத்தை வாரி
இறைத்துப் பாழ்செய்வதைத் தாங்கள் அறிவீர்கள். இந்த
நாட்டிலே, பல கோயில்களிலே, முடங்கிக் கிடக்கும் செல்வமும்
அவ்வப்போது வீணாக்கப்படும் பணமும், ஏழைகளிடமிருந்து
அவர்களுடைய உழைப்பின் மூலமாகத் தட்டிப் பறிக்கப்பட்டது
என்பதை உணரப் பிரமாதமான மேதாவித் தனம் தேவை
யில்லை, சாதாரணமாகப் புரியக்கூடியதே. இவ்வளவு
செல்வமும் ஏழைகளை ஈடேற்ற, அவர்களுக்கு வாழ்க்கைக்கு
வழிகாட்டும் தொழிற்சாலைகள் அமைக்கப் பயன்படுமானால்,
எவ்வளவு நன்மை கிடைக்கும் என்பதை எண்ணும்போது,
இதனைத் தடுக்கும் மதப்பித்தம் மட்டந் தட்டப்பட்டாலொழிய
மக்கள் மீளமார்க்கமில்லை என்று எவருக்கும் தோன்றும்.
திருத்தலங்கள் எனப்படுபவை பல. அவைகளுக்கு மகத்துவம்
கற்பிக்கத் தீட்டிய கதைகளும் பல. அக்கதைகளை நம்பி
ஏமாந்த மக்கள், காசைக் கரியாக்கிய கதைபோல, உலகிலே
மடைமையும் கொடுமையும் நிரம்பிய வேறோர் கதை பார்க்க

முடியாது.

இப்படிப்பட்ட திருத்தலங்களிலே ஒன்று
திருவண்ணாமலை; அங்கும் அதைச் சுற்றியுள்ள

## English Translation

Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar,

You know that in this country, where famine and disease have long prevailed and the poor writhe and suffer like worms, people's money is at the same time poured out and wasted in the name of religion. No extraordinary genius is needed to understand that the wealth lying idle in many temples in this country, and the money periodically wasted there, was wrested from the poor through their labour. When one considers how much good could be achieved if all this wealth were used to uplift the poor and establish industries that would show them a way to live, anyone must feel that the people have no path to recovery unless the religious frenzy obstructing this is curbed.

There are many so-called holy places, and many stories devised to confer greatness upon them. Nowhere in the world can one find another story so full of folly and cruelty as that of deceived people who believed those tales and turned their money into charcoal.

One such holy place is Tiruvannamalai. Even today, many thousands of poor people in and around it suffer and wonder why they should live. They labour like cattle for a single meal. Many go hungry at night, and some remain hungry by day. There are people who regard even gruel and fermented porridge as though they were milk and fruit. They rise with the fowl, labour like animals, and pass the night in misery with an arm for a pillow. Countless homes never see ghee. Countless people think, when illness comes, that nothing but death can follow, for where is the money for medicine? How many labour until their sinews snap in the weaving trade and still cannot eat their fill! There are people, orphans, helpless women, refugees, sufferers from disfiguring disease, and people with streaming eyes, by the thousands in that town.

All this poverty and distress exists even now in Tiruvannamalai, where Annamalaiyar dwells. But inside the temple! The spectacle in which Tiruvannamalaiyar is enthroned, and the scale on which people have contributed wealth to make it magnificent, are enormous. How can anyone accept these words unless they are in a position to regard insult itself as homage? No one can call you a person so devoid of general knowledge of the world as to say that this is no concern of ours. Can you be the one to do this?

If people who do not understand the country's condition, and who cannot feel the anguish of those waiting after sending death an invitation to take life from its cage, say, “What if famine dances wildly? I shall perform a panchamirtha ablution and worship Palaniappan,” there is a reason for it. Such people reject knowledge and desire only the temple, and can be cast aside. But you founded a university; you declared that culture and music must become one; you know the nature of the poor man's tears. You know moral teaching and understand that if love is offered as the ablution in the temple called the heart, God will accept that alone with joy. You reign in the world of scholars. In the very field of charity, you showed a new path to Tamil Nadu generally and to the land of the mercantile community especially.

At this hour, when the poor suffer, and on a day when the government itself campaigns against squandering the increase in money and urges people to save it for use during the monetary scarcity that will follow the war, is it right to spend a great sum on an act without necessity, urgency, or benefit? Is it good for the country or suited to the time? Will it not draw onto a wrong road many wealthy men who think your course ought to be their own? Tell me.

Some arrogant people will twist this article and say that I condemn religion, insult Maheswara, and attack the wealthy. I know that you, a person of culture, do not approve of their conduct. I also know that you will receive loving counsel of this kind with a smile rather than blaze up and ask who I am to find fault with your method. I therefore speak without concealment. This deed saddens me and shames me. I ask, as one possessing the right to ask, why you came forward to undertake it.

Raja Sir! In Tamil Nadu there is no worthy body other than the Self-Respect Tamils to honour, praise, and support you, and to stand beside you and fight when Aryanism attacks. That is the condition of the country, not empty writing. There are calculating performers who praise you loudly rather than sincerely, secure an interview, and lead you toward the treasury. They say that everything you do is right. We are not like them. We say that a fault is a fault even if a third eye is shown. Nor are we people who recite the *Tirumurugatrupadai* out of fear that we will drown in water. When pointing out a fault, we will not bow our heads even if a sword or cudgel comes. We do not change our position or trade with our tongues. Our course is distinctive; you know that well.

Can giving a young, flowering woman in marriage to an aged Brahmin be called love shown to that blind man? If compassion arises for a blind man, it is proper to give him a staff for his hand; is it right to offer a maiden for intercourse? Does anyone who delights in a cobra's dance think of lifting it to the crown of his head and kissing it? If such a thought arises and is acted upon, can that person thereafter remain alive in the world? Alms must be given to a worthy vessel; a woman must be given with regard to her age. If life is to prosper, an ill-matched marriage becomes a draught of injustice.

The revolutionary poet Bharathidasan presents a bedchamber scene to explain an ill-matched marriage. A grey-haired man with an open mouth and a face without a smile lies snoring on a bed. A lamp in that room shines red, it is said, from the redness of the young woman's cheek. Youth and beauty belong to her; decrepitude and horror to him. She is a princess of the realm of delight. He has lost the right even to remain in that inner chamber. Her limbs gleam with the qualities of youth. Time has wholly ruined his body: its bonds are loose, it has wasted, dried, and grown weary, and is fit only for the funeral rite. Is he a match for her?

Her mother, unable to understand this, bound them together and lay thinking that both lived in pleasure. One day she happened upon the truth: the sweet-spoken woman had resolved that poison was a better feast than suffering beside that tottering man. That is the cruelty of an ill-matched marriage.

Compare the money you intend to spend with that innocent woman, compare Aryanism with that old man, and compare the consecration with the marriage. See yourself in the position of that mother. Money will be ruined as in an ill-matched marriage. Milk bursting with richness will become still more feasting, hospitality, gifts, dhotis, and honours for those who wander well-fed and burdened with flesh, while the hungry lie like underfed cattle whose bellies do not fill even after grazing. Is this charity? Is it a virtuous deed? Or is it an incident showing that Aryanism has caught even the king of Chettinad firmly and strikes him on the head, demanding principal and interest without delay? Can people like us, who value thought, refrain from considering it?

I do not say, nor can I expect, that you must adopt a revolutionary change in believing religion or desiring a religious path. Let anyone who wishes declare that this human body is the product and consequence of deeds, flesh cast outside when its maker leaves it; that it is the abode of attachment, a vessel of faults, a serpent's anthill, and a gathering of enmity. Let them understand the human frame in that way, cease regarding it as wondrous, sing, “This body is false, merely a bag filled with air,” recite, “Even an eyeless needle will not come with us along the final road,” praise and worship the form of Satchidananda, and ask, “Great Lord, grant me freedom from rebirth.” I will not stand in their way.

But when wealth, time, and thought that ought to benefit people, especially people who suffer like motherless children, are used in the name of religion and devotion for acts such as consecrations that are useless, can produce only harm, and can feast only wastrels and rogues, it appears to me even crueller than an ill-matched marriage.

Through your compassion Annamalaiyar will be bathed in ablution. At that very time, in how many homes will poverty bathe your own siblings, Annamalaiyar's children, in tears? If the two were photographed simultaneously and shown together, your cultured heart would be wounded.

A hundred thousand lamps or chandelier globes may shine in the temple through your donation. Devotees may praise the Lord as radiant with the brilliance of ten million suns, and use light to see him clearly. But at that same time, how many homes will be surrounded by darkness? How many lives will be darkened? Who will remind you of that then?

On one side drums and cymbals will sound amid dancing, singing, coupling, searching, seeking, and running. Elsewhere devotional singing, inquiries after courtesans, litanies of names, and poetic garlands to women will proceed. In that same place and hour one can see beggars extending their hands after displaying leprosy and driving away the swarms of flies settled upon it. The blind, the hunchbacked, children, people who have lost limbs, helpless women, and orphans lie at the gate of Annamalaiyar's temple with no one to support or console them. They are there not from faith that his grace will reach them, but from the hope that some fragment of the wealth of a rich man seeking his grace may fall to them.

Many poisoned hearts, seeking alluring women suited to the soft bed and taking refuge in excessive lust, arrive and cry aloud, “Arohara! Annamalaiyar!” Then they say, “Look at her! Alamu, come!” They tug at a garment and delight in touching a chin. If they see someone they call a nuisance, they trip him, clap and laugh, and wink. Men with eyes sharper than sharp scissors smear themselves thickly with sacred ash, stand, fall in worship, and finish their work.

Then elegant women cry out that a long gold necklace or the screw from their hair has vanished. The police take notes. A husband complains, “I poured out money for it,” while a woman who has lost her jewel loses her smiling face. Those nearby inquire into the jewellery-losing woman's grief; once at a distance, they mock her: “She deserved it. The unrestrained little woman sways whenever she sees a man. Only losing a jewel will teach her sense.”

One devotee sings in the Todi raga. If the young woman standing at the edge is captivating, he sings of the sin of hero and heroine. One youth says, “Come, my dear!” and calls for a Mangamma dance on seeing a woman's gait. Another sings, “Look at me, look at my beauty, look closely.” Another raises the pitch with “O Swami!” Yet another youth sings movingly, “Are there any who do not swoon at seeing you?”

The favourite women of priests, the “familiar dolls” of wealthy devotees, ornamented women known to officials, pleasure-givers favoured by musicians, and women who seize upon men with money all help increase the splendour of devotion. Tank banks become swings; halls become couches. The power of devotion brings new couples together and acts as a tonic for old couples. Many things happen when eyes meet at such festivals.

The whole sacred utterance is not *Tiruppallandu*. It becomes praise of a married woman, a bedchamber centenary, a lyric of desire, and a *viraha* venba, with poetic flavour flowing through it. Reality will be greater than what I have written, not less.

My questions are whether this is an act that satisfies God, and whether, in this country and at this time, people like you may waste money on such an act. I do not ask because you are bound to answer me. I raise these questions because questions like them should stir your thought.

Will God, Siva, bestow grace only if a consecration is performed for him? Can anyone tell you, who established a charitable institution, a university that gives the eye of knowledge to so many thousands of young people, that grace will not reach you unless you perform this consecration? Whichever Puranas and epics extol the greatness of services such as consecration, do the many stories in those same books give us even the confidence that God thinks at leisure and distributes his grace fittingly to the deserving? They do not!

Father of Tamil music! You follow the path of righteousness, possess a compassionate nature, and perform public service. Are these not enough to draw Easwaran's grace to your doorstep? Listen, and I shall tell you the kinds of people upon whom Siva bestowed his grace. This is not my invention; I take it from the Puranic books.

Long ago, in the country of Kampili, a man named Velvithathan had a son named Gunanidhi. Wine and women were his worship of Maheswara. Gunanidhi spent his time massaging the flower-like feet of desirable women and, after learning their wishes, caressing their bodies. His mother concealed the matter so that his father would not know, fearing that he would punish the son for his misconduct.

The matter later came to light. When he learned it, Velvithathan condemned the mother for failing to rebuke the son who committed improper acts, and married another woman. Gunanidhi thought that he needed neither mother nor father so long as he had dancing women and drink. He lay at the feet of beautiful women until his wealth ran dry.

On a Sivaratri night he entered a temple where wealthy devotees of Siva had gathered, not with thoughts of Siva but to steal property. The host of devotees closed their eyes while he searched for valuables. There was no light. He therefore trimmed the lamp, thinking that brighter light would make theft easier. He stole, stumbled, and fell. The sleeping people awoke, caught him red-handed, beat him, and killed him.

Anyone may ask whether that is the whole story. Had it ended there, it would have taught people a moral: he rejected his father's word, did what was wrong, and was killed. The Puranist feared that lesson, and did not end Gunanidhi's tale there. There is more. Listen.

“When Gunanidhi died, Yama's servants arrived to take him away.”

You may say, “Now we are speaking plainly! Did they drag Gunanidhi to hell?”

“Oh! First they would have taken him to Yama, Chitragupta would have examined his account, and only then would they have punished him. So Yama's messengers dragged Gunanidhi to Yama's court. Is that not the story?” you may ask.

“No. At the very moment Yama's servants came to drag Gunanidhi away, Siva's attendants appeared and threatened them: ‘We are taking this man. You must not touch him.’”

You might say, “Because he entered Siva's temple and stole Siva's jewels, Siva must have sent his attendants to bring him there so that he could examine him directly and impose punishment.”

That is not what happened either. “Gunanidhi is supremely virtuous, a devotee of Siva, and has received Siva's grace,” said Siva's attendants.

You will ask me how a scoundrel who stole in Siva's temple on the sacred day of Sivaratri could receive Siva's grace. Yama's servants asked Siva's attendants exactly that. “Do you not know? This Gunanidhi trimmed the lamp in Siva's temple on Sivaratri. Was that not a great virtuous deed? Siva has favoured the devotee who served him on Sivaratri,” the attendants said, driving away Yama's forces. Thus Gunanidhi, who had trimmed the lamp in order to steal, received Siva's grace and was born in the Kalinga country as Daman, son of King Arundaman.

King of Chettinad! While wandering amid drink and dancing women, he entered the temple to steal and trimmed a lamp for the sake of theft. Siva is said to have regarded this as a virtuous act worthy of divine grace. To what kind of justice does this belong? What kind of lesson can it give the people? Does our Siva not know the difference between a thief and a devotee? Would a god bestow grace like this? Should he?

If an employee you appoint steals and eats fruit purchased for you, could you think: “His eating that fruit was right. Had he not eaten it, I would have eaten it and suffered stomach pain. I should increase the salary of this employee who helped spare me pain”? Could you, or anyone else, think so and give him a reward? Yet our Siva is said to have bestowed grace because this thief, libertine, and drunkard trimmed a temple lamp for his stealing!

This bestowal of grace upon an unworthy man, found in the sacred tale called *Kasi Kandam*, does it belong to justice? Must you, who live righteously, perform a separate consecration to ask grace from Hara, who favours such a degraded man? One might bring a case against Hara in the court of reason: without right or reason, and without even understanding the nature of devotion, God is said to have shown kindness because a thief's hand trimmed a lamp. Does that not show that Hara lacks deep reflection?

Even the servant who deceives his master with an ingratiating smile has no respect for a master who gives him a dhoti and money. “Our master is a simpleton; I can fool him in a moment,” the servant will say. Had Gunanidhi, who remained sunk in lust until Sivaratri, gone to the temple that night to steal, witnessed the virtue of Siva's devotees, reflected, beheld Siva, regretted his former state, and appealed to the Lord, “Crescent-crowned one, forgive my fault,” and had the bearer of the pure white moon then appeared riding the bull, the Purana would have had some meaning. But nothing of that kind occurs. Even when Siva's attendants arrive, the story does not say that Gunanidhi acquired devotion or virtue. If he nevertheless received Siva's grace, is that fitting?

Why should Hara, who bestowed grace so incongruously upon base people, need a consecration before showing compassion to a good person like you? King of commerce, hear another strange tale, another sacred tale! This is no mere amusement.

God once bestowed grace upon three beings: a bull, a donkey, and an outcaste woman. You will ask why. The bull did not repeatedly bow in worship. The donkey did not melt in tears and plead with Kapali. The woman performed no worship. They happened to be beaten opposite a Vinayakar temple. To escape those beating them, the bull, donkey, and woman ran around the temple. This was regarded as the sacred act of circumambulation, and grace was bestowed upon them. As they ran, grass fell before the temple from the woman's lap and from the mouths of the bull and donkey. This was regarded as an offering. God's favour is said to have reached the three devotees who offered worship and circled the temple.

Neither the bull, donkey, nor woman acted from devotion while running around the temple. They did not sing in search of the Supreme; they ran because their backs could not bear the blows. Hear the full story.

Long ago, a king named Sulaban ruled Avanti. His queen was Sulabai. One day a Brahmin named Madhusudanan begged for alms, and the king laughed at him. Enraged, the priest said, “Arrogant king, I curse you to become a bull.” Sulabai counter-cursed him: “You who failed to forgive a mistake the king committed unknowingly shall be born a donkey.” The Brahmin then cursed her: “You shall be born an outcaste woman.” Accordingly, Sulaban was born a bull, Madhusudanan a donkey, and Sulabai an outcaste woman.

One day in a village, before a Vinayakar temple, the bull pulled at the bundle of grass the woman had gathered, and she struck it. The donkey pulled grass from the bull; the bull butted it, and the donkey kicked back. Some Brahmins who saw this mischief beat both animals and the woman. Unable to bear the blows, the bull and donkey ran round and round the temple; the woman also ran, and grass fell. This was regarded as circumambulating the temple and performing worship, and divine grace was supposedly born.

What can one understand from this? Mere appearance is taken for devotion even by the Supreme. How is one to describe this pitiable state? If a jeweller paid the price of a real diamond for an imitation jewel, what would the shopping street think of him? If he continued paying the price of genuine diamonds for false ones, how long would his shop survive? The two incidents I have shown describe a counterfeit god dispensing grace. How many thoughts they provoke!

See here how far caste arrogance goes. The Puranist makes the woman into an animal species like the bull and donkey. See how much caste arrogance this displays! These very Puranas and epics say that God's grace can be won by performing ablutions, worship, consecrations, and other services. I ask: can you believe these Puranic works? Can you waste wealth through belief in them?

When the consecration takes place, many will praise your “charitable thought.” The priestly crowd will flatter you. Puranists will say that the consecration has earned you exceptional merit. A Tevaram devotional group will perform on one side. On another, discourses will narrate the *Tiruvilaiyadal*, *Periyapuranam*, and similar stories. Kripananda Variyar will stand elsewhere. In another place will stand ornate preachers, learned pundits, and connoisseurs of art. Everyone will praise you and applaud your deed. But each of them will speak differently in private.

People like me cannot be present in that great gathering. At that very time, however, we will be thinking, “What a good man! And on what a bad act his money is being wasted!” The number of those who respect our view is small, but it is growing. Any place that gives room to informed thought will certainly not applaud your deed.

Is that all? If you yourself reflect calmly, you will realize that this act is neither necessary nor suited to the people's needs. I will be satisfied if this letter serves only to awaken that thought in your mind.

I am not a madman who imagines that you will stop the consecration the moment you see this letter. Nothing but a government order can create such an “obstruction.” I have issued this letter so that you will not suppose that only singers of praise exist; so that you do not undertake more such acts; so that many others do not follow your example into the same action; and so that our nature of declaring a wrong to be wrong, even when committed by a Raja Sir, suffers no dishonour.

Do not think otherwise in your heart. Do not dismiss this as a matter unworthy of regard. Consider whether it is truly necessary.

People of complete understanding, who do not forget what they have learned, who do not surrender dignity for livelihood, and who clearly know the doctrine of God, have stated firmly that such temple works are empty ostentation and do not satisfy the Supreme. I shall quote one or two of them. Thiru. V. Kalyanasundaranar, who never forgot Saivism, once wrote in his journal:

“While walking gods
fill the country,
and Brahmins dwell
in temples of images,
Tamilian, do not lose your money
in the name of a stone god!”

A Siddhar asks with some anger:

“Your god is stone;
the god carried in procession is bronze;
we are the god that speaks;
what other god is there?”

Pattinathadigal asks, “Does our three-eyed Lord dwell in stone and bronze?”

Only Brahmins tell you of the greatness of temple renovation, and they waste money. They waste resources that ought to serve the country's welfare, the afflicted, and the walking gods. I ask whether it is right for you to follow the road they show. Do not be angry.

Yours affectionately,

Bharathan

Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 28 May 1944.

## Translator's Notes

The translation preserves all twenty-nine scans in order: the opening poverty argument, the complete temple expenditure catalogue and recurring expenses, the appeal to Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar, the ill-matched-marriage analogy, the festival critique, both Puranic examples, all three closing quotations, the signature Bharathan, and the dated publication credit.

The title ராஜா சர் அண்ணாமலைச் செட்டியார் அவர்களுக்குப் பகிரங்கக் கடிதம் is rendered “An Open Letter to Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar.” The historical honorific “Raja Sir” and the author's direct second-person address are retained throughout.

Several catalogue terms name ornaments, ritual fittings, vehicles, or old monetary entries for which exact modern English equivalents are uncertain. They are rendered conservatively from context. மகிரி is transliterated as *magiri*; லஸ்தர் குளோப்புகள் is rendered “chandelier globes”; and தளவரிசை is rendered “floor paving.” These terms are also recorded in the separate difficult-terms register.

The printed label of the final Rs. 2,700 entry on scan 5 is physically obscured. Its amount is readable, but neither the Tamil source nor the English translation invents a label. This source defect is recorded separately in the OCR-concerns ledger.

Seventy-seven correction events were applied before translation. They restore the title, damaged prose and quoted verse, detached or omitted table amounts, and numerous scan-backed readings across all twenty-nine pages. Period spelling, colloquial Brahmin dialect, rhetorical claims, Puranic narratives, and the author's polemical language are preserved rather than silently modernised or reconciled with later scholarship.

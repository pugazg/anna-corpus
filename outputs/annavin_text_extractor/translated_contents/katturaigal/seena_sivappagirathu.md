கொண்டு, அதற்குத் தகுதிவாய்ந்த தலைவரைத் தேர்தெடுத்துக்

## English Translation

“Even if American assistance were increased tenfold, China cannot be saved unless it somehow strengthens its internal power and chooses a leader worthy of it.”

*New York Herald Tribune*

“Unless some startling changes occur in the Kuomintang organisation, it may be concluded that the rule of Chiang Kai-shek and his associates is approaching its end.

“For a long time that group has been inviting defeat. It adopted immoral methods as instruments of its course and exercised autocracy. It made no effort to approach genuine Chinese leaders with democratic qualities and establish a strong government.

“America should have made political reform a condition of its aid. Instead, that aid was poured into the bottomless pit of Kuomintang greed.”

*News Chronicle*

The newspapers of America and Britain respectively write in these terms about the Chinese dictator Chiang Kai-shek after seeing the defeat of Chinese government troops in Manchuria.

America is said to have received reliable “news” that Chiang’s own associates in power have resolved to remove him from office. American military officers and influential experts reportedly welcome such a change in China, though they do not say so openly. They had long known that Chiang was a major obstacle to reorganising the Chinese government. American observers had also concluded that he was wholly unfit to serve as a military commander and that his colleagues confused affairs, lacked integrity, and possessed narrow minds. This is neither abuse nor slander; it is a true portrait of Chiang Kai-shek and his adherents.

Those who proudly claim that spiritual wisdom once blazed in the East to a degree never witnessed in the West, and who say that adopting it is the noble path indispensable to the future welfare of Western countries, should reflect upon China’s ancient history.

Had Chinese society been founded upon good culture, would that country’s long history remain a caricature fit for the mockery of others? Elsewhere in the world, are there ruling masses of flesh like the petty savage kings who appeared in every part of China, ignored popular welfare, arranged only their own magnificent lives, tormented the people in many ways, and submerged them in poverty and ignorance?

If rulers in Western countries behaved so, one might find it unsurprising and even pardonable, for those countries supposedly devote themselves to worldly pleasure, surrender their hearts to greed, employ any degraded method for private life, and discard the principle that the king exists for the people, not the people for the king.

But our learned men tell stories that our ancestors discovered transcendent silence, gained clarity through that path, ordered life accordingly, and possessed a subtle wisdom conferring happiness in this world and the next, which Western countries must kneel before us to learn. We ask them: why, even today in China, one of the Eastern lands, do those seated in power willingly commit immense crimes against the people in the name of democracy, crimes the West supposedly forgot?

China never possessed popular government or good administration in accordance with the developing political philosophy we know today. The country was divided into many small regions, each with its own commander and little army. These leaders considered fighting one another and plundering the people for military expenses an excellent form of government.

At times some warlords supported Japanese imperialism; on other occasions they favoured American imperialism or assisted British imperialism, always increasing their private gain. In most cases, victory or defeat in the civil wars they launched depended upon support from one of those three imperial powers. The history of the Manchu dynasty that ruled China was the same.

Sun Yat-sen, proudly described as the father of China, overthrew Manchu rule. A republic was established in name, but no revolutionary change occurred in either the conduct of rulers or the mental strength of the people. Sun Yat-sen’s knowledge and ability were exhausted in praising, preserving, and cultivating the old social order. That order had lost the quality of benefiting the people, enabled foreign economic exploitation, produced numerous self-appointed warlords, gave multitudes no opportunity except to enter unemployed armies and plunder others, and lacked the power to instil the unity and consciousness of rights needed for national liberation.

Thus, even after the Manchu dynasty was abolished, stable republican government did not arise, nor did the people gain benefit or progressive thought.

Neither Sun Yat-sen nor anyone else who founded the republic wished to investigate, understand, and destroy the fundamental causes that kept in place the obstacles to Chinese welfare, and then adopt good government capable of securing the people’s needs. Instead, to preserve and prolong the old condition through a powerful central government, Sun and his associates chiefly sought the cooperation of warlords who possessed not a trace of patriotism or concern for popular welfare.

In 1919 an agitation began against Japanese imperialism. Southern Chinese resolved not to buy Japanese cloth. Chinese mill owners and British and American imperial interests supported and cultivated the movement. It was conducted most strongly in Shanghai, the principal commercial city. Workers offered immense support, and students joined them in the struggle.

They decided to strike in factories under foreign control. The movement reached its peak in 1922 in Hong Kong, then a British colony. At the beginning of the year, thirty thousand dockworkers struck and demanded higher wages. Hong Kong harbour lay idle for two months. The government intervened, outlawed the trade union, arrested its leaders, and imposed martial law. In response, two hundred thousand workers employed in other sectors also struck.

The Hong Kong workers’ strike against British imperialism inspired labourers throughout China. Workers in Shanghai, Canton, Hankow, Tientsin, and other cities undertook sympathy strikes. Sympathy processions and protest meetings were held. Students participated. Chinese capitalists contributed money, as did small traders. Just as the Japanese had earlier suffered loss, British imperialism was eventually damaged by this popular movement.

The dockworkers prevailed. They received a twenty-percent wage increase and pay for the strike period. The Chinese government at Canton had supported them; the union’s central organisation was therefore moved there. The Kuomintang government in Canton was ultimately compelled to join the anti-imperialist struggle. British imperialism consequently treated it as an enemy, conspired to overthrow it, and succeeded. Sun Yat-sen, a principal figure in the Canton government, was driven out. Later, with Soviet assistance, he succeeded in re-establishing a national government at Canton.

The boycott of Japanese cloth was like a double-edged knife. It won support from domestic mill owners, but also attracted the opposition of wealthy merchants who sold Japanese goods. No capitalist ordinarily desires disruption of trade. The Hong Kong dockworkers’ victory and the solidarity shown by workers in other cities made it clear to the warlords, American and British imperialism, and Chinese capitalists that, beyond the warlords long active in politics, another important new movement was taking shape.

Under these conditions, an organisation was established to unite workers scattered across the country and construct a labour movement on an organisational basis. In 1922 two hundred workers met at Canton and resolved to strive for China’s liberation from imperialist exploitation.

This resolution angered the imperial powers exploiting China. They concluded that only by crushing the labour movement could they protect themselves.

The labour movement would not stop after striking only in foreign-owned factories and remedying the grievances of workers there. It would also attempt to remove the grievances of workers employed under domestic capitalists. Though this was not openly visible at first, Chinese capitalists learned from experience the great truth that the labour movement would inevitably assume this form over time. They therefore reconciled their differences with the imperial powers and united to crush the workers.

Repression was rehearsed upon coal miners at Shili. In 1922 armed police shot and destroyed workers. The drama of repression was then staged along the British-financed Peking-Hankow railway. An attempt was made to unite workers scattered throughout that industry. They had spent two months arranging a conference. At the last moment, after many delegates had assembled at the appointed place, the government prohibited it.

They defied the ban and met. Their first resolution condemned military rule. The government surrounded the conference building, arrested the leaders, and punished them.

A strike began to condemn the government’s objectionable conduct. When it denied the right of assembly, ordinary people rose in anger and condemned it. They demanded inquiry and punishment for the officials who dispersed the conference and arrested labour leaders.

The crisis deepened. Capitalists united without regard to race and urged the government to suppress the agitation. Workers along the seven-hundred-mile railway were shot down. In savage fashion, labour leaders were hanged in broad daylight in the middle of streets. At the same time, the government energetically crushed workers in every heavily industrialised city and succeeded. The workers were defeated, but neither their enthusiasm nor discipline was broken.

The labour movement selected Shanghai as the field for its liberation struggle. Shanghai’s decision would echo the next day across northern China, and workers there prepared for another battle. At the same time, the labour movement in Canton was again taking shape.

In May 1925 a strike occurred at a Japanese textile mill in the city printed in the source as “Tishingto.” The Chinese governor ruling that region was a creation of Japan and immediately sent the army to break the strike. Workers in Japanese mills in Shanghai held a sympathy strike. Japanese police and the Chinese army acted there too to crush them. The strike leader was arrested and killed.

The strike spread to other factories. British police arrested some movement leaders. A popular procession reached the police station intending to release them by force. Police fired upon the crowd. Many died and many were seriously wounded. This occurred on 30 May 1925.

The people’s resolve did not weaken. They rose with still greater anger, and many sections of society joined the agitation. A demand arose to abolish every privilege enjoyed by foreign capitalists in China. British police became frightened. They did not trust Chinese troops and sent armed soldiers from their warship to suppress the movement. Meetings were dispersed, processions prohibited, and firing upon crowds became ordinary.

Even this repression could not suppress the popular uprising. Imperialism adopted another method: creating division among those in the movement. Despite the agitation, work continued properly in Chinese textile mills. The British owners of the electricity-generating station announced they would no longer supply them power. At once the patriotism of Chinese capitalists flew away. They betrayed the movement. Others too withdrew, leaving the workers alone. Even then, the workers continued the struggle without losing enthusiasm or unity.

When news of the Shanghai repression reached Canton, the people there launched a liberation agitation. They gathered against the British and French. Police fired upon the crowd. In retaliation, Chinese workers employed in white households and workers in Hong Kong factories went on strike.

Many methods were used to break it. Leaders were arrested, unions dissolved, and meetings prevented. Consequently all Chinese workers left Hong Kong. The city was cut off. This non-cooperation lasted fifteen months. British trade was reduced by half. The pride and power of imperialism were made ridiculous by popular strength. This became a major event in China’s liberation struggle and its battle against imperialism.

Chiang Kai-shek was then head of the newly established Whampoa Military Academy. A rumour arose that some people were conspiring to seize the Canton government. Using this as a pretext and aided by academy troops, Chiang seized Canton. He also obtained the cooperation of the workers’ guard.

The first measure he took to strengthen his authority was to dissolve the workers’ guard that had won him victory. On 20 March 1926, within half a day of gaining power, he employed every form of repression and suppressed opposition. He searched labour organisations. Nevertheless, he still possessed support in the country.

Had Chiang devoted even a small portion of the measures he took to enlarge his territory and increase his authority to enriching popular life, the crisis confronting his rule today might have arrived only some years later. Chinese people had lost their lives and lived as walking corpses under imperial control, exploitation by Chinese capitalists, and plunder by military landlords. Chiang attempted to preserve that very condition and destroy resistance through cruel measures.

As the foremost servant of military magnates’ tyranny and other exploitation, Chiang ruled dictatorially with American assistance. That rule is now shaken. China, which would have turned red after some more time, is turning red rapidly minute by minute while Chiang still sits upon the throne of authority, driven there sooner by his lust for power and disorderly tyranny.

He watches it happen. The triumphant joy he felt upon gaining power, the delight he felt while eliminating comrades, and the happiness that arose when workers’ blood stained his hands cannot be present today as the country turns red. What can he do? China is turning red!

The popular power that lay hidden but smouldering when Chiang gained office in 1926 now again hastens along the road to victory. Chiang passed his days believing he had suppressed the earlier popular uprising with attractive ideas and verbal ornament that bewitched thought. Everyone seated in power would do well to take the approaching end of Chiang as a warning.

(Published in the 14 November 1948 and 21 November 1948 issues of *Dravida Nadu*.)

## English Translation

## At the Beach

"Brother, where did you get your boots polished? They look splendid."

"Really? There is a boy outside the Elite Hotel, sir. His name is Ezhumalai. He is very skilled. He polished them. A nail had even come loose in the boot, and he repaired that too. Only half an anna."

"The boots look very stylish. A polish certainly makes them beautiful."

## In the Slum

"Hey, Ezhumalai! Why have you smeared cart grease on your hand? Are you going to dress up as Gangamma?"

"No Gangamma costume and no Mangamma performance. I polished a gentleman's boots outside the Elite Hotel. A nail had come loose underneath. Without noticing it, I pressed and wiped that part with my hand. The nail tore my hand badly. That is why I applied cart grease."

"Get away, you fool. Should you not work carefully?"

The wound in a worker's palm, caused by labouring to make a pleasure-seeker's footwear shine, cannot be treated merely by applying cart grease or some other medicine. Only the wound in his hand will heal. There is another wound in his mind. As the days pass and his labour increases, that wound will continue to grow. It too needs medicine.

Every day, Ezhumalai calls, "Sir! Polish! I will polish them, sir!" Is it really he who calls out? Hunger is calling.

At the Elite Hotel, someone asks a man, "Why are you saying two idlis are enough today, sir?" He replies, "I don't know. I am not hungry today," and walks outside. Yet the boy who polishes his boots does not receive enough food to satisfy his hunger. And the boy works directly in front of a famous hotel in a civilised city.

The boy's eyes dart towards everyone wearing footwear. "Sir! Polish! Shall I polish them?" he asks. Does that mean only, "I shall make your boots shine"? No. Does it not primarily mean, "Sir, I am hungry. I will work; give me food"?

Is even that the whole meaning? No. He is asking: "Sir, I labour. I make your life shine. I give it the polish called civilisation. You enjoy it and delight in it. I provide the lustre. I do not ask for that lustre or shine myself; give me food so that I may live."

Does this happen only outside the Elite Hotel? Is it only the boy polishing boots? The man who builds the mansion, the one who provides clothing, the one who constructs the beautiful city, every worker does the same. Through his labour, he polishes our lives, giving them shine, brilliance, and glitter.

What does that noble person ask after giving so much labour? A means to live. What does he receive? Not merely the wound in Ezhumalai's palm. A wound arises in the worker's heart. The reward he has received through labouring and labouring, generation after generation, is a wound in the mind.

The boy Ezhumalai applied cart grease to heal the wound in his hand. To heal the wounds in their hearts, workers apply the ointments prepared by religious teachers: an illusory universe, rebirth, and deeds from a previous birth.

Ezhumalai's friend advises him, "You fool! If you had worked carefully, your hand would not have been wounded." Religious teachers who claim a relationship with the Lord of the world preach to workers: "Be patient. Endure. Continue worshipping. The Supreme Being is testing you. You must experience the consequences brought by sin. Pray to the Lord, and he will remedy your suffering."

Before his wound has healed, Ezhumalai returns to work. He stands calling, "Sir, polish! I will polish them!" He follows people wearing boots and asks, "Sir! Polish!"

Before the wound in his heart has healed, the worker too continues the work of "polishing" the world and the pleasure-seekers who live within it.

22-11-1946

## English Translation

“Varadachari and Company!”

“The bandicoots of Mount Road!”

“Takers of bribe money!”

“Destitute carriers!”

“A reactionary gang!”

Who are the prominent men adorned with this garland? Who became targets of such severe condemnation? Who uttered these words?

Many may ask rather angrily: whatever the party rivalry, may people vilify others in this fashion—condemning them as bandicoots, receivers of bribes and reactionaries?

The hasty among them may say: “We know! It must be those Dravidar Kazhagam people who abuse others with such insolence. That is their nature.”

But the “ritual praise” quoted above did not come from any member of our Kazhagam. These are the censures directed by actual Congressmen against other actual Congressmen.

A Madras Congress periodical described several Congress members of the Madras Legislative Assembly in this fashion—and supplied a reason.

The words appear in *Gandibam*, a weekly serving as the court messenger of the daily *Dinasari*.

Against whom is so much anger directed? These censures are hurled at T. Varadachari, Madurai Vaidyanatha Iyer and some of their associates, who won elections in Congress's name and sit as members of the Madras legislature.

We could not condemn those prominent men in this measure or manner even for reasons stronger than the one offered here. If we did, *Gandibam* itself would bathe in the Cooum, gather refuse in its hands, run towards us and crow: “Ah! Do you condemn Congress leaders without restraint? See whether I let you go!”

Even now one often hears the sweet “music of nectar” produced when some honourable Congress supporters who write newspapers condemn other honourable Congressmen.

A scheme to abolish zamindari and inam tenure was introduced by the Madras minister, the Honourable Kala Venkata Rao, and has suffered every imaginable ordeal for several months.

Seeking to destroy it in the womb, opponents first hurled great law books at it.

*The Hindu* strung together every thunderous word available in an English dictionary and wrote many editorials seeking to eliminate the scheme.

Conferences were held in pleasure pavilions and open fields, where speakers beat their chests and claimed the scheme would harm the people's welfare.

Conferences of conservative moneybags threatened: “Total destruction will occur, you wretches!”

Progressive thinkers from several parties urged people not to be disturbed by this wild clamour and strengthened Kala's hand and resolve.

In the legislature, Varadachari and Vaidyanatha Iyer girded themselves for battle. “Do not touch the inams! They are gifts made in religion's name. To destroy them is a great sin and offence,” they said.

An overwhelming majority in the legislature supported the zamindari and inam abolition scheme.

Varadachari and his associates immediately tightened their belts at the Congress party meeting and tried to destroy it there. Their effort failed there too.

Did they give up? Would they? Certainly not. They were, after all, descendants of people who in ancient days entered the forest and performed severe penance to obtain boons. Poor men, they performed penance facing Delhi and carried the *kavadi*.

They obtained enough success to sing, “Our penance has borne fruit.” The Congress high command began showing support for people such as Varadachari. It listened to the poisonous propaganda demanding that inams be exempted from Kala's law, displayed sympathy, and sent the Madras ministry some new suggestions.

The rebels rejoiced and Kala was distressed. Kala was a minister; a majority of legislators accepted his scheme; the Madras Provincial Congress Committee supported it; and the country, though considering the scheme less radical than expected, gave some support because at least this much was being done.

Despite all this, a small group without influence in the legislature, Congress party or country could destroy Kala's scheme.

The zamindars seated in palaces at Pithapuram, Bobbili, Vizianagaram, Ettayapuram and elsewhere could not shake it. But this little group arose in support of inamdars and obtained victory.

The lion is disgraced; the little fox triumphs.

How can a minister possessing the least concern for self-respect consent to such degradation? How can it be tolerated?

Had strong opposition arisen in the legislature, party or country, it could be regarded as a heroic contest testing the minister's ability and manliness. This is no heroic contest. It is conspiracy inside the fort, the disgrace of betrayal among companions who associate closely. How can it be borne?

The minister's heart must ache: “What use is it that I am a minister supported by the majority? My courage is being disgraced, earth is thrown upon my intention and soot smeared upon my face.”

The rebels, meanwhile, rejoice: “What if he is a minister or member of the ruling group? What if his hand is strong in the party and the country gives him immense support? What can he do against us? We are breaking his scheme. Let the minister learn the extent of our domination.”

The people and we had supported the minister's scheme. He enjoyed enormous support in the party meeting and legislature. Everyone believed that even if a few madmen opposed it, they could do nothing and the scheme would be successfully enacted.

Then came a directive from the high command—one that would rescue the inamdars and increase the compensation paid to them.

With what arrogant and contemptuous eyes must that small group have looked upon the minister that day? What sorrow and shame must he have felt?

The minister appealed once again to the party meeting. He explained that paying compensation in the manner and measure demanded by the Congress high command would load an unbearable burden upon the province.

Experts said—and the minister announced—that under the high command's suggestion, the province would bear a burden of nearly thirty crores of rupees. Instead of producing benefit, the zamindari and inam abolition scheme would cause a great loss and pointless trouble.

The Congress party too appealed to the high command: “Do not place this immense burden upon our province.”

It was then that *Gandibam* fitted its arrows of words against Varadachari and Company.

Why does this small group obtain the high command's favour and smear soot upon the minister's face? *Gandibam* gives a reason capable of making anyone who values dignity bow their head. Listen:

“Silver coins circulate like water.”

After taking silver coins from people affected by inam abolition—in other words, receiving bribes—this group travelled as messengers to the Congress high command, burned incense before it, and damaged Kala's scheme. It is not we but a Congress-supporting newspaper that writes this openly.

The country knows which Congress members entered the effort to deform the inam abolition scheme from the beginning. *Gandibam* informs the nation of their worth. It says we must not imagine they behave this way because of policy or mistaken opinion: it is all the work of the Lord of Money.

These honourable men can hear such a public accusation without the slightest tremor or agitation.

Silver coins flow like water—where? In the holy land. When? Under the rule of saints.

For silver coins, some dare to ruin their own party's scheme and betray the poor. These very men are the people's representatives and legislators. What a fine character!

The public knows one fact well: most inamdars are Brahmins. It also knows that the Brahmin leaders who allegedly accepted silver coins, carried accusations to the high command and sought concessions for inamdars were men such as Vaidyanatha Iyer and Varadachari.

People know that this joint conspiracy succeeded enough to disturb the province's minister. What will those who understand it say about the country's condition? How deeply will their hearts seethe?

We ask those friends who win the title of intellectual and the distinction of conciliator by condemning our Kazhagam members as destroyers who criticise Brahmins unnecessarily: what do you think of this joint conspiracy occurring in our country in our time? How do you tolerate it?

Whenever we said Brahmins could protect their interests and preserve their domination under any condition or government, you mocked us as deranged people beating a dead snake. What do you say now?

What became of the minister? What use was the legislative majority? What purpose did his immense support within Congress serve? By burning incense before the high command, they can deform his scheme. Shame and grief tear the heart. What do you say of this condition?

And all of it, according to *Gandibam*, for silver coins.

Such people still sit grandly in the legislature as jewels of the glorious Congress assembly.

We reproduce everything *Gandibam* wrote. Read it thoughtfully:

> The Zamindari and Inamdari Abolition Bill has again stirred an uproar. Varadachari and Company cries that the Madras government refuses to heed the Central Parliamentary Board's suggestion and that Revenue Minister Kala Venkata Rao acts as he pleases.
>
> Many newspapers have written editorials following this. No one need suspect that they wrote in favour of abolishing inamdari. They wrote only to ask whether the Madras government could behave this way. Expecting them to support inamdari abolition is itself a mistake. Expecting the bandicoots of Mount Road to support abolition is like expecting a thief to condemn thieving.
>
> We understand that the Madras government's bill caused trouble for the Central Parliamentary Board. Had the Board wished, it could have prevented the trouble. It need only have denied interviews and opportunities to present arguments to certain destitute carriers who accepted bribe money from those affected by inamdari abolition and ran to Delhi.
>
> The Central Parliamentary Board failed to do so. That failure caused the entire uproar.
>
> Although the Congress party in the Madras legislature had already approved the bill, some members act directly against it. There is no count of the tricks they perform to belittle it. Besides speaking at Legislature Party meetings, they stir conflict outside. Their sole object is to kill the bill.
>
> It is certain that this aim will not succeed; even that reactionary group knows it. One may therefore ask why they utter such barren cries. The reason is that silver coins circulate like water. If the flow of silver stops for a while, the group will curl up its tail and sit down. Until then these reactionaries will keep wailing. All one can tell them is: “Wail freely.”

Angered by the condition, *Gandibam* abuses Varadachari and Company as bandicoots, bribe-takers and destitute carriers. What will abuse do to them? How can it correct the condition?

A directive from the Congress high command arrived two or three days ago. After 10 August the Madras Congress party will meet and reconsider it.

Is this condition right or proper? Why does an institution called the high command exist, and under what political justice must the rulers here submit to it? Is it right to compel representatives elected by the province's people to act against the verdict of that same majority? Are these not the questions *Gandibam* should have considered?

So long as an institution called the high command exists and possesses final authority, some “company” will repeatedly arise—whether after receiving silver coins or gold bangles—to carry tales and conspire.

Provincial autonomy and popular government are reduced to farce, with no one to question it.

Through the high command's favour, worthless men can disturb ministers' schemes. Can this be called democracy?

Why is this condition permitted?

Had the high command desired political honesty and respected popular government, should it have listened to tales carried by a small group? Its leaders should have said: “Go! Obtain the support of your province's people. Appealing to me is useless. I cannot intervene in provincial autonomy.” Did they say so?

*Gandibam* itself says the trouble arose because interviews and opportunities to present arguments were given to “destitute carriers who accepted bribes and ran to Delhi.” Why does the high command place such value upon their speech—more value than upon the minister's?

Very well: the “destitute carriers” run to Delhi for bribe money. Why does the high command value the words of such people? What is the reason?

Did the silver coins remain with the carriers, or did they act as postmen and deliver them where they had to go? What is the mystery? *Gandibam* has a duty to explain, if it can.

Leave that aside. The high command somehow became absorbed in the words of allegedly bribed carriers, refused to respect the people's verdict and dared to disturb the minister's scheme. *Gandibam* knows its conduct is wrong. After knowing it, has the paper written even one word condemning the high command? Would it dare?

How freely and courageously it hurled the insult “bandicoots of Mount Road.” Will even one-thousandth of that courage arise to condemn the high command?

We ask whether the ministers, powerful Congress leaders and legislators—not poor *Gandibam*, a paper that merely carries somewhat spicy writing—can pass a resolution saying:

“The Madras provincial government was constituted by the people of this province. This zamindari and inam abolition scheme is its scheme. Please do not intervene. Do not take away our rights.”

They know that passing such a resolution would endanger the ministry itself.

Varadachari and Company already threatens the minister and his supporters: “Will you accept the Congress high command's order or not? Do you know what will happen if you disobey it?”

Do the minister and others respond: “High command or any other place, we shall follow our conscience. We shall no longer permit anyone to interfere in our rights”?

No. They say only: “We shall appeal to the high command and offer an explanation; in the end we shall act as it directs.”

They refuse to understand that this trembling is more disgraceful than the bribe money received by those destitute carriers.

An institution called the high command will exist. One group will go there carrying accusations; another will offer explanations. Between them will stand provincial autonomy. What a degraded condition, what a farce!

Is not our effort to free them from this degrading condition precisely why we say “Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians”?

If a directive from the high command is final law for the people of this province, then the provincial government is merely a large district board in status, quality and capacity. Is this provincial autonomy?

What use is served by *Gandibam* fitting arrows against Varadachari and Company? Courage is displayed without knowing the target. People who do not understand the condition speak of justice. Those who dare surrender rights parade before local people dressed as heroes. It is useless work, done for empty pride.

If there is even a mustard seed of concern and righteous feeling for protecting the people's rights, the welfare of the poor, the honour of the legislature, the minister's standing and the party's dignity, then let *Gandibam* be the Pasupata weapon and the political Arjunas who wield it must turn towards Delhi and dare to say:

“Stop a moment! We shall not accept this autocracy!”

Can they? We doubt it.

*Dravida Nadu*, 8 August 1948

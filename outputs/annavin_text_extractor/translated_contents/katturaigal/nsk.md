## English Translation

“Everyone, clap your hands once.
Won't you clap? Never mind!”

This is not merely a humorous line, nor merely dialogue written for a film. It is a snapshot of the heart of Comrade N. S. Krishnan, the king of comedy; it is the guiding principle he has held throughout his life. He once lived surrounded by friends, with laughter ringing around him and every day celebrated like a festival. Today? It pains us to describe his condition. Yet he himself would remain in the same “Never mind!” manner, because that is not merely a line: it is his heart.

Many people have used the field of comedy in many ways and for many purposes, obtaining many kinds of benefit from it. Until now, however, no one had made comedy an instrument for instilling good ideas among the people and thereby gained, not only the central place in the world of actors, but also an important place in the front rank of the nation's sculptors of thought. No one has made us laugh as he has; and no one else has ever made us weep as he has. The tears Tamil Nadu shed that day have become a spring. Tamil Nadu has honoured actors and, to use the familiar expression, showered them with gold; but it had never shed tears when an actor met with adversity. Only during the Krishnan-Bhagavathar affair did a vast multitude transcend the restraints of party and other divisions and weep.

N. S. K. was not merely an actor; he became a symbol. Just as Bharathidasan became a symbol showing that revolution had entered the world of poetry, N. S. K. became a symbol demonstrating that comedy had passed beyond the condition of being an idle occupation that merely made the people laugh and had reached a stage where good ideas could be conveyed with great ease. He gave the people the medicine of reform mixed with honey. Even in giving it, he used the mature tenderness with which a mother administers medicine to her children.

Many actors have made people laugh until they shook by performing crooked antics: whitening the area above their eyes with lime, painting their lips red, walking bent over, stammering, stumbling and falling, or walking upside down. They could make stomachs burst with laughter, but they could not make a new idea take root.

“I cannot bear this stomach ache, man!”

“Why?”

“That comedy Buffoon Pakiri did in last night's play was tremendous!”

“What did the buffoon do?”

“Good heavens, you should have seen all his antics! The actor playing Hanuman came onstage. The buffoon followed him, stuffed Hanuman's tail into his own nose and began sneezing. He sneezed and sneezed for ten minutes; the whole theatre shook! The audience laughed until their stomachs hurt. He was terribly funny. He barked like a dog and sounded exactly like a real dog.”

Before N. S. K. arrived, this was all the people could say about the comedy they had seen. With his arrival, they came to understand the extraordinary power contained in that field. They did not merely laugh and forget. They laughed and carried some new ideas away with them:

> “You fire a salute, saying,
> ‘In childhood, in chaste Tamil,
> he sang a song.’
> Can one write poetry
> before becoming learned through education?
> Cast aside these fabricated tales;
> the fraud will be exposed!”

Krishnan sang this in *Poompavai*. We heard it and laughed. We did not stop with laughter; we thought. “Is it not true? Can one write poetry before becoming learned through education?” we asked ourselves. At once our reason answered, “No!” The next moment, without even realising it, we exclaimed, “What a sound question he asked!” and laughed once again.

Many foods are tasty while we eat them. Only a few continue to yield their flavour long afterwards whenever saliva passes through the mouth. Krishnan gave the people comedy of that kind.

He explained the condition of the people, the mentality of the upper castes, the misery of the worker and the attitudes produced by caste distinctions. Through comedy he offered numerous ideas needed for building a new world, including the truth that many notions regarded by the people as sacred principles were mere deception or devoid of meaning. He performed this good service to the country through comic acting, a field previously used only for clownish antics. Before him, actors had not turned comedy into such a platform for reform.

Even among celebrated Western comic actors such as Harold Lloyd, Buster Keaton, Laurel and Hardy and Robert Woolsey, most merely presented amusements for entertainment; they did not become teachers. To some extent Charlie Chaplin used comedy to convey ideas in a few films, especially one such as *City Lights*. The others performed comedy such as mistaking a man for a woman and kissing him, slipping and falling, or cleverly deceiving an opponent. They did not, like N. S. K., coat strong medicine with sweetness and offer it in such a way that we willingly bought it and swallowed ten doses instead of one:

> “Man and woman are one;
> the brain that knows it not is clay.”

It is therefore clear that there was no other comic actor, not merely in Tamil Nadu but in all India, who made comedy such a pleasant instrument for introducing reform. Though the number cannot be stated after a census, we may confidently say that more people accepted new reformist ideas after seeing his comedy than were reformed by the propaganda of a platform speaker.

Because he was the first actor to introduce reformist ideas through amusing speech instead of presenting them in the form of denunciation, N. S. K. certainly belongs in the company of the poets, orators and writers who laboured to cultivate the country. Today the country may refuse to accept that his work produced benefits as great as theirs. But when the day of true assessment arrives, it will assign the proper value to this service.

Even today, if we happen to go near a tank and see an orthodox Brahmin performing his twilight prayers while looking all around him, how could *Uthama Puthiran* fail to come to mind?

Do we not hear Krishnan's voice in our ears saying, “Very fine! Beautiful! So proper, just like a Brahmin woman”? Do we not see Madhuram standing exactly like the lady of an Iyengar household, staring blankly and rebuking her husband: “Are you doing Harijan service, Harijan service?”

Was that mere comedy? Was it not a picture of the country's condition? Did his acting serve only to make us laugh and forget? It has taken its place in our hearts. Whenever the problem of caste is discussed, how can we forget *Salivahanan*?

> “The salt tastes salty,
> the chilli tastes hot;
> when water is drunk, the chest is not blocked.
> If you have doubts, come and eat:
> the meal is ready!”

How skilfully he introduces the lesson through that song in the film! How can it be regarded as mere comedy?

His gift for portraying the labourer's suffering can be seen in *Sakuntalai*:

> “Sir! In the river we cast our fishing line;
> if two fish are caught in it,
> that provides gruel for half a stomach, O God!
> The family is starving, O God!”

You have heard N. S. K. sing this song. How movingly he portrays in it the pitiable life of the Parathavar fishing community! Their wretched condition comes steeped in song and takes such a place in our minds that it cannot be removed.

In every film he worked to introduce some new and progressive idea. Even when the original story was mythological, he skilfully found room for his “service” without diminishing the flavour in the least.

His successful use of comedy to instil good ideas among the people can be seen wonderfully in his *Nandanar*: it contains not mere mockery but a lesson. In the guise of Krishna Bhagavathar, he said in *Nandanar* that if the oppressed communities advanced through education, they could free themselves from the grip of the curse of caste. The country did not hear only the story; it absorbed the idea as well.

Had imprisonment not intervened, the country might by now have listened with delight to the revolutionary ideas for creating a new world in his *Kalakandar* and benefited from them:

> “See, the guise of the mendicant foreigner has flown away:
> Dandayudhapani, Parankiri, Senthur, Palani, Tirupati;
> see, the guise of the mendicant foreigners has flown away.”

We could all have heard the song, enjoyed its flavour and, along with that enjoyment, absorbed its revolutionary message.

Thus he introduced into the long-established field of comedy a method no one before him had used and achieved success. Through that very success he performed the good service of instilling sound ideas in the country and earned a place among the great people who spread revolution. More than that, by creating a situation in which the acting world could not avoid following his method, he prompted many others to undertake that good work. He created a new climate.

If anything can be compared with the rare service he rendered through comedy, it is the climate created before the revolutions in France and Russia by poets, writers, speakers and actors.

The poet of the Kongu country, Udumalai Narayana Kavi, stood beside him and enabled him to perform this rare service so ably. Not only he and Madam Madhuram, who performed an equal share of this work, but we too, together with the lakhs of people moved by the desire that good ideas should spread throughout the country, now wait in longing and ask: when shall we receive the king of comedy among us again, so that he may resume his service to the country through comedy?

17 November 1946

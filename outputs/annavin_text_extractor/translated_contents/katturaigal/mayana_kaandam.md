## English Translation

"You are the man who cremated a body in the burial ground, are you not?"

"Yes."

"Whose body was it?"

"My son's."

"Very well. What is your caste?"

"Adi Dravidian."

"Adi Dravidians must cremate their dead only in the place allotted to their caste. But you cremated your son's body in the place reserved for members of other castes. Why should you not be punished for this offence?"

"What offence?"

"It is an offence to take the body of an Adi Dravidian and cremate it in the place designated for the bodies of caste Hindus. Why did you do so?"

"I did it unknowingly."

"Is it enough to say that you did not know? By transgressing the prescribed boundary, you committed an act that wounded the feelings of people attached to caste and religious custom. You must therefore be punished."

"It rained that day. In my haste, I did it without knowing."

"That is not an adequate explanation. Very well: we sentence you to four months' rigorous imprisonment."

This is no cremation-ground episode from the age of Harishchandra. It happened in 1946. The eldest child of an Adi Dravidian man in Madurai died, and he went to the burial ground to perform the last rites. How could a man grieving over his dead child have kept in mind that, "In this sacred land, our elders devised these scripturally ordained arrangements in ancient times, and the white man's government respects and follows that Vedic arrangement"? Poor man, what thoughts must have filled his mind as he wept? The rain added to his trouble. The body was cremated at some available place.

That place, it appears, had been arranged for the exclusive use of caste Hindus. When he violated the arrangement, the earth did not split open, no deity grew angry, and the people did not come to quarrel.

The law became angry.

For the offence of acting against the people's religious customs, that Adi Dravidian comrade was sentenced to four months' rigorous imprisonment. The conversation reproduced above is between the law and the man struck by this distress.

The case went before the appellate court, and the lower court's judgment was upheld. Legal arguments too were reportedly advanced there. It was pointed out that in 1938 the Madras government had enacted a law permitting members of every caste to enter public places other than religious institutions. Yet the law stood by convention and scripture, and the man received a sentence of four months' rigorous imprisonment.

It was only a burial ground. He might have thought: when we have obtained permission to enter even the temple of Meenakshi with Sundareshwarar, what does it matter where in a burial ground we cremate a body? Four months for him! He is a direct descendant of Nandanar. The puranic expositors say that Nandanar imparted knowledge of Siva even to a Vedic scholar. Of what use is that?

The law says that the feelings of the religious will be wounded!

Who is president of the Provincial Legislative Assembly? Comrade Siva Shanmugam, an Adi Dravidian. In Madras he is president of the Assembly; in Madurai, a man of his caste receives four months' imprisonment for cremating an Adi Dravidian body in the place where the body of a high-caste Hindu was supposed to be cremated.

Minister Kurmaiah is an Adi Dravidian. Comrade Parameswaran, the young Adi Dravidian serving as parliamentary secretary to Comrade Bhashyam, the Vaishnavite Brahmin who is Law Minister, may hold that office. What of it? For acting contrary to caste custom, the law has sentenced an Adi Dravidian comrade in Madurai to four months' rigorous imprisonment.

There must be no separate electorate in politics, but there must be a separate allotment in the burial ground. What kind of justice is this?

An Adi Dravidian may become Speaker of the Legislative Assembly or a minister, yet discrimination against an Adi Dravidian persists even in the place where a body is burned. What manner of justice is this?

As though the discrimination practised throughout the country were not enough, caste discrimination continues in the burial ground too. The Madras burial ground contains arrangements and signboards directing that the bodies of particular castes may be buried or cremated only in particular places.

Every party accepts the principle that the cruelty of caste discrimination must be eradicated. Besides giving the oppressed people a new name, the Congress party lectures them that they must not demand separate electorates in politics. Congressmen govern, and the president of the Legislative Assembly is himself a comrade from the oppressed community.

Even in plays, friends sing: after death, where is the key to the strongbox, where are the heavy ornaments, where are the vessels for earning interest? In the same way, though everything else departs, caste alone follows one even to the burial ground, stands there jeering, "Whatever else may have gone, I remain!" and compels obedience.

The Madras Congress ministry was asked to abolish this discrimination. We understand that the ministry ordered the prisoners' release by remitting the portion of their sentences remaining after the time already served. But the order does not clearly explain whether it applies only to this one incident, or whether all oppressed people will henceforth be allowed to cremate their dead wherever they wish within a burial ground.

*Dravida Nadu*, 16 June 1946.

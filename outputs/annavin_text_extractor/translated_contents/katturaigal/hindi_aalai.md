## English Translation

Sir,

I am a Telugu. I am attached to the principles of the DMK. My elder brother lives in a town called Kallur. I wrote him a letter. Deliberately, I wrote the address in Hindi, which the northerners intend to make the official language. I have sent you the card so that you may see what an ordeal it underwent.

Yours,

Erraiah

We have received such a letter. The comrade says he did it deliberately. The result was that the letter suffered untold trouble for many days, reached the General Post Office in Madras, had its address translated into English, and only then proceeded to its destination. The reason was simply that postal employees were not sufficiently familiar with Hindi. This is the present condition.

Hindi enthusiasts may point to this and argue, "That is precisely why everyone must learn Hindi." Learning it and living with it come later. This is the condition now!

In Delhi's Parliament, which represents all thirty-five crore people of the Indian subcontinent, Speaker Mavalankar is said to have raged against southern representatives a few days ago. A minister reportedly answered in Hindi. A southern member, unable to understand it, asked, "I do not understand. Could you please translate it into English?"

That turned Mavalankar into a tiger. The man who asked was a representative of the people. He had reached Parliament by winning the votes of lakhs of taxpayers. Mavalankar, who had obtained the opportunity to preside over them, delivered this "judgment":

> No. If you can understand what is said in Hindi, understand it. Otherwise, leave. It cannot be translated like this. Hereafter, there is no need to do so either.

It seems translation will no longer be possible. They must simply remain bewildered! Perhaps because he displayed such arrogance, several members of Parliament introduced a motion declaring that they had no confidence in Mavalankar. The motion is unlikely to pass. Nevertheless, the very appearance of such a motion disgraces Mavalankar's position. The world will ridicule it.

Let that matter go as it may. Look at the linguistic arrogance of the man fortunate enough to conduct Parliament. He says a translation cannot be provided. And that place is Parliament.

There, such conduct.

Here, this letter.

"It is ignorance to imagine that a language can be enriched by issuing orders."

Ignorance! The journal *Mithran* writes this! A very severe word has come from that quarter. We are delighted. We were mocked by these very people when we spoke of Hindi's intrusion. Now that camp speaks. Listen to what it says:

> Before one runs, one must know how to walk. It is evident that Hindi has not yet properly learned even to walk.

> In truth, they grow furious in their desire to impose that language by force.

> If Hindi has not spread rapidly, it is because the language has not yet acquired sufficient richness.

> The insistence of Hindi fanatics that the language be used immediately for administration, statutory language, and higher education is utterly meaningless talk.

> In acquiring the necessary capacity, the language has not crossed even the first stage.

> It is disastrous that those concerned with Hindi's growth are thinking of still more ways to punish people who do not know Hindi.

It does not even know how to walk. An attempt at imposition. They grow furious. It lacks richness. Language fanatics. Meaningless talk. The impulse to punish. Disaster.

The *Swadesamitran* of the fifteenth heaps up all these words in its denunciation. We received baton blows, tear gas, bullets, and exile to the forest even for pregnant women merely because we explained Hindi's unfitness in language far more refined than this. Now *Mithran* says it!

However much wholesome counsel is offered, the northerners' arrogant domination does not appear to have diminished even slightly. Armed with the single qualification that Hindi is spoken by a large number of people in the Indian subcontinent, they continue behaving with the fanatic belief that speakers of other languages are their slaves. No one can deny this. Even nationalist comrades have repeatedly received blows from them!

Yet there are still those who sigh, "Someday..." and dream that "Lady Hindi" will sit enthroned. They are the people who cover their ears when they hear our slogan. The *Mithran* circle belongs to that very kind.

They must think about the future. How can a single language operate throughout such a vast territory inhabited by many crores of people? Is it possible for Hindi to reign from the Himalayas to Kanyakumari? Can a time ever come when everyone, from the little child in our home to the child in a northerner's home, speaks the same language?

The Englishman ruled this country for two hundred years, and his language functioned as the government's language. Even so, vast numbers of people still stare blankly when they hear English. Vast numbers!

In this condition, even if Hindi becomes the official language not today but fifteen years from now, who will benefit? Just as English collectors once came here, the man who knows Hindi will enter. A Hindi speaker will come even for the post now occupied by one of our own people earning his livelihood in the postal department. What else can we expect?

Everyone must think about this.

If one asks whether there is an alternative proposal, there is none.

The slogan "Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians" alone appears capable of ultimately securing victory.

Comrades may grow angry when we say it now. Truths are not always sweet at first. So it is with our ideal.

*Dravida Nadu*, 19 December 1954.

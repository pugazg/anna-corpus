## English Translation

## One Episode in a Continuing Story

Those who stand in worship will weep and then grow quiet!

It is the Dravidian who will write the final chapter.

The “Kamaraj incident” has today created indignation throughout Tamil Nadu. Meetings of condemnation are being held in many places. Some newspapers publish reports of these protests. After praising Gandhi, they condemn Achariyar for having led him down a mistaken path. We cannot say that this will produce any remarkable result or that the indignation now present in Tamil Nadu will endure. The blow has not landed where it should. The pain is severe; anger will remain as long as the pain does. Afterwards the matter will subside with the thought, “Let things proceed as they were allowed to proceed.”

Those who closely examine the activities of the Tamil Nadu Congress know that the “Kamaraj incident” was not the first event of its kind in 1946. Beginning with Kanchipuram, the Aryans of this country have always sought to retain either the principal role or the role of stage-manager in their own hands. They have crushed those who revolted, not by their own strength but with the support of northern leaders.

From then until today, the Tamil Nadu Congress leaders known to and supported by the northern leaders have all been Iyers and Iyengars. Others may possess indescribable influence among the people here, yet remain unknown within the All-India Congress organisation, or must seek the assistance of some Iyer merely to obtain an introduction. Added to this, because the central structure of the All-India Congress is in the north, the “court of appeal” for every dispute has been established there. If the judge of that appellate court favours him, must not the case end in the Aryan’s favour?

This has happened for many years, happens now, and will continue. On many occasions, quarrels have arisen in Tamil Nadu between Brahmin Congress leaders and non-Brahmin Congress leaders. The people condemned the Brahmin leaders and expressed confidence in the non-Brahmin leaders. Yet the northern leaders accepted the messages sent from here by the Brahmin Congress leaders, delivered ex parte judgments in their favour, appointed Brahmins themselves as Congress officials, and blackened the faces of the non-Brahmin Congress leaders. The expression Gandhi used today against Kamaraj had already been applied by northern leaders to non-Brahmin leaders of Tamil Nadu.

Kamaraj appeared at Kanchipuram in 1925, meaning that a revolt against Brahmin leadership began there. Today Kamaraj shed tears; at Kanchi, sparks of fire were scattered. Neither that fire has died nor those tears ceased.

“Why not announce here and now who the candidates in the election will be? Congress notables from across the province have come. If we state now who will stand for Congress, they can return to their districts and campaign conveniently,” said the leader from the Kongu country.

Mylapore Iyengar replied, “Naicker, there is no need now to decide whom to field. Let us appoint a committee, and later that committee may nominate the candidates.”

The man who stood like a minor king of Kongu Nadu said, “This will not do.” Words grew heated. “This is dishonourable conduct,” said the Kongu leader.

“Naicker, you must withdraw that word,” said Mylapore Iyengar.

“I cannot.”

“Withdraw it! Withdraw it!”

“Never.”

“You must withdraw it.”

“Find someone else for that.”

He put on his tasselled sandals and turned once to look at Mylapore Iyengar and his group.

“Do you ask me to withdraw my description of this as dishonourable? I cannot. I shall hold a meeting in this very town and expose this dishonour. I shall proclaim it throughout the country. I leave now,” he said and departed.

The journey that began that day continues even now. It began when he had a moustache and continues after his beard has grown. The moral struggle Periyar Ramasamy launched that day is still proceeding. At Kanchi he vowed to make the country understand how Brahmins used the Congress to ruin non-Brahmins, and he continues according to that vow.

Periyar left Kanchi after hurling fiery, severe words at the Thirty-First Tamil Provincial Congress Conference. During a committee meeting in the conference pavilion, the Kongu leader understood and condemned the desire of the Mylapore S. Srinivasa Iyengar group to use Congress to seat Brahmins in the “sanctum” of authority.

It was election time. The subtle purpose behind Periyar’s insistence that the conference itself announce the candidates was that, if the decision had to be made publicly there, a large majority of Brahmins could not be placed in the eight constituencies. Mylapore Iyengar wanted a committee because, if candidates were secretly nominated without informing the conference, most could be Brahmins. In the end, Mylapore Iyengar’s desire was fulfilled. Even so, a storm arose from Kanchi.

Before the Brahmin-non-Brahmin dispute exploded within Congress, it had long been rumbling. Periyar began his journey in 1925; until then he had fought from within. The conditions requiring struggle never changed. When a small Brahmin group sought to seize a great national organisation, just as it had seized many institutions already capable of conferring influence, and use it to increase its self-interest, how could truth-conscious non-Brahmins avoid fighting? One may say the conflict had continued since 1919.

In 1919, while Periyar was in Congress, the Nationalist Association was established at Erode on Achariyar’s proposal. Salem Vijayaraghavachariar was chosen president, Rajagopalachari general secretary, Prakasam organiser for Andhra, and Periyar organiser for Tamil Nadu. V. O. Chidambaram Pillai’s name was proposed for vice-president, and Dr Varadarajulu and Periyar supported it.

Achariyar did not consent. At once the matter raised the scent of a Brahmin-non-Brahmin dispute. Achariyar used his stratagem and said it could be considered in the afternoon. Both parties came to the afternoon conference with their ranks. Achariyar proposed that there be five vice-presidents and Chidambaram Pillai be merely one of them. In other words, he devised a method of making the vice-presidency tasteless and without substance. Periyar and Varadarajulu argued on behalf of non-Brahmins and prevailed. The defeated Achariyar sorrowfully said, “The Justice Party is a hundred times better than you.”

In 1920 the Provincial Conference met at Tirunelveli. The Justice Party was fighting for communal representation. Periyar’s group argued that the principle was just and therefore supported by non-Brahmins; Congress should adopt it both to strengthen itself and to bring the non-Brahmin community into its fold. Periyar had first discussed it with Achariyar during the 1919 Tamil Provincial Congress Conference at Tiruchi and obtained his consent.

At the Tamil Provincial Congress in Tirunelveli, Periyar and Chidambaram Pillai introduced the same communal representation resolution, passed it in the Subjects Committee, and brought it before the conference. The conference president, Mylapore Srinivasa Iyengar, used his authority to reject it. The Kamarajs seethed.

The conference of 1921 was held at Thanjavur. When Periyar arranged to move the communal resolution, Brahmin leaders assembled and promised that justice and protection would be secured for non-Brahmins everywhere, including within the Congress organisation. Thus they caused the resolution to slip away.

The 1922 conference met at Tiruppur. Dr Varadarajulu, then renowned as the Tilak of the South, enjoyed support throughout the province to preside. Yet Brahmin newspapers, especially *Mithran*, schemed to make an Iyer named Vasudeva Rao president. The Brahmin-non-Brahmin quarrel grew so fierce that it nearly came to blows. At that conference Periyar spoke against caste distinctions. It was there too that he said the *Ramayana* should be burned.

Later the Provincial Congress Committee met at Tiruchi and elected Periyar president. Immediately the Brahmins gathered forces to say he must not remain president. V. V. S. Iyer introduced a no-confidence motion against him. Kalyanasundaranar warned that the motion arose from Brahmin-non-Brahmin hatred and ought not be brought; if it were, non-Brahmins would later have to resolve that they had no confidence in Congress. The motion was nevertheless introduced, and the Brahmins suffered a crushing defeat.

The Tamil Provincial Congress Conference of 1923 was held at Tiruvannamalai under Periyar’s presidency. His presidential address contained many truths concerning non-Brahmins. The non-Brahmin hand rose in the conflict, but Mylapore Iyengar disrupted that rank. He scattered money and split the force. His hand became strong at Kanchi.

The Kamarajs were there. Periyar decided he need no longer remain a Kamaraj; he would leave and expose the fraudulent drama through which Brahmins operated Congress. Subramania Nainar, Janab Thangameeran, Sirkazhi Saminatha Chettiar, Ayyamuthu, Coimbatore Subbaiah, Mayavaram Subramania Pillai, S. Ramanathan, Dandapani Pillai, S. V. Lingam, and others followed. Today Periyar’s ranks contain thousands because of that resolve, fearless heart, and Tamil spirit.

In 1927 the Tamil Provincial Congress Conference met at Gokhale Hall in Madras. Marudavanam Pillai of Thanjavur was chosen president. But the power of that small group caused him to resign, and K. V. Rangaswami Iyengar presided.

The conference of 1929 was at Vedaranyam. There was abundant resentment. Sardar Patel had been invited to preside. The Mylapore group sent numerous telegrams asking him not to come and made many attempts to prevent the conference. They failed. Patel presided. A no-confidence resolution against Mylapore Iyengar was introduced. After enormous effort, the Brahmins caused it to be withdrawn. Muthuranga Mudaliar became president of the Tamil Provincial Congress Committee. Before that year ended, however, Achariyar became committee president. Meanwhile the Brahmins continued their “work” without pause.

The Thirty-Fourth Conference assembled at Madurai in 1930. The Tamil group wanted Rukmani Lakshmipathi to preside; the Brahmins wanted Satyamurti. The contest was severe and resentment abundant. Satyamurti obtained more votes and became president. That vote too resembled the Tiruchengode incident: a district committee arbitrarily announced its vote for Satyamurti without holding a proper election or meeting and seated the Iyer. The next conference was not properly held because of the satyagraha.

The Thirty-Sixth Conference was held at Coimbatore under Achariyar’s presidency. The vice-presidential contest was between Satyamurti and Sardar Vedaratnam. It went to a vote three times. Though the sacred thread worked vigorously, Sardar Vedaratnam won all three. The Tamil demonstrated both courage and ability; wishing also to show magnanimity, he resigned the office he had won. Satyamurti then became vice-president, after losing three times.

Then came Karaikudi. The position now held by Kamaraj was then held by Muthuranga Mudaliar. Tamil support for Mudaliar was complete. Nevertheless Achariyar boldly launched a battle to make Satyamurti president. Telegrams flew and emissaries departed. Achariyar had earlier found fault with Satyamurti’s public and private life, but for communal loyalty he forgot it all and worked without sleep night or day. The intrigue succeeded. Muthuranga Mudaliar lost and Satyamurti won.

As in Tamil Nadu today, there was indignation and condemnation then. Achariyar paid no heed. Following the principle that accomplishing the object mattered more than valour, he thought, “They will speak until their mouths ache for four days. Let them; what is that to us? They cannot oppose with Naicker’s persistence.” Having crowned Satyamurti, he adjusted his sacred thread and looked at the Tamils with a dismissive smile.

Within a few days, all who had raged and fought declared, “We surrender at Achariyar’s sacred feet.” Why? Achariyar showed that the northern leaders stood behind him. Brahmin newspapers gathered strength for them. At Karaikudi Achariyar saw Kamaraj with tears in his eyes; he sees him so today.

To show that the indignation then equalled today’s, we offer one example. Condemning Achariyar’s conduct at Karaikudi, T. S. Thiagaraja Gnaniar, a Congress comrade from Tiruvarur, wrote:

“Having learned of the course taken by the recent political conference at Karaikudi, the intrigues through which Brahmins such as Rajagopalachari, who visited it, secured the presidency for a Brahmin, and the defeat in the presidential election of the rare and great non-Brahmin Congress patriot Muthuranga Mudaliar, who sacrificed body, wealth, and life to the commands of Congress, if we remain silent it has become perfectly clear that in future Congress Brahmins will neither consent nor bear to grant non-Brahmins any high position in politics or any other field, or allow us to obtain one ourselves. It is certain that they will create many obstacles and erect barriers through covert intrigues to prevent us from gaining office.”

Thiagaraja Gnaniar wrote this during the Karaikudi incident nearly ten years ago, at almost the same time of year. He wrote on 28 February 1936. In February 1946, people like him again write columns every day in agitation and anger. How much longer they will continue writing like this, no one knows.

Kamaraj’s tears arise not from a hardship born today or yesterday. Wherever and whoever resists Aryan domination, the Aryans will make the resisting Kamarajs weep. They know that servants who stand worshipping can only cry out their grievance and exhaust themselves; they cannot resist and demand justice or fight and destroy injustice. They also know that a “devotee’s” anger will not last long. Show one small thing that offers satisfaction, give a faint smile, or utter one or two sweet words, and the tears will stop and the hands will again fold in worship.

By now Achariyar and his community will have discovered how to pacify Kamaraj and may already have begun carrying out the plan. Soon we may see Kamaraj wipe his eyes and move about again. But whenever a Tamil who refuses to let self-respect weaken thinks of Kamaraj’s fate, sparks cannot fail to rise in his eyes.

We are not deceived by the indignation raised by the “Kamaraj incident.” We do not treat that indignation as reliable stock in the belief that it will endure. We attend to it only as one stage in the story of the Aryan-Dravidian struggle, not as an exercise in recruiting followers.

The Kamaraj incident is one episode in the sorrowful continuing story of Aryans suppressing Dravidians. We know that those who stand worshipping will weep, exhaust their tears, and grow quiet. But it is the Dravidian who will write the final chapter of this continuing story. He knows what that final chapter is. Its title will be: “The Dravidian Land for the Dravidians!”

(Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 3 March 1946.)

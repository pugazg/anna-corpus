## English Translation

*Questions raised by the great scholar Anna: Can they give even one purana an interpretation grounded in reason? Have they done so? Will they? Written under the pen name Bharathan in the 7 May 1944 issue of Dravida Nadu.*

Why the armour of sacred ash?

Why the long rudraksha beads?

Why immerse yourself in water?

Why stand and pray?

Why compose hymns of praise?

Why pluck flowers?

Why decorate the wickedness that practises falsehood?

Why all these outward disguises? The heart is a robbers' cave and the deed that of a red dog! Sacred ash upon the forehead, a rosary in the hand, rudraksha around the neck, a dictionary under the arm and the four Saiva saints upon the tongue—why this drama? An eminent man asks this. He is not a Self-Respecter, but one still bound to some extent by antiquity and devotion. Yet even he cannot tolerate the conduct of these masqueraders.

“What a disguise! Why so much deceit? What benefit comes from such rites?” he wonders in anguish. From that anguish spring his questions, one rapidly following another: “Why do you smear ash? Why wear long rudraksha beads?” He rebukes them: “Does a person as wicked as you need all this?” Nor does he stop there:

“There is no liberation, no attainment,

no power of grace, no benefit whatever.”

“Why put on a disguise, useless fellow? What fruit do you expect? Nothing will come to one who wears a false guise: neither liberation nor divine power,” he says.

Why does he speak this way? Only after asking, “What use is devotion when truth is absent?” does he say all this. Your speech is false, yet you smear sacred ash? Your lies encompass the universe, yet you wear long rudraksha beads? There is no truth—why the disguise? These are the questions of our musically gifted comrade Lakshmana Pillai. He is no Self-Respecter, no person known for scorching words, and no one who rages at Saiva devotees. Yet even he cannot bear the state of religious impostors. A rationalist will naturally feel still greater anger.

Everyone knows that such costumes appeal to the uneducated. As a warrior needs a sword, a puranic performer needs his decorative guise. One may equally say that just as a blind person needs a staff, the puranic performer needs such a costume. A puranic lecturer without costume is as rare as a woman without adornment, a pigeon without a mate or a princess without a companion.

Their costume helps them collect money and once secured them respectability. The speed of the age is now knocking that respect away. There are still places where devotees in unenlightened congregations give these puranic performers milk and honey, silk and ceremonial robes, jewellery and gems. But once a little reason develops, one can only pity them and ask, “Why do these performers survive by telling such lies?” Can a rational world respect those whose tongues speak falsehood for material gain?

Whether their money diminishes quickly or not, the prestige of puranic liars must dissolve as the age advances. I warn those who refuse to notice the times and arrange their lives so earrings swing at their ears, a weighty voice sings, heated milk runs down the throat and their eyes search for helpless patrons: they will later suffer greatly.

Enough of standing in decoration, lying and deceiving ordinary people. They must adopt the higher principle: “Hereafter we shall nourish our bodies by speaking truth.” How many lies! What gigantic fabrications! Are the puranas they narrate compatible with reason, founded on inquiry, useful to society or capable of clarifying thought? Can they offer a rational interpretation of even one purana? Have they? Will they?

They conceal their baseness, shake their heads feverishly and pronounce curses. Beyond this, can these book-bearers explain cause and effect? Trading in words will no longer succeed, and the country will no longer endure it. At a time when the conviction grows that the worker must be freed from enslavement by the idle, what benefit is there in singing of Parvati's lover in the bedchamber? What greater social betrayal could there be?

If, while a father is dying, a mother's cries split the heart and relatives wail, the son sits in a flower garden singing Punnagavarali, does not his music itself become disgraceful? Would no one say, “Fool! Enough music. Go inside and see the agony of the woman who bore you”?

Likewise, the country is wasting away, people suffer, intelligence lies dimmed and poverty runs rampant. At such a time we hear of the nectar of Tiruppugazh, recitations of the Tevaram, the delight of Kamban's poetry and the fivefold nectar of purana. When one asks why, is it enough to melt in tears and cry, “Will you not cut out the questioner's tongue, Namashivaya?”

Kirupananda Variyar is a puranic preacher, learned in classical Tamil, familiar with poetic wealth, a servant of Kandan and disciple of Kapali. His discourse is said to be delicious, his puranic knowledge deep, his delivery swift, his quotations astonishing and his income abundant. The question is whether his work suits this moment.

Let him possess Arumugan's grace and mastery of the threefold Tamil. I ask whether his eloquence and labour help even by the smallest measure to wipe away today's suffering, remove poverty, abolish discrimination, overthrow the base and destroy ignorance.

In an age when aircraft fly through the sky, is it right, beautiful, fitting or conducive to intellectual growth to read: “Without being seen, our Lord, the Lord of all, Ekamban of Kanchi and bridegroom of Kamakshi, arrived in the form of the bull-mounted deity; immediately celestial drums resounded and the gods showered flowers”?

Under the name of sacred-story entertainment, spreading empty matters and cosmic falsehoods among people is a murder of time and thought. Is it fitting to practise this murderous trade? Do these agents of the priestly order who read puranas possess the maturity or character to answer rationalists' doubts? There is no answer; they cannot give one. They continue their business in a trembling voice saying, “Come privately, please,” but do they give a clear and direct reply?

They pour out what they have memorised, collect applause and money, and depart. Do they possess any other method?

When nationalist masqueraders cannot answer questions, they know a noise by which to escape: they shout “Victory to Mahatma Gandhi!” and the meeting ends. Similarly, when questions arise at puranic gatherings, Saiva elders rely upon “Nama Parvati Pataye!” as the means of closing shop. Vaishnavas have their cry, “Refuge at our Lord's sacred feet!” If these supposedly supreme beings know nothing but ending meetings with a shout whenever rationalists ask questions, will the people not begin to think?

Will they not ask, “What is this? Why do these masters of every art become confused by ordinary questions from very ordinary people, seek divine grace and look in every direction for an escape route?” Will they not begin asking whether this is a livelihood worthy of scholarship?

Puranic scholars should consider it calmly. They cannot continue forever singing, “With the Lord of Alavai, Arumugan's spear and the elephant-faced god beside me, I neither fear nor see anything to fear; we are subjects of none.”

At a temple gathering in Kumbakonam, the eminent devotee Kirupananda Variyar reportedly conducted a puranic discourse on Seemanthani. One slip of paper disrupted it. Some questions were written upon the paper. Confusion arose. The preacher invited the questioners to his lodging, promising an answer there. Some said, “Let everyone hear; speak here.” Others said no, and still others ordered them to stop.

Trouble followed. Having heard some puranic narrator tell a story of a holy man worshipping Siva with stones, some people reportedly threw stones. The police entered. Rather than grieving that matters should change this way, the learned must recognise that people's minds will no longer submit to sweet falsehood. I say this with goodwill and knowledge of the country's condition, not as abuse. Puranic performers should please trust me: I am their last friend—truly!

Near Kolar, some people reportedly attempted to stage “Kamban,” then abandoned it after learning of the local Tamils' anger. A man who came to read the Ramayana in Pattukkottai reportedly rolled up his manuscript, tucked it under his arm and strode away. Devotees of Kamban gathered in a corner of Kanchi, where questions arose too; opponents of the Kamba Ramayana mounted the platform and stated their case.

R. P. Sethu Pillai may say, “We shall speak tirelessly of the master of words even if the sky falls upon our heads.” Meenakshisundaranar may thunder in his majestic voice, “Whatever happens, we shall complete the task undertaken.” Thiru. V. Kalyanasundaranar may declare, “I shall not fear this; I shall fear none of it; I fear nothing.” They may truly be brave.

But one thing is certain: inwardly, must they not recognise that purana survives today only because of their courage? Should their knowledge and ability be spent guarding a corpse? The country asks their service for many other good works.

A great thinker answers the question “Who are social traitors?” thus: first, one who does not use his ability; second, and still more treacherous, one who uses his ability improperly. I say with genuine sorrow that people of such learning and talent beat their breasts for a useless cause.

Some try to hide the situation with the single phrase “This is Self-Respect hooliganism.” Is that not like the desert bird that hides its face and foolishly imagines it has escaped the hunter's eye? Were the question sheets issued by Self-Respecters at this gathering hooliganism? They asked doubts raised by the times. Is not a lecture without explanation food without salt?

These dignitaries call Self-Respecters uneducated. Yet why do questions from these “uneducated” people instantly drive subtle intelligence from the minds of speakers who live by narrating things that never existed? The uneducated are supposed merely to clap and nod, not ask what words mean.

I ask again: has any puranic preacher ever established his case through argument? He cannot, because the puranas contain so many lies.

Consider even the story of Seemanthani. What path does it show for improving people? What real benefit comes from reading or hearing it? Attaching refined Tamil to it disgraces Tamil. These useless stories move about only by borrowing the talent of sweet-speaking performers like Kirupananda Variyar. Instead of fibre gaining fragrance by association with a flower, Tamil and Tamils, good people and eloquent speakers, are corrupted by joining themselves to purana like a calf joining a pig. Is that good?

Seemanthani was the daughter of King Chitravarman. An astrologer predicted that her marriage pendant would be severed in her fourteenth year—that she would be widowed. Knowing this, she observed the Monday fast. She married Chandrangadan, king of the Nishadha country.

Chandrangadan travelled with friends in a boat upon the Yamuna. The boat capsized and everyone died, but Chandrangadan alone escaped and went to the serpent world. The serpent king received him and sent him away with many gifts. Meanwhile, believing Chandrangadan dead, people dressed Seemanthani as a widow.

One day Chandrangadan came to the Yamuna bank to observe the Monday fast, and Seemanthani arrived at the same time. Seeing his wife in widow's dress, Chandrangadan spoke to her as though he were a stranger and asked why she appeared so. She told him her husband had died, Nishadha had fallen to enemies and Chandrangadan's parents had been imprisoned.

He consoled her, saying, “Do not worry; your husband will return within two days.” He then went to Nishadha, defeated its enemies, freed his parents from prison and married Seemanthani again.

The purpose of telling this is to delude ordinary people into believing that observing the Monday fast brings exceptional benefits. Vast numbers in this country suffer the pity of going hungry for weeks through poverty. No fast has yet relieved their disease of hunger. What benefit comes from telling a story about the Monday fast and making people enamoured of it? Will reason grow? Will courage grow? Will comfort increase or property accumulate?

Many women will think, “Because Seemanthani observed the Monday fast, her marriage pendant was not severed. Let us too fast and remain auspicious married women.” They will bathe, apply turmeric and vermilion, and eagerly worship. Temples will consequently gain revenue and Brahmins receive gifts.

Those who describe the rules of the Monday fast do not fail to mention that an honorarium must be given. Whether or not the devotees receive a benefit, the Vedic priests receive theirs immediately.

The Seemanthani story was written so ordinary people would perceive the necessity of the Monday fast. Kirupananda Variyar performs it to display his classical Tamil skill. The course of the story and the preacher's eloquence enchant the public, which begins fasting on Mondays. This gives Aryanism another opportunity. Those who bow at priestly feet and offer money earned by hard labour are ordinary people. Someone writes a story in a book; another tells it in poetic speech. The result is that ordinary people lose money, time, thought and dignity while Brahmins grow fat.

Whether knowingly or not, these preachers act as commission agents for Aryanism. Is that not clear? Puranic speakers escape by prefacing the performance as a sacred tale illustrating devotion. Otherwise, how many contradictions, absurdities and obscenities these stories contain! If people regarded them merely as ordinary tales, they would not listen patiently for even a minute.

The Self-Respect Movement exposes the frauds in such puranas so as to sharpen public intelligence and create indifference and revulsion towards them. Is that wrong?

Are there millions of widows in the country today because they did not know how to observe the Monday fast? Even the puranas and epics contain many widows. Did they not know that fasting on Monday would preserve their marriage pendants?

If the Seemanthani story truly teaches that a Monday fast can strengthen a pendant destined to be severed, Sumitra, mother of Rama, could have fasted to prevent Dasaratha dying or make him rise again. Why did she not? Did she not know its glory, or did she not wish to recover Dasaratha?

Kunti need not have lost Pandu. She could have observed the fast and obtained immortality for her husband. Countless women have lost husbands. Did they become widows because they did not know how to observe this fast? If the glory attributed to it were true, women who lost husbands in “Bharata Khanda” would never have mounted their husbands' funeral pyres. If there is honesty in proclaiming the fast's power, should not all this be answered and explained?

A physician sells a pill in the morning, saying, “This is a marvellous medicine. Mix it with milk and drink it only three times; even the severest stomach pain will fly away.” That very evening his cries from stomach pain echo through the street. Does this not reveal the pill's worth? Had it possessed the power he claimed, he would have swallowed it himself and suffered neither pain nor agony. He praised it not because he knew it had that power, but because the pill must sell and he must make a living. Hence the lie.

The puranic preachers' position is the same. How many widows are there among the Brahmins singled out as worthy recipients of worship during the Monday fast? Did they not know how to observe it?

Leave aside the fraud of the fast's supposed glory and examine the story's course. Does it accord with reason? Despite fasting, Seemanthani loses Chandrangadan. A man submerged in the Yamuna supposedly reaches the serpent world. How? Is the road to that world beneath the Yamuna?

Chandrangadan possessed enough power and merit to enter that realm and be honoured by its king. Had he used a little intelligence, he might have thought, “My wife will suffer without seeing me and conclude that I drowned.” He could have asked the serpent king to send Seemanthani news of his welfare, sparing her widow's garb. Why did he not? Perhaps the chief glory of the Monday fast is losing one's reason. Such stories help multiply the irrational. Is spreading them not social betrayal? Whether traitors wear tulsi or rudraksha beads, betrayal remains betrayal.

Even if Chandrangadan lacked the sense to send word, does the story proceed rationally afterward? He returns to the Yamuna and sees Seemanthani as a widow. Any intelligent man would run to her and say, “Beloved! What fortune that I see you! Did you dress as a widow because you thought me dead? Abandon your grief; come, let us go home.” The puranic character does not. He pretends to be a stranger. Why this deception? Perhaps deceit is another part of the Monday fast's glory.

Many more contradictions remain. The Self-Respect Movement asks these men not to corrupt ordinary people by reading such puranas. Some listeners ask doubtful questions of those who spin stories incompatible with reason. If they can answer, they must. If not, they must close shop.

Instead of doing either, what kind of conduct is it to shout “Nama Parvati Pataye!” the moment a question arises? Is it a heroic roar, a verse of wisdom, an aged man's venba, an antati of fearful inquiry or a century of agitation? I do not know where it belongs.

One thing is clear: “Nama Parvati Pataye!” cannot long remain the answer to rationalists' questions. The people will perceive the truth and laugh, clapping their hands: “Look at these long-tongued puranic speakers! When Self-Respecters are absent, they roar like great champions. Before Self-Respect questions their throats dry, eyes wander, limbs weaken, valour collapses, sweat pours and they stand terrified, immersed in anxiety, crying ‘Dweller of Kailasa! Abode of compassion!’ What a spectacle.”

Before that humiliating state arrives, I advise puranic performers to burn their palm-leaf manuscripts and establish a new way of life. The country is rapidly acquiring sound understanding.

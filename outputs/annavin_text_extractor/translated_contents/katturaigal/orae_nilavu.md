## English Translation

*(An article written on the occasion of revolutionary poet Bharathidasan's sixtieth-birthday celebration)*

Those who see him may jeer and clap their hands;

there may be mocking laughter, ridicule and condemnation,

the howling of herds and the judgement of fools;

he is a "king" whose mind cannot be bent.

The stirring tribute heaped upon him by people's hearts,
undying fame, influence beyond reach,
and the people's unfading love:

these alone are his crown!

Money for a poem, a prize for a thought,
an award for victory, an offering to fame;
the ruler's command, "A song for hire, let it be done, give it!"

is no more than dust to him.

His thought throbs; his heart sings!
Dreams burst open and poems blossom.
Tears flow; a stream of words pours forth.
The war-drum rises! Sorrows depart!
Trouble, poverty and want:
to abolish them all, he raises his song.

— Pushkin

A poet is a mirror of his age,
not a reflection of the hue of antiquity!
Fervour in his heart, a storm in his feeling:

these raise the song of his heart.

Alliteration, rhyme and graceful simile,
captivating description and a polish upon the old:
he does not run everywhere searching for these,

nor is he a fisherman knotting a net of words!

— Walt Whitman

Fragments of history that give form
to the sublime moments of noble hearts:
those are poetry; that is the book of life!

— Shelley

Sweet music that awakens and displays
lofty thought and elevated feeling:
that is poetry.

— Voltaire

The statements placed above are explanations given by Pushkin of Russia, Voltaire of France, Shelley of England and Whitman of America concerning who a poet is and what defines poetry. Anyone may ask whether their words can be the final judgement. Yet one cannot readily say that anyone has a greater right than they to speak on the matter. The world has long acknowledged them as incomparable poets occupying an exalted place in the literary histories of their respective countries.

All of them, however, were heralds of a new age, cultivators who scattered the seeds of renaissance in their languages. Before their emergence, a poet was thought to be one blessed by God, whose duty was to sing of the greatness of temples and the glory of miracles, or to compose verses about the might of kings and the adornments of their beautiful queens. In our own country, until yesterday, it was believed that a poet required divine favour and the grace of the goddess Saraswati. Poets painted verbal pictures only of the glory of Hari's incarnations, the splendour of the other world, the baseness of this world, and the necessity of worshipping God. An epic would either concern Maheswara or the languid pleasures of palace-dwellers sporting with beautiful women. Nothing about the people arose beyond these themes.

That unfortunate condition has now been brought to an end. In our generation, Tamil Nadu has produced an incomparable poet. Today the country proudly declares that the Tamil language has never seen another poet like him. Tamil itself fondles and plays in his poetry.

There is a breeze there, and there is a storm. There is fire, and there is cool shade. Smiles, tears, sighs, cries, lamentation, heroic speech and a child's prattle are all woven together. There one sees a noble portrait of the Tamil people's heart. People see distilled into poetry all the struggles raging in their minds. They regard it as a feast that has come to impart energy to their fallen lives.

Those poems contain no refrain praising antiquity. They do not beat time to the songs of ancient poets, nor have they been pawned to a grammar book. People sincerely feel that the poet has poured the music of his heart into the vessel of literature to inspire the awakening of the race. His poetry explains the country's affliction and the necessity of removing it. It gives advance notice of the society that must arise in the future. A new ray of hope enters people's lives; a new awakening appears in lives that had lain asleep. Tamil Nadu therefore welcomes the poet responsible for this transformation and blesses him, saying, "Long live the poet!" The man who has won that distinction is Bharathidasan.

To understand Bharathidasan's greatness, a portrait of the Tamil Nadu in which he lives is indispensable. Circumstances create a poet. The question is whether he became a slave to those circumstances or showed a road from them into the future.

Here is the Tamil Nadu in which he lives. Tamil receives no elevation here; it is made to remain degraded. There is an abundance of needless caste-fire, widowhood without dawn, cruel child marriage, marriage in which love cannot flourish, poor people without even gruel, hard-hearted brokers of God, and rich men who torment labourers. These sights shake the poet's mind. His heart grieves: "My precious Tamil land, how low you have fallen!"

He recalls the sweetness of the Tamil language and knows the ordeals it suffers. Sanskrit, brought by deceivers, quarrels with it. The arrogance of English tries to extinguish Tamil's light. Hindi, which came from somewhere else, longs to mount the throne. How many arrows can Mother Tamil endure? Setting Hindi's rise against Tamil's decline, he therefore asks:

"Is there no coolness in the fragrant breeze?
Is there no shade in the grove?

This anguish is impossible to assuage:

in the Tamil streets of Tamil Nadu,
Tamil itself is absent."

These are not mere strings of words but an emotional picture of the country's condition. A breeze cannot exist without coolness; a grove cannot exist without shade. Yet Tamil names are absent from Tamil streets in Tamil Nadu. What a strange sorrow! That is why he can speak like the fiery lava of a volcano:

"When harm has come to classical Tamil,
what profit is there in this body remaining?

From which side will it break in?
However many battalions Hindi may gather,
if disgrace befalls our mother tongue,
we shall offer our body's fresh blood.

We shall resist the harmful Hindi;
we shall not reckon the sweetness of life.
The flower garden where the mango-cuckoo sings
is the prison that seeks to confine us!"

No other poet has written as many songs as he has about Tamil's distinction and the duty of protecting it; nor could another do so. Such is his inexhaustible love for Tamil.

Mayakovsky, praised as the foremost poet of the socialist age, said: "I did not labour all my life merely to create beautiful songs, pour the honey of fine words into listeners' ears and ravish their hearts. My struggle was to overturn certain people and certain arrangements. In my twenty years of movement work, I have truly been conducting a battle."

Mayakovsky's words are especially true of Bharathidasan. His literary service has been a boxing match. In the early days, those called eminent scholars did not include him among the poets. Scorn, mockery and ridicule were his rewards. When the poet Tagore, celebrated as the gold of Bengal, composed his songs, Bengali scholars would give them to their pupils and ask them to correct the grammatical errors! A similar situation persisted here.

There was honeyed classical language in Bharathidasan's poems, but he did not think their purpose was merely to captivate the heart. He regarded his struggle as one meant to overturn certain people and arrangements. Thus, when he saw the cruelty imposed on widows, he asked:

"If a good young woman, like an unfading flower,
loses her husband, is it wrong for her to marry again?
Has anyone seen bees that do not sing,
a breeze that does not roam,
or a healthy stomach that never hungers?"

At those who condemned marriages of love he hurled these angry words:

"Once two lovers have united in their purpose,
why arguments and quarrels?
What concern have others in it?
O world filled with deceit,
O dark chamber of fraud!"

He portrayed the cruelty of child marriage with compassion:

"She is a cuckoo chick that has not learned to call,
a little fawn that has never leapt, untempered iron;
her marriage thread severed,
this little girl remains in her father's house."

Of caste cruelty he asks:

"If thirty-three crores of people live here,
the divisions surrounding them are as numerous.
How are we to remove this mire of distress,
this depth that cannot be climbed?"

These poems were not merely lashes upon society's agents of decay; they became instruments for abolishing those cruelties. One may say that they rendered great service in overturning these social arrangements.

If Iqbal was the guardian of Islamic culture, Bharathidasan may be called the guardian of Dravidian culture. One day the world will have to acknowledge that the poet's *The Smile of Beauty* surpasses Tagore's descriptions of nature. Shelley directed the power of his poetry against tyrants; Bharathidasan's poems tell us that he surpassed Shelley even in this. Walt Whitman removed the moss-grown doctrines of antiquity and polished the way for a new society. Our poet's songs dig a grave for the old and resound like a war-drum welcoming a new life.

The poor labour while pleasure-seekers consume the fruit of their work and grow fat. Shelley, speaking of this, said:

"People, why do you plough and sow for the rich who humble you? Why do you provide fine clothes for those who disdain you? Why, from birth to death, do you sustain with food and clothing those thankless drones who drain your sweat, drink your blood and torment you? Do you possess good food, clothing, rest, shelter, comfort or peace? You sow; another reaps. You weave; another wears. You forge weapons; another uses them against you."

The poet Iqbal said: "The capitalist enjoys luxury through the worker's blood. The landlord ruins the cultivator's life and lives in pleasure. The ruler rejoices after fastening the worker to the yoke. But people caught in oppression do awaken. Sometimes a lion's blood flows even in the bodies of the weak; at times a spark of fire appears even in a drop of water."

Bharathidasan, expressing that same truth, asks:

"We cleared the forests and made
good fields; we ploughed them
and built nations, laying out
streets in all four directions.

We wove clothes; we bent
great rivers and planted rows of paddy.
We brought this once unfit world
to the condition in which it now stands.

Yet we wore rags; stretching out
both hands, we covered our bodies.
Many of us crowded around a single pot
to drink gruel and lay hungry.

Like cattle in the market, we had
no house in which to dwell permanently.
Our minds wasted away.
Is this the gratitude for all our service?"

Comparing the three passages quoted above bears witness to the concentration of thought and subtlety of meaning in Bharathidasan's songs.

Old literature ended by blessing Vedic orthodoxy and instilling barren Vedanta. This is why a poisonous tree took root in Tamil hearts. Poetry did not become a companion to life. The poet's *Family Lamp* and *The Dark House*, by contrast, set out a grammar for living. His writings are treasuries of tools useful for bringing a new life into bloom.

There is a group that complains that the poet's literature has a propagandistic purpose. Such people are not found here alone; they have existed in every country. The plays of the late Bernard Shaw, the foremost genius of English dramatic literature, were accused of being propagandistic, mere conversations and history lessons. Shaw is said to have replied: "I have certain principles to tell society. Drama is a convenient means for me to express those principles, and that is why I write. My purpose is indeed propaganda."

Yet Shaw's plays earned the name of great literature. Thus all this cry of "propaganda, propaganda" is nothing but a stratagem for wrecking an endeavour.

Today, 29 April, is our poet's sixtieth-birthday celebration. Tamil Nadu's revolutionary poet steps into his sixtieth year. Happiness and fulfilment overflow in our hearts! Bharathidasan is the one moon in the Tamil sky. He is the treasure of the Dravidian land. His poems are an immortal fruit dripping honey. Today Tamil Nadu offers him its heartfelt wishes: "Long may he live! May his fame spread throughout the world!" Our own hearts filled by the joyous festival of Tamil Nadu, we too dedicate our heartfelt greetings to him.

Long live, long live our poet!

*(Dravida Nadu, 29 April 1951)*

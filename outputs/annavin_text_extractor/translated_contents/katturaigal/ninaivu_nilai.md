## English Translation

Eyes showing a gentle laugh; a smile within a toothless mouth. The elderly Anjugam would become as a dry leaf turning into a fresh shoot, not on seeing the prosperity of her children, not on seeing cattle or property, and not on seeing relatives, but whenever she heard news of the movement and its victories.

I have seen mothers complain, "My son pays no attention to the house or to his work. Please advise him."

I have seen mothers speak of their sons by asking me to recommend that they buy a house or a garden.

I have seen mothers say emotionally, "My son has grown as thin as a splinter. He wanders from town to town. I raised him with so much affection, and now he lies in such a ruined condition."

I have seen mothers cry, wondering what food their sons were being given in prison and how they were being made to suffer.

I have seen mothers indirectly reproach me by saying, "He is a good boy. It is by joining those companions that he has become like this."

I have seen indifferent mothers say, "What do we know or understand?"

But I had never seen a mother who, the moment she saw me, poured forth her enthusiasm with questions such as these: "Our party will capture the mayoralty, won't it? We must not let that go. They say a new law is coming; what are you going to do? When will the agitation to reduce prices begin? What news about our party is in today's papers? They say a paper printed a cartoon mocking our party; did you see it? What are you going to do?"

Perhaps it was because younger brother Karunanidhi had received such a motherly treasure that he was given the name "Nidhi", treasure.

She was seventy-two. Her appearance was of an unadorned village simplicity, without ornaments. In that household, old Mrs Anjugam moved about like one of the mothers who had come from another town for a Kazhagam conference, seeking her pleasure in talk of the movement alone.

The mother who moved among us has now become a picture.

From the days of the Self-Respect Movement onward, I have seen the mothers in the homes of movement workers attend conferences.

I know too how they would show one another various objects and say, "I bought this at that conference and this one at another."

But I heard this elderly woman speak far more often about political questions: "What happened to that issue? What happened to this one?" I listened and marvelled.

If a son such as this had possessed a mother of a different disposition, public life would have become filled with domestic resentment. I recognize that the smile and affection Karunanidhi received from his mother at home gave him a great opportunity to engage in public life with a smile.

Were I to write about all this, I could write a great deal.

The more I write, the more the younger brothers who already lie weeping may increase their tears. I therefore stop.

Mrs Anjugam's clarity, firmness, and enthusiasm have not disappeared. Karunanidhi, Maran, Amirtham, and Selvam must say, "See them sprout, flower, form fruit, and ripen through us."

Mrs Anjugam would never permit them merely to lie crying or suffer in bewilderment.

She had resolved that her children belonged to the country.

Her son accepted hardship and loss in order to perform public service. Her grandchildren involved themselves in different fields and carried out distinguished work. She saw this with her own eyes, heard with her own ears others speak of their good names, attained a full heart, and passed away.

It is natural to think that she might have remained with us longer; there is nothing wrong in that thought. Yet the whole family should find consolation by remembering that she lived at least this long and considering how much distinction she gave us while she was here.

I consider the bond I have formed with that family a natural one. I therefore offer Karunanidhi and the others this affectionate counsel: let the thought that my support and attachment are with you gradually lessen the anguish of losing your mother, and continue to shine as jewels that make Mrs Anjugam's fame radiant.

The cuckoo has flown away; its music endures.

The flower has fallen; its fragrance remains abundant.

Anjugam has passed away; her memory endures.

Written by Perarignar Anna on the death of Kalaignar's mother, *Dravida Nadu*, 3 February 1963.

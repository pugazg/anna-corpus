28. வெட்கப்படுகிறோம்! -
வேதனைப் படுகிறோம்! இல்லை;
விரட்டப்படுகிறோம்!

சென்ற ஆண்டு நாம் நமது தலைவர் பெரியாரின்
71வது ஆண்டு விழாவைச் சிறப்பாகக் கொண்டாடினோம்.

இந்த ஆண்டு அவர் திருமண வைபவத்தைக்
காணும்படி நம்மை அழைக்கிறார் - இல்லை - அறிவிக்கிறார்.

கடந்த ஐந்தாறு ஆண்டுகளாகப் பெரியாருடைய
உடலைக் கவனித்துக் கொள்ளும் திருத்தொண்டிலே தன்னை
ஒப்படைத்துப் பணியாற்றி வந்தார் திருமதி மணி அம்மையார்
இந்தத் திருமதிக்கு வயது 26.

அவர்கள்தான் பெரியாருக்கு மனைவியாகும்
தொண்டில் இப்போது ஈடுபட நேரிட்டிருக்கிறது.

சென்னையில் இவர்கள் பதிவுத் திருமண மனு, பதிவு
நிலையத்தில் கடந்த ஒருவார காலமாக ஒட்டி வைக்கப்
பட்டிருக்கிறது. பலர் பார்த்துத் திகைப்படைந்துள்ளனர்.

பெரியாருக்கு வயது 72.

மணியம்மைக்கு வயது 26. இவர்களின் பதிவுத்
திருமணம் நடைபெற இருக்கிறது.

“என்னிடம் அன்பும், எனது நலத்தில் கவலையும்
உள்ளவர்களுக்கும் சற்றுப் புரட்சியாகவும் திடுக்கிடக்
கூடியதாகவும், இயக்கமே அழிந்துவிடுமோ என்று பயப்படக்
கூடியதாகவும் எனக்கு ஒரு கெட்ட பேரும் இழிவும் ஏற்படக்
கூடிய பெரிய தவறாகவுங்கூடக் காணப்படுவதாகத் தெரிகிறது”
என்று பெரியாரே 28-6-49 விடுதலையில் வெளியிட்ட
அறிக்கையிலே இதைப்பற்றிக் குறிப்பிட்டுள்ளார்.

கோபம், கொதிப்பு இவை மட்டுமே இதனால் ஏற்படும்
என்று அவர் குறிப்பு காட்டுகிறார். ஆனால் இவை மட்டுமல்ல
இந்தச் சேதியாலே ஏற்பட்ட விளைவு - கண்ணீர்! தூய்மை
யுள்ளம் கொண்ட ஆயிரமாயிரம் இயக்கத் தோழர்கள்

கண்ணீர் சொரிந்த வண்ணம் உள்ளனர்.

இந்தக் கண்ணீரை அவர் எதிர்பார்க்கிறாரா? அல்லது
மதிக்கப் போகிறாரா என்பது ஒருபுறமிருக்க, எதற்கும்
கலங்கா உள்ளம் படைத்த இளைஞர்கள் இந்தச் சேதி கேட்டுக்
கண்ணீர் வடிப்பதுபோல வேறு எப்போதும் நடைபெற்றதில்லை
- உலக சம்பவங்களிலேயே இதற்கு இணையானதாக வேறு
ஒன்றைக் காட்டவும் முடியாது.

## English Translation

Last year we celebrated splendidly the seventy-first birthday of our leader Periyar.

This year he invites us - no, informs us - to witness his wedding ceremony.

For the past five or six years, Mrs Maniammai has devoted herself to the sacred service of attending to Periyar's health. She is twenty-six years old. She must now undertake the service of becoming Periyar's wife.

Their application for a registered marriage has been posted at the registration office in Madras for the past week. Many who saw it were stunned. Periyar is seventy-two. Maniammai is twenty-six. Their registered marriage is to take place.

In the statement he published in *Viduthalai* on 28 June 1949, Periyar himself wrote of it: "To those who have affection for me and concern for my welfare, it appears somewhat revolutionary and startling; it may make them fear that the movement itself will perish; it may even appear a grave error that will bring me a bad name and disgrace."

His note indicates that this will produce only anger and fury. But those are not the only consequences of this news. There are tears. Thousands upon thousands of comrades of pure heart are shedding tears.

Whether he anticipated or will value these tears is another matter. Never before have young people with hearts that would not falter at anything shed tears on hearing a piece of news. No parallel to this can be found even among the events of the world.

Countless young people who walked erect as messengers of self-respect and soldiers of liberation, with the proud gait of ascent, now carry broken hearts. They wipe their streaming tears and move about with faltering legs and wringing hands, afraid of who will see them and how they will mock them. Even a heart of stone would melt at that sight.

Marriage is a personal matter. Even marriage in old age is a personal matter. If a private individual, or merely the leader of a political party, marries in old age, hearing it may startle us, invite ridicule or provoke anger, but it will not bring tears. Today, on hearing the news of Periyar's marriage, tears flow in a flood.

We have not regarded him merely as the leader of a political party. Every member of the movement has accepted him as the head of his own family and the guide to his life, showing him a degree of reverence and affection shown to no other movement and no other leader.

To our eyes he has appeared as the great man who would restore our honour, and as the heroic warrior fighting to rescue us from the slavery that has tormented us for ages. His word has been law to us. The path he laid down has seemed the road to liberation. We followed him almost as devotees follow incarnate divinities.

The reason was that we sincerely believed he possessed an individuality and character found in no other leader. As he grew older, he seemed unconcerned with life, family and personal comfort, living like an ascetic, travelling and labouring arduously without regard to night or day, bearing privation and pain so that we might live. We therefore took pride and exulted that he was entirely worthy of the honorific name Periyar and that there was no one else like him.

He told us what others did not; he explained what no one else explained. He devised and showed us a plan by which we could understand ourselves and preserve our honour.

He did not speak merely about politics. He spoke about life, about removing life's difficulties, and about the ways by which new changes could be brought into life.

He demonstrated that the doctrines said to have been uttered by ascetics and recipients of divine grace had made our lives contrary to human nature. "Break those commands," he urged. "Form a new but sacred ideal for life." Thus he delivered his heroic addresses.

The portion of the problem of life on which he spoke with exceptional force and clarity was marriage. No other leader spoke as he did to shatter the superstitious customs in marriage, end the villainy that made women into instruments, and restrain men's cruelty.

Nor did it stop with speech. The whole town changed; our hearts changed. The schemes the rishis had left behind changed. We rejoiced, took pride and even grew haughty. When he condemned mismatched marriages, old men were disturbed and young women rejoiced.

If men roaming in the madness of lust tempted a young woman in their old age with promises of property and comfort, or induced her to consent to marriage on any other pretext, he thundered from thousands of platforms: could young men concerned for honour and dignity permit such a marriage? He infused all of us with fresh vigour.

From thousands of platforms he condemned the cruelty of seeking a wife merely because one had no child and no heir to one's property. He proclaimed that a mismatched marriage was a great curse upon the country. From Dasaratha of old to the aged men of today who seek "gold tonic," he held them all up to ridicule.

Wherever the Self-Respect Movement flourished, if a mismatched marriage was to occur in any home for any reason, public feeling rose so fiercely that police protection might be required. Even those who had already entered such marriages were struck by shame and pain.

"Old men like me should not even think of marrying. If somehow such a thought does arise, let him at least find a woman of forty or fifty whose tie has been severed - that is, a widow - and marry her. Should he marry a woman fresh as a green creeper, a woman of the age and maturity to discover life's pleasures? Let him offer a thousand reasons: what man of honour would call that marriage right? Who would consent to it?" There was no town that had not heard him speak in this manner.

When the man who taught such counsel is to enter a registered marriage at seventy-two with a woman of twenty-six, what can we offer but our tears?

"Why, Dravidar Kazhagam man! Is your leader getting married?" The question pierces the heart like a sharp arrow.

"This is a reform movement, is it? Look here, fellow: reform means a marriage between seventy-two and twenty-six!" They mock us, and on hearing it our hearts burst.

"A staff in the groom's hand! A black dress for the bride!" they jeer.

"Advice is only for the town, is it?" they taunt.

"'What does it matter to me? I am over seventy, with one foot at home and the other in the cremation ground. If I die there is no one to weep for me, and there is no one for whom I might die weeping.' The Periyar who said all this is getting married, fellow!" The marketplace says it and laughs, clapping its hands.

"O peerless champions who condemn the injustices committed in the town! O heroes of heroes who drive out social indignities! O elders who wage a mighty battle for women's liberation! O honourable men who condemned mismatched marriage! Here is your leader, in ascetic garb, at a tottering age, marrying a young woman. What has become of your principles? Where is the worth of your propaganda? What do you say to this? How do you tolerate this injustice, this wrong, this repulsive obscenity?" They speak as though lashing us with a whip. They will speak so still more.

What shall we do? What answer can we give? How can the wound in our hearts be healed? How are we to preserve our honour? Thinking these thoughts, our comrades weep with a grief greater than that caused by the death of a mother, father, wife, daughter, brother or sibling. We too are weeping as we write this article through our tears.

A mismatched marriage! The mismatched marriage of a sacred leader! No assault by an enemy and no action by a government has ever bewildered our comrades as this has today.

We have faced many crises and dangers. We cannot bear this thunderbolt. We cannot; we cannot; we certainly cannot.

He has smeared soot upon our faces and cut off our noses. Since learning the news, we have sat in a corner and sobbed.

We are ashamed to face our neighbours.

We suffer in solitude.

We try to wipe one another's tears. We writhe because the scorpion of sorrow has stung our hearts.

How sharply and passionately have we condemned those who dared enter mismatched marriages! Now our leader announces, with such casual disregard for us, our feelings, our principles and the movement, that he will marry at seventy-two. He declares that he will turn us into walking corpses and make us objects of public laughter: "Go away, ashamed and with bowed heads; what is it to me?"

Alas! What a tremendous blow! What an act of humiliation! Was it for this that we rose so high? Did we grow so far only to accept this blame and disgrace?

What a condition is ours! What a fate! Where is our refuge? What future have we? Great darkness ahead; mockers all around; we stand between them, great pain in our hearts, water in our eyes and trembling in our limbs. This is what we are. Was all this growth for this?

Leader who made us into men! Was all that labour meant to bring us to this condition? What wrong did we commit against you that you should make us unable to show our faces before the world? We walked the path you showed us. Is this our reward?

Whenever you commanded us to go against the storm, we answered not merely in words but in action. Is this our reward?

Father, we have never seen a mother bite off the nose of a child that came to kiss her. What wrong did we commit that you should perform this improper act and sever our self-respect? Why?

What offence have we committed? Why do you obtain for us a disgrace that can never be erased? How shall we bear this destruction?

Whatever thousands of reasons and clever explanations may be offered, the fact of seventy-two and twenty-six cannot be denied. It cannot be hidden that this is a mismatched marriage.

That you, a champion of reform, should do this is a stain time itself cannot wipe away. That cannot be denied. Why do you do this and make us objects of ridicule?

These are the questions thousands upon thousands of young people ask while wiping their tears.

Our hearts throb. Even one who knew us for a single day would melt, but our leader refuses to accept any of our words.

None of us had imagined, even in a dream, that this mismatched marriage could take place. Periyar's appearance, age, speech and seeming lack of attachment to life never led us to think that a "woman" could enter his heart. And what kind of woman?

Maniammai came into the movement five or six years ago to attend to matters such as protecting Periyar's life and health, giving him appropriate food and medicine, and arranging convenience during travel. Her town is Vellore. Her father was Kanakasabai Mudaliar, who too was devoted to the movement. He died; Maniammai's education stopped, and she set out to serve Periyar.

Periyar has no son or daughter. He has an elder brother who could perform service like Maniammai's early service; that brother has two daughters and three sons. Periyar also has an elder sister, with three daughters and two sons. Before a woman from another family came from Vellore and joined him, none of them had refused to attend to Periyar or undertake movement work.

Comrades Gandhi, B.A., and Miranda, B.A., have participated in several conferences. They belong to Periyar's family.

They are not alone. Hundreds of pure-hearted people would compete with one another to attend to Periyar's health. None of them was needed. Maniammai had to come.

I thought a storm was entering. For that I was denounced as a base fellow.

Seeing that lady's devoted service, Dravidars lost the aversion they had initially felt. "Appa! Appa!" she would say, cooling her heart and lips and the ears of those listening. "Amma! Amma!" Periyar would call her, filling the listeners with pride and delight. Seeing this, thousands happily considered Maniammai Periyar's foster daughter.

That foster daughter is now to become Periyar's wife, through a registered marriage.

Who, for whatever reason, can call this condition ordinary?

At hundreds of conferences, mothers proudly pointed Periyar out to the children holding their hands and said, "Look, there is grandfather. Greet him." We heard and rejoiced. Pointing to Maniammai attending beside him, they said, "Grandfather's daughter."

That grandfather is now to marry the maiden who attended him.

The world asks: is it right? Is it proper?

Periyar has some people in the movement and in his personal life who are considered close friends. In every town there are several who happily say, "Periyar discussed this matter with me," or "He asked me to perform this task." There are many such people in Madras too. I too once had that opportunity, but it broke off along the way. - C.N.A.

Despite all these people, Periyar did not speak to any of them about this marriage, seek their counsel, or give even a hint.

They each say in bewilderment, "I did not know! He did not tell me!"

We asked former minister Muthiah Mudaliar: "Periyar holds you in high esteem. Did he perhaps tell you? He visited you on the day he dined with you; did he say anything then?" In a tearful voice he replied, "I did not know. He did not even hint at it."

When Arasu is asked, "You knew, did you not?" he says, "Alas, I knew nothing. Periyar asked me to remain outside and went alone to speak to the Registrar." So says C.T.T. Arasu, a leading member of Periyar's corps of attendants.

"You travelled with him until yesterday. Did you know of this?" Comrade N. V. Natarajan sobs, "I did not."

E. V. K. Sampath, his elder brother's son and manager of *Viduthalai*, did not know. Comrade Gurusami, editor of *Viduthalai*, did not know. Treasurer Govindasami did not know. None of them knew. He spoke to none of them, not even indirectly.

But he discussed this with Governor-General Rajagopalachari at Tiruvannamalai. Our leader himself says so in his statement: he says that he spoke to Rajagopalachari about a matter he did not discuss with any of you and saw no need to discuss with you.

Rajagopalachari is fortunate. We, who have sincerely believed that there is no other leader but Periyar and laboured like bullocks at an oil press until our bodies were worn down, did not receive that good fortune. Our dear Achariar did.

(At the Coimbatore conference I asked what the two of them had discussed. Many said it was wrong of me to ask. - C.N.A.)

Even after consulting Achariar, he did not breathe a word of it to any comrade in the movement. He began the arrangements for the marriage in the utmost secrecy. Somehow the truth emerged. With his heart boiling, Comrade N. V. Natarajan wrote Periyar a letter through his tears.

The letter was disregarded. Saying that he was leaving Madras for Erode, Periyar instead went to Yercaud and stayed there with Maniammai.

Those here who learnt the news trembled and wept. They decided to send a delegation, and the first delegation set out: *Viduthalai* editor Gurusami, Cuddalore comrade Gurusami, Vellore Tirunavukkarasu, Comrade N. V. Natarajan and Kumbakonam comrade K. K. Neelamegam.

They described the bewilderment of the Dravidar Kazhagam and the public's mockery, and they pleaded with him. Periyar gave them an explanation and an answer, not with hesitation but with certainty. He did not speak as one who wondered whether his act might be wrong, but as one asking, "Who are you?"

Another delegation set out: Parthasarathi, editor of *Tamil Nadu*; Kanchi Manimozhiyar, editor of *Porval*; Kannabiran, organiser of the Madras Dravidar Kazhagam; and C. V. Rajan, one of the commanders of the Anti-Hindi Volunteer Corps.

Periyar told them that he would not change his decision and that after the people shouted for four days they would subside by themselves. Meanwhile many letters were sent to him. None of this helped to stop the marriage arrangements.

Believing that it was proper to express their views collectively, the Madras District Dravidar Kazhagam met at Periyar's own residence on 27 June 1949. The following is the report.

After all this, Periyar issued a statement explaining the marriage arrangement that had startled us, made us sob and provoked the town's mockery. That explanation clearly shows that the marriage now contemplated is not an act that ends with itself, but a warning of the greater danger and greater disgrace to Periyar that are to follow.

Maniammai is to be not merely Periyar's wife; she is to acquire the qualification to lead the movement. A new Annie Besant is apparently setting forth.

Periyar, we ask whether it is right to enter so unjust, anti-principled and mismatched a marriage, one at which civilised people will laugh.

His statement replies, in effect: "Go away! Am I merely marrying? I am handing the movement itself to Maniammai. I am marrying precisely in order to hand it over to her."

It is like boldly telling a distraught woman who asks, "Alas, why are you killing my husband?": "Do you think I kill him without a reason? I do it in order to possess you." In the same manner, he declares: "I have resolved somehow to make her my legal 'heir,' so that after me Maniammai may receive the responsibility and opportunity to conduct the movement, its property and its affairs."

What is to occur is not only a mismatched marriage but a new coronation.

One wrong is offered as the reason for another. We are not merely to endure the dishonour and blame caused by a mismatched marriage. In future we must also be prepared to work beneath Maniammai, who will become our "leader" through this improper method.

There are people in the movement who assert many claims of right:

"I have been in the movement since the Chengalpattu Self-Respect Conference."

"I have undertaken movement work since the early days of *Kudi Arasu*."

"I stood alone in my town and faced the hostility of the whole place."

"Look at this scar; I received it in the disturbance at such-and-such a town."

"I campaign six days a week."

"I am an expert at writing rebuttals to opposition newspapers."

There are people who speak of such claims. There are those who have worked for a long time and those who have worked ably. There are those who served at the cost of estrangement from their families and those who lost property and comfort. Some lost an eye or a hand.

None of them could become the heir. None was given the opportunity to conduct the movement. Maniammai has received it.

By what method? By the method of becoming Periyar's consort.

With what qualification?

Periyar says in his statement: "Maniammai, whom I have known for five or six years, in whom I have confidence, and who has acted with concern and care for my welfare."

This, apparently, is the reason for the mismatched marriage and for the intolerable arrangement for a new coronation through it.

He has known the lady for five or six years. That acquaintance has revealed to him her extraordinary intellectual ability. He has acquired sufficient confidence in her to entrust an entire great movement to her.

Like Achariar, the lady too is indeed fortunate.

Otherwise, would Periyar say: "This Maniammai has the qualification to conduct the movement properly for my lifetime and, as far as possible, after my lifetime"?

"She has studied up to the tenth standard," Periyar is said to have told the second delegation.

The genuine attachment and concern for his welfare and the movement's welfare that none of those who have laboured and endured blows and kicks from enemies since the movement consisted of twenty or thirty people possesses, this lady possesses. Our leader discovered this subtle truth during the five or six years in which he knew her.

Forgetting principle and even entering a mismatched marriage, he makes that lady our "leader." The movement supposedly needs an heir.

Why does the leader of a rationalist movement suffer the anxiety appropriate to the Nizam of Hyderabad or to heads of religious monasteries?

Why a hereditary method? Who is making this arrangement? To what age does this method belong?

Is appointing an "heir" to a movement compatible with democracy? Can it succeed in practice?

Are the Dravidar Kazhagam and the property said to belong to it a jungle kingdom to be transferred to another person by hereditary succession?

Was it for this that *Viduthalai* thundered, "Throw in your silver coins and see the work proceed"?

If the person presently leading a movement believes that anyone within it is a traitor or malefactor, and if he can prove it, the proper course is to expel such people and allow those with genuine attachment to and influence within the movement to conduct it after him. Is he instead to speak of hereditary entitlement and say, "I hand it to my heir"? Is he to seek a new lineage for that purpose?

The leader's explanation of the nineteenth shows how deeply conceived his arrangement is. He says: "I am greatly concerned that I must appoint someone as my heir and make arrangements through that person before I go."

This explains what the "heir" he appoints is for. Only Maniammai possesses the qualification to become that heir. To make her his heir, the leader considers marrying her more suitable than any other arrangement.

The statement comes in the manner of: "Accept this, or I will destroy you." Many more statements may follow.

"I have no confidence in anyone in the movement. I see no one worthy of me or my confidence. I do not think anyone else has the qualification and ability to conduct the movement."

Although Periyar had expressed these views many times before, comrades thought: "He says this because he is angry with someone; he says it to frighten him; he is speaking in anger." Now he has made it clear.

He says categorically: "I have no confidence in any of you. More than that, I have confidence in Maniammai alone. Therefore I make her my 'heir' by registering her as my wife."

By means of his propagandist skill, Periyar may now even begin explaining this as "a revolutionary plan introduced in the name of marriage to make the party permanent and crush traitors."

But we must ask ourselves what use there is in working under the leadership of one whose confidence we cannot earn.

How can we trust one who does not trust us? Why should we trust him?

How can we trust, to the extent of accepting her as leader merely because he alone trusts her, the leader who says he cannot trust us? Why should we?

The confidence we had until now concerning the lady has itself been destroyed. Until now we trusted that she was a daughter attending to the father of the Dravidars. No, the registered marriage tells us that in law she cannot be called a daughter and must be called a wife. In this situation Periyar's statement gave those already weeping over the mismatched marriage still another pain.

Does it not tell us: "Do not remain with one who has no confidence in you. Do not work under his leadership. Run, run"?

When we heard of the mismatched marriage, we were ashamed. Periyar's statement in answer to it caused us pain.

Now we are being driven out: from the fortress we built and from the field in which we laboured. The father drives away his children after finding a new heir.

We have become helpless and bewildered, deprived of the fruits of our labour. Seeing the young woman who entered the household attempt to wield the sceptre, we groan, shrink and cry aloud as we leave our own home.

From inside, he issues an order: "This lady is my wife and your leader. Do you wish to stay or not?"

### Periyar's Explanation Is Unsatisfactory: Resolution at the Madras District Meeting

Many members of the Madras District Dravidar Kazhagam executive and the movement leaders named below met at 8:30 p.m. on 28 June 1949. Each separately expressed his view and firm resolve that Periyar's statement about his marriage published in *Viduthalai* on 28 June 1949 was in no respect satisfactory, and that they would stand against and take appropriate action regarding any further measures in the matter.

It was further resolved that if the leader convened a meeting of leading members or a committee meeting to take vigorous action over the anti-Hindi issue, they should inform him that they could not cooperate until he cancelled his arrangements concerning both his marriage and the movement.

Those who attended and signed the meeting were:

C. T. T. Arasu, Kanchi Manimozhiyar, Ma. Ilanchezhiyan, A. S. Venu, R. S. Mani, C. Thangarasu, Su. Balasundaram, V. Sankaranarayanan, Pa. Govindarasan, M. R. Pandian, P. V. Muthusami, S. P. T. Dravida Mani, K. Govindasami, Se. Devasahayam, R. Pandian, Ve. Tha. Periyasami, R. Angamuthu, A. Jayaraman, S. Kanniyappan, M. Dharuman, Mu. Madurai, C. V. Rasan, Perumal, R. Subbarayalu, Ki. Bhaktavatsalam, A. S. Raman, Ka. Sokkalingam, K. Kannabiran, So. A. A. Balraji, A. Sankaran, C. Ganesan, K. Sundaramurthi, N. V. Natarajan and Ma. Su. Muthu.

We declare that this marriage is a mismatched marriage. Since both his entering it and the new arrangement he intends thereby to make for the party cause us to lose our confidence in him, so long as the Kazhagam remains under his leadership we cannot work within it or in its name. Even if plans are devised to soothe young hearts through direct action such as anti-Hindi agitation, picketing Marwari shops and defying Section 144, we cannot participate in them. We inform comrades of the Kazhagam and the public accordingly.

This marriage humiliates us, brings our principles into disrepute, and places an indelible stain upon the movement.

We consider Periyar's excuse that this is not a marriage but merely an arrangement made in the name of marriage to be a mockery of our reason.

We believe the arrangement he says he will make for the party sounds the death knell of democracy and our self-respect.

We therefore request leave to state that until he abandons these arrangements, we will not work in the Kazhagam under his leadership.

We ask comrades in the movement to think and express their respective views.

Before attempting to remove his leadership or undertaking work of another kind, we ask them first to announce that, as a sign of condemnation of Periyar's arrangement, they are standing apart.

Those who agree should write to the journal *Dravida Nadu*.

Make it known that thousands refuse to accept this arrangement.

Tell the world that if a leader acts contrary to principle, you will not follow even him. Preserve your self-respect.

Answer the mockery of our opponents. Make it known. Show them that we have declared we will not accept his leadership if the leader does not abandon this improper arrangement.

Dispel the belief that anything he does will be digested.

Through your condemnation, show the world that you belong to the true Self-Respect Movement.

Do not worry about what to do if, even after the condemnation, the leader remains obstinate.

Five lakh rupees and several years of labour will go to the lady who entered midway. That is no great loss. Our honour will remain, and we can conduct the movement in a more substantial manner than this.

The movement is our labour; it is not the leader's property. His saying that it is his movement and that he will give it to Maniammai is one of the movement's waste products.

The movement exists in our sweat, tears and labour. It will not go anywhere.

Let us show the world that we are not partners in this injustice. Let what has been lost go. With what remains, and with the strength left after our labour, we can build the Self-Respect Movement.

Dravidar comrades, do not waver. Tell the leader: "We will not accept your arrangement; we condemn it. As a sign of condemnation, we hereby suspend our work with you and stand apart."

If our tears, condemnation and resolve to stand apart change his heart, if he abandons this unjust arrangement, we shall ask his forgiveness, stand once more at his flower-like feet and resume our work.

Otherwise, on one side will stand he, Maniammai, five lakh rupees and this heir.

On the other will stand we, our pure hearts and our capacity for labour. Let there be not the slightest doubt about it.

This is the greatest trial of our lifetime. The country's future depends upon the purity and firmness we display in it.

Think. Do not lose heart. Decide. Do not be hasty. Fear not. Victory is ours.

Affectionately,

**C. N. Annadurai**

## Translator's Notes

The article repeatedly uses the legal and political term `பாத்தியதை`, here translated as a "claim of right" or "hereditary entitlement" according to context.

The printed transliteration `அனிபெசண்ட்` refers to Annie Besant. It has been identified in translation but retained verbatim in the Tamil source.

The phrase `தும்பு அறுந்ததாக` literally evokes a severed tether or tie and is immediately glossed by the author as `அதாவது விதவையாக` ("that is, a widow").

The source names many movement members using Tamil initials. Their names are transliterated conservatively; initials and uncertain historical spellings have not been expanded without documentary support.

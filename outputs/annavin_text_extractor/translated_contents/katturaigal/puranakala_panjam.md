## English Translation

The many kinds of crises and hardships caused by the Great War combined to create a food famine in our country and brought into existence a system of food rationing that had never before prevailed here. Many expected this deficiency to disappear when the war ended. Contrary to expectation, however, it is precisely now, when the clouds of war have dispersed and the light of peace prevails, or is about to prevail, that the food famine has spread through the country with its full force and made the entire world act with agitation over it. We see it being discussed everywhere.

The reason why the famine in our country, and especially in the Madras Presidency, is now worse than before the war ended is the devastation caused by the cyclone and the failure of the rains in this Presidency. The people, caught in the tempest of war and tormented for want of sufficient food, had longingly asked, "When will this wicked war end? When shall we obtain enough food for our hunger?" At that very time nature unleashed her own storm, withheld the rain, and destroyed the yield. The heavenly benefactors, the gods who are said to be able to calm storms, cause rain, make fields fertile, and provide whatever is needed whenever it is needed, remained idle. Even those called "friends in danger" and "protectors of the destitute" allowed their millions of devotees to undergo untold suffering from pitiable hunger. Even while they themselves lacked enough food, the hosts of devotees continued, and still continue, to perform without any reduction all the rites owed to their gods. Yet "those gods" showed not the slightest compassion toward "these devotees." Never mind their failure to show compassion; they have even shut the door against the seasonal rain! Why? Are there no devotees worthy of the gods, or no gods worthy of their devotees? We are not examining here which of these is true; nor is that our task.

We uphold human knowledge and research capable of bringing rain when required and averting it when it is not required, and we desire that such knowledge and research spread throughout the world so that nations and peoples may prosper. More than merely holding that faith and desire, we believe that nothing is impossible through effort. Those who think the earth can be made prosperous by divine grace may engage in that "noble" work; we do not stand in their way. But we cannot refrain from noting one thing here.

Famine is not a novelty that has arisen only now. It has occurred many times before. Famine occurred in our country even in an age when gracious miracle-workers supposedly accomplished whatever they thought of at the very instant they conceived it: they turned a bone into a woman, raised the dead, made the mute speak, changed a male palmyra into a female one, turned poison into nectar, changed a fox into a horse, restored a child swallowed by a crocodile, made a palm leaf travel upstream, drew a dry leaf green from the fire, made a stone float upon the sea, cooled a burning lime kiln, turned elephants and horses into aerial vehicles (aeroplanes), opened and closed temple doors, and instantly restored an abraded elbow and shorn hair. A famine occurred even while such gracious ones walked the earth alive.

What kind of famine was it? The very cause now assigned to the present famine was assigned to that ancient one too. Sekkizhar says:

> "The sky above the earth proved false and the rivers failed;
> living beings grew dim-eyed and their food changed...
> seeing a great and surpassing hunger arise in the world..."

That is, rain failed, the Kaveri consequently had no water, cultivation yielded nothing, and the people fell victim to unbearable hunger. The Periyapuranam says this famine occurred while Appar and Sambandar, travelling together from town to town, were showering grace. It arose while the "good devotees," blessed by the Lord to remove evil and perform good, traversed the country. Sambandar and Appar saw this famine and wished to end it. Why did that desire arise in them? Was it because they saw the pitiable condition of the people wasting from hunger? No. The famine that afflicted everyone afflicted the Lord's devotees too.

Wishing to remove the hunger of their followers, Appar and Sambandar supposedly fell into a "blissful" sleep while meditating upon the divine parents. The Lord who shares his form with Ambikai appeared in their dream and commanded: "Though this is a change of season, hunger will not affect you in the least. We cannot bear to see the devotees surrounding you grow weak from hunger. Until the famine ends, we shall place one gold coin each day upon the lower and upper pedestals of the temple. Take one apiece and remove the hunger that has befallen the devotees." Gold coins accordingly appeared in the temple. Rice was bought with them and food was served to the devotees. The coins continued to appear upon the temple pedestals until the famine ended; when it ceased, Siva stopped issuing them. This incident appears in the history of Tirugnana Sambandar in the Periyapuranam.

We mention it here because, although the causes of today's famine and that earlier famine were the same, scholars who discover marvels in the Puranas ought to compare the method used then by the devotees with that now used by learned people to end famine. It must therefore be examined.

Appar and Sambandar had travelled through many regions with their groups of followers and reached the town called Tiruvalimilalai when famine struck the land. They gave not the slightest thought to the suffering of the country's people. Nor did their leader Siva care even a little about it.

Sekkizhar sings that Siva said:

> "Though the fierce hunger born of the evil brought by worldly change
> will not reach Uma and you, we have granted a coin every day
> to remove the distress of those beside you who follow the way of Siva..."

In other words, Siva said only: "Although this cruel hunger, caused by a seasonal change occurring in accordance with worldly nature, will not afflict you two, Appar and Sambandar, we shall give a gold coin daily to remove the hunger suffered by the Saivite devotees who came with you." He did not graciously enable all the people distressed by the famine to relieve their hunger with the aid of his gold coins. He defined and divided them bluntly: "only those beside you who follow the way of Siva." Yet the famine of that day did not affect only the crowd of followers who accompanied Appar and Sambandar.

Sekkizhar himself says, "Living beings grew dim-eyed and their food changed..." That is, all living beings had become dim-eyed from hunger. Even so, the compassionate three-eyed benefactor, who is said to regard all life as one, felt no pity for those suffering creatures.

Why did the Siva described here behave this way? I wondered whether there might perhaps be other gods like Siva, and whether Siva therefore arranged food only for his own Saivite devotees, supposing that the other gods would feed their respective followers. Seeking any evidence for this, I continued reading the Periyapuranam. In the very next verse, verse 563 following verse 562 in Sambandar's history, I found:

> "Becoming sky and earth and fire,
> becoming wind, the two lights, and water,
> becoming flesh, life, and consciousness,
> becoming all the worlds,
> and beyond the worlds,
> he stands in an imperishable form..."

After reading this I understood nothing at all. Is Siva not spoken of as all worlds and as the father of every living being? Yet this god endowed with such a "universal nature" is said to have separated only some of his children, awarded them the title "Saivite devotees," and supplied gold coins solely to cure their hunger. The Purana says nothing about how, or by whom, the hunger of people and other beings outside that title was removed. Nor can we assume everyone then was a Saivite devotee and that everyone's hunger was therefore relieved by the coins, for the Purana itself says other people existed. How, in these circumstances, can the Siva described here be accepted as the one supreme and universal God? If anyone asks, eloquent men come forward shamelessly to call the questioner an atheist until their tongues and mouths stink.

Next, on seeing the unbearable hunger of the devotees and deciding somehow to remove it, Appar and Sambandar are said to have enjoyed a blissful sleep while thinking of the Lord. Delighted by that blissful sleep, the Lord appeared in their dreams and went away after granting gold coins. The devotees were suffering uncontrollable hunger, while those who meant to relieve it enjoyed boundless blissful sleep! Seeing people in torment from hunger, they should have been unable to sleep from excessive anxiety, their hearts distraught and unable to find a course of action, thinking, "Nothing more can be done by my action; now I realise, O God, that all is your action." Instead, we are told they slept blissfully and that the Lord, greatly delighted by it, gave them gold coins. We do not know what principle this tale follows.

To end today's famine, our leaders have gone to foreign countries such as America. Their moving appeal can soften even the stoniest hearts and bring listeners to the point of offering whatever assistance they can. Yet the request of those who sought to end the ancient famine was supposedly fulfilled very easily, through blissful sleep and without any trouble. Although "our ancestors" demonstrated so accomplished a method of ending famine, why do our leaders neglect it, travel through many lands, deliver persuasive speeches, and exhaust themselves?

Even if those now abroad seeking food do not know this "truth," scholars who have read the Periyapuranam over and over six times might have stopped them, taught them this subtle technique, and told them to follow it. If the Puranic scholars think the leaders cannot do so because they lack true devotion, then those scholars, as "true lovers of God," could have intercepted them and offered reassurance: "Why should you suffer? We who have received the grace of the poison-throated Lord are here to end the famine; fear not." By thinking of the Lord and enjoying a blissful sleep, they might then have obtained at least silver coins, if not gold, and ended the famine. Did they come forward? No. Can they do it? They cannot.

Why? The purpose behind composing the Puranas was not to remove the people's real and necessary wants and guide them along a good path. They were created so that a few could use the Puranas as instruments for a comfortable life without physical labour. The Periyapuranam itself supplies the evidence: it says that Appar and Sambandar used the gold they received to feed only the devotees attached to them. There is no need to seek evidence elsewhere. Even today religious books such as the Periyapuranam serve the labour-free comfort of a select few. The purpose upon which those books were founded continues to prosper.

Famine prevails, and our people, anxious to find some remedy for the pitiable condition of those wavering for want of food, travel abroad and undergo hardship. Even in this situation, some use as their instrument the Periyapuranam, which claims that blissful sleep brought gold coins and ended devotees' hunger. Instead of offering whatever help they can toward the people's present need, or at least remaining quiet if they lack the ability, they seek only their own comfort. Are such persons to be counted among humankind, or placed in some other class?

In America, Sir Ramasamy is explaining the cruelty of the famine here and appealing for support, warning that many hundreds of thousands will starve to death unless the required food is supplied. At this same time, near Kanchipuram, four miles away at a place called Kuram, an old Siva temple lies in ruins. It was constructed of polished black stones cut into small pieces the size of bricks. A Puranic poet appeals to the public to renovate it, claiming that allowing such a temple to perish would bring great danger upon the country. The very poet who believes the Puranic story that the Supreme Being obtained gold coins to end the people's hunger now asks the people for gold to build a temple for that same Supreme Being.

What a spectacle! The Lord who could not bear the people's hunger and supposedly scooped up all his wealth in gold coins, leaving himself destitute, now lacks the means even to repair his ruined dwelling and must petition the public through his poet-devotee. See how very "pitiable" this is!

Consider the conditions when Appar and Sambandar supposedly received gold coins from the Supreme Being to end the famine. The account says there was no rain; therefore the Kaveri had no water, the fields produced no crop, and hunger struck the people. Yet the moment the gold coins appeared, all the rice and other food they required became plentiful in that very town. As soon as the Saivite devotees offered the coins, the merchants reportedly declared, "We shall give whatever you require," and supplied food generously.

Is it not a wonder beyond wonders to say that, in the very country where famine arose because rain failed, the Kaveri ran dry, and cultivation ceased, food nevertheless became abundant as soon as money was offered? Nor does the account say food was imported from lands without famine. Even if we assume that to be true, why had the townspeople remained idle until Appar and Sambandar arrived, slept blissfully, received the Lord's gold, and relieved the famine? If they had the facility to import food from places without famine, could they not have cured their hunger before the saints came? Did no one in the town have money? If truly they had none, how could its merchants have imported food? Nor does the story say Appar and Sambandar used the Lord's coins to import provisions. The Purana says the Lord supplied only enough gold each day for that day's food.

There must therefore have been a place close enough to send that day's coins and receive that day's food, with crops untouched by famine. If so, how could the famine caused by lack of rain and water in the Kaveri have affected Tiruvalimilalai alone? A food famine caused by the Kaveri's failure cannot strike that town alone; it must affect every place irrigated by the river. Thus it is not credible that provisions were brought from neighbouring towns. Moreover, in that age there was no rapid transport capable of bringing food daily from distant places beyond the Kaveri's irrigation region.

What, then, is the novelty inserted into the Periyapuranam through this famine episode? Can there be any truth in it other than this: to revive a declining Saivism, supernatural powers were attributed to Saivite teachers such as Appar and Sambandar, such marvels were introduced into the Puranas, and the people were made to believe them? Readers must reflect upon this.

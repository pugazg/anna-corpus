## English Translation

The great man arrived!

He granted *darshan*!

He conducted prayer!

He asked them to sing *bhajans*!

He received offerings!

They worshipped with folded hands!

They fell at his feet and bowed!

They performed *arati*!

They held a procession with elephants, horses and camels in front!

Hundreds of thousands waited for him.

They garlanded him and tied a ceremonial turban!

They accorded every temple honour!

He stood in the sanctum for an hour and meditated!

He was transported by devotion!

Radiance shone from his face!

A grandeur sounded in his voice!

He raised his hand, and everyone who saw him seemed bound by magical power!

The whole town gathered to see that noble man!

The entire country rejoiced in beholding him!

If these sentences were recited without attaching the date 1946, no one would need to wonder for whom the celebration was held. One could say without hesitation that some great saint, incarnate being, religious preceptor, miracle-worker or blessed man had arrived: perhaps Ramadas, Kabir Das, Ramanuja or Chaitanya. Such pomp must have been for one of them, because this is *Bharatavarsha*, with its hereditary custom of welcoming holy men and ascetics.

Yet all these ceremonies were performed for Gandhi, described as the one who sowed the seed of the Indian subcontinent's freedom struggle, opened its path or formed its lifeblood. Congress devotees may believe and rejoice that this gives national strength. But anyone who reflects after the festival must ask whether it belongs on the credit side or the debit side. Clear-minded people will enter it as expenditure, not income. Why?

Gandhi accepted Congress leadership and came forward to guide the people along the road to freedom. Leaving aside whether his various struggles were strategically proper, he conducted movements capable of captivating the public, trained the people and awakened desire for self-rule. When it was said that many lakhs were ready to accept his leadership and obey his command, one could declare that a new light had appeared in India's dark political sky, that its path was becoming clear and that the national struggle had gained new power. This could be entered as income.

One could proudly tell other nations: “Look how much greater our leader Gandhi's influence is than that of your public heroes.” One could compare him with Garibaldi, who liberated Italy; Napoleon, who laboured to raise France; and Abraham Lincoln, America's eminent leader. India too had a Gandhi whose influence was immense. In this way his influence became a great asset entered on the credit side of the national ledger.

But today? A merchant may declare an income of one lakh and then list eighty thousand in income tax, other taxes and fines for violating control rules. Likewise, Gandhi's influence once harnessed popular power to the freedom struggle and stood as income in the national ledger, but must now be written as expenditure. Political leaders have praised a leader once considered fit for politics and liberation as “Mahatma,” made him the subject of devotional singing, and transferred his name from the roll of political leaders and freedom fighters to the list of religious heads, preceptors and Mahatmas. They have written it off as bad debt. This cannot be forgotten.

Elsewhere we have read of holy men and ascetics abandoning saffron robes and water pots to join national liberation struggles. Here, ritual orthodoxy, seeking relief from its own famine, drew to itself the man who had come forward to conduct the struggle.

It resembles the tale of a prince who set out to marry a princess but became infatuated with the maid carrying her letter. “We shall read the letter later; graceful woman, give me a kiss,” he pleaded. The maid, delighted that she had received the embrace intended for the royal maiden and imagining it might make her a princess, offered herself as the feast. So too did ritual orthodoxy draw to itself the Gandhi whom nationalism had sought and obtained.

Suppose Gandhi had not become a “Mahatma” and there had been no need to adorn his worship with *darshan*, prayer, *bhajan*, ecstasy and temple honours. Suppose instead he had remained the leader of national liberation, with no other party divided from him, able to clarify every doubt through speech, bring everyone beneath one flag and create a united front. Comrades who took part in the *bhajan* should consider what a magnificent gain that would have been for the freedom struggle.

What is the position now? Ritual orthodoxy has snatched away the title of incomparable commander of the liberation army from the man who awakened the people's thirst for freedom. Before your hearts could perceive the trick played before your very eyes, it moved the name engraved upon the roll of freedom fighters onto its own list.

Great man, Mahatma, radiance, worship, ecstasy, *bhajan*, cymbals, full ritual vessel, *darshan*, sacrifice, offering and service at the feet: in what context and for whom are these words customarily used? In histories of freedom fighters, or stories of religious teachers? They belong to religious stories, where such events occur.

If you say it is delightful that Gandhi enjoys the same greatness, listen. What we desperately sought here was a leader to wage the struggle to redeem this country, not another religious teacher. By the Lord's grace, we have never suffered a shortage in that field. The country already supports any number of Mahatmas, great men and maharishis. The arrival of one more is neither astonishing nor something that needed to be welcomed.

Mother India is not barren; she is accustomed to repeatedly bearing Mahatmas of this kind. At the very moment we longed to hear that a mighty hero had arisen from her precious womb, united the people and waged the freedom struggle, you took the very Gandhi whom you had identified as that hero, called him Mahatma and visible God, and entered him as expenditure in the national ledger. You did not realize that the nation and seekers of freedom suffered the loss while ritual orthodoxy received the profit.

Other countries welcome the fact that ritual orthodoxy has drawn him in and that Congress not only permits but celebrates it. Those strategic nations think the greater his “spiritual grandeur,” the better for them.

When Congress says, “Why not grant the self-rule Gandhi demands? Do you not know his tremendous influence? Fifteen thousand gathered before dawn at Villupuram; there was a vast crowd at Madurai; his prayer meeting was a sea of heads,” foreigners will reply: “True, he has tremendous influence. He is a Mahatma, and people worship Mahatmas in that fashion, especially Indians with their deep religious faith. We too have seen and photographed them celebrating Gandhi as Mahatma and becoming absorbed in his *bhajan*. Yet devotees themselves cannot carry out many things Mahatmas teach. Is self-rule alone an exception? People respect him in matters of the spirit, but political disputes remain and cannot be resolved through Mahatmas. They guide the soul; their political opinions are generally impracticable.”

However one looks at it, a credit has become a debit.

Why does ritual orthodoxy rejoice? In the twentieth century, leaders with a militant determination to break antiquity appeared everywhere. Whichever way orthodox authorities turned, they heard the death bell sounding for their rule. They were disturbed to see common sense draw people toward rational inquiry. They lamented as radio replaced the sound of the Vedas, parks appeared where temples had dominated, hospitals where ashrams had stood, and playing fields where *gurukulas* had been. Seeing their livelihood shrink, they grieved.

The practice of *sati* was stopped, the Sarda Act was born, and people spoke of equality between women and men. Orthodoxy concluded its age was over and began to sob. At that moment Gandhi arrived and granted it refuge. Orthodoxy took possession of the man who came to give refuge. He said he would reform it; it admitted him into its sacred company and placed a mark upon his forehead.

Nationalist comrades should ask, at least after memories of the festival have faded, whether this is a cause for satisfaction. Young people, and especially students, must think.

“All religions are one.

“Hari and Hara are one.

“Truth alone triumphs.

“Do not regard distinctions of caste, religion or clan.

“Sing of the Supreme with devotion.

“God dwells within you.

“He is everywhere and in everything.

“Worship him wholeheartedly.

“Only one with a stainless mind will see God.

“The Lord resides in the temple of the heart.”

Did these and similar teachings arise today or yesterday? Was there ever a great saint who did not say them? Is the saying that saints of universal wisdom recognize no evil distinction of caste, religion, high or low a novelty? No. These are the teachings left by saints who appeared here, verses still sung with drums and cymbals in devotional halls.

Having sung those hymns and built devotional halls, did we redirect society according to the teaching embedded in them? Had we done so, the number of preachers here would have declined. In a country that has merely used instruction to strengthen loud voices, what new thing will Gandhi accomplish by preaching?

We ask those who say, “He may say anything, sir; the moment we stand before him our bodies thrill.” Their bodies have thrilled in the same way countless times. Did harmony blossom in the town? It did not. What he says is not new, nor are you unfamiliar with hearing such instruction. What, then, do his worshippers expect to happen now?

They may answer that his demand to abolish untouchability is new, revolutionary and reformist. But even this cannot be said. Centuries ago the Alvars and Nayanmars proclaimed that even outcastes who flayed cattle and ate the flesh were gods worthy of worship if they worshipped their Lord. Such sayings demonstrated the speaker's generosity, universal wisdom and maturity; they did not abolish untouchability.

Why should Gandhi's campaign produce a different result? The Alvars and Nayanmars could only appeal to morality and love in supporting the untouchables. Gandhi can add reasons clearly intelligible even to calculating orthodox people: statistics are now breaking temple locks.

Out of a population of forty crores, ten crores are Muslims. Most descended from the Hindus of old *Bharatavarsha*. Had they not become Muslims, could orthodoxy's income have fallen? Ten crores now boycott death rites, ancestral ceremonies, marriage fees and temple consecration donations. They once were Hindus but entered another faith because you did not abandon untouchability. Likewise many lakhs became Christians, and revenue gradually fell.

More than six crores are Adi Dravidars. If they continue to be excluded as untouchables, they may change religion and declare that they do not want Hinduism. Thus ten crores of Muslims, six crores of Adi Dravidars and four crores of Dravidians may determine they are not Hindus. Gandhi asks with figures: if twenty crores depart, what will become of you? The Alvars did not possess this advantage in their day.

When Gandhi cites statistics, points to approaching danger and asks for untouchability to end, this is the act of a protector of Hinduism. How can it be called novelty or revolution?

Instruction can never become the path of revolution. Revolution would not consist of advising caste Hindus to abandon untouchability. To be revolutionary, the speech must address those whom the wicked call untouchable: “Do not accept the condition assigned to you. Demand and obtain your rights. If they are refused, do not bow your heads.”

Gandhi instead tells caste Hindus: “Hinduism is decaying; remove untouchability to save it.” He advises the untouchables: “Do not forget your duties; learn to remain clean.”

A revolutionary would say: “You have laboured for the worthless until your bodies are transformed. The cruelty that keeps you in this condition is the cause of your living amid filth. From ancient times they arranged for you to wallow in it. The work assigned to you, the wages you receive and the segregated settlement allotted to you prevent you from obtaining civilization, a good life, cleanliness or health. Having reduced you to this condition, they rebuke you for being unclean. This is a spear thrust into a wound. Do not lose strength and languish from the pain. Awaken; demand and obtain your rights.”

## English Translation

The N.G.O. association prays to God to save its members from the cruelty of poverty and the terror of starvation!

They had pinned cards bearing these words to their shirts.

The sun was hot. Labour had left them tired and hungry. They held packets of food and vessels carrying meals.

They did not open the packets. They did not uncover the vessels containing food.

They all gathered together and prayed to God.

More than two hundred N.G.O.s held a prayer meeting at lunchtime on the fourteenth, in the government building at Chepauk.

At the command of their central committee, they appealed to the Lord. And they have adopted this prayer as their final measure.

They have discovered prayer to be the last weapon in their struggle for rights. Their final effort is prayer. That itself is curious!

The curiosity grows when one hears what they prayed for.

They suffer in poverty and are afflicted by hunger. Yet they did not ask the Lord to remove their poverty and grant them a life worth living. They did not seek a boon that would end their hunger and show them the way to a full meal.

They are the supplicants, and the grievances to be removed surround them. Even so, those N.G.O.s did not plead with God, "Resolve our grievances."

They began by calling upon the Lord and ended by saying, "We beseech you." But in the boon requested between those words, they did not ask God to do anything directly for them.

The very form of the prayer is astonishing. More astonishing still is the essence of the boon they requested in it.

This was their association's first prayer meeting, but it was the last means they had adopted to reduce, or rather remove, their grievances.

Even in this final effort they could not act directly. Instead, they offered guidance to God!

"Lord, we fall at your feet and beseech you: give abundant funds to those who have come to govern us."

That was the boon they sought. If this boon were granted, it seems, their grievances would disappear!

They sought it because they believed that a shortage of money was why the government would not turn its attention towards the N.G.O.s.

But they know that those who came to rule are short not merely of money, but also of compassion!

If money arrives and their hearts still do not soften, it appears another prayer meeting will be necessary!

What can they do? What other course is open to them?

They pray, and even while praying they must make their request in great fear.

It is they who enable the rulers to parade through the world as rulers. Yet they themselves are trapped and wasting away in poverty; they languish in hunger.

If they open their mouths and ask that their suffering be ended, they are threatened.

The ruler can intimidate them, and they have to submit.

The government is the husband; the official is the wife. This married life is the country's system of government!

The husband threatens and bullies, grooming only himself, putting on a spotless shirt, and displaying his splendour to the town. At home, meanwhile, an empty pot sits on the stove and an emaciated cat beneath it. The wife, her heart aching at the sight, mutters to herself, with rags for silk and tears for ornaments. This is hardly a scene unknown to us.

The N.G.O.s themselves bear witness that there is no difference between that scene and our government.

When the wife describes the state of the household, the husband threatens her, rolls his eyes, and abuses her indiscriminately.

In just the same way, whenever the N.G.O.s have tried to explain their position, the rulers have interrupted and silenced them.

When Omandurar occupied the seat of government, the N.G.O.s followed the path of strike shown by Jagadeesar.

Are not betrayal and defeat two sisters? One after the other, they came to offer condolences.

The government is Satyavan; the N.G.O. is Savitri!

When Yama carried away Satyavan's life, Savitri followed him and would not let him go. She asked for a boon, and he agreed to grant one.

Yama said that he would give her any boon except the life of her husband.

Savitri thought. At last, she asked that she be blessed with a child. In his haste, Yama granted the boon.

Savitri then asked how she could have a child without her husband. Yama was startled awake!

Our gods are accomplished at granting the boon of children. Nor is there any shortage here of deities who grant such boons and then look about furtively!

Savitri did not ask directly for her husband's life. The story says that, knowing a direct request would not succeed, she asked for a child and thereby tried to obtain what she really wanted.

The N.G.O.s bear Savitri's likeness.

After their strike failed, they tried many courses. They met ministers and explained their grievances. Whenever an opportunity arose, they tried to make their case.

They tried petitions, appeals, accounts of their suffering, and a strike.

Now they have stepped down from the strike and sought a prayer hall.

Even in prayer, they did not directly ask that their grievances be removed. They prayed that God grant the government abundant funds, believing that their grievances would thereby disappear, just as Savitri asked for a child and then tried to recover her husband's life.

Thus, even in their prayer, the voice of a faithful wife can be heard.

The N.G.O.s pray that greater wealth be given to their rulers.

The rulers should treat the N.G.O.s' prayer as a national issue and strive to find a sound solution.

*Dravida Nadu*, 19 August 1951.

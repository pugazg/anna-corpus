## English Translation

**1. You speak of Aryans and Dravidians on the basis of cultural history found in old texts. Why do you refuse to accept that racial characteristics have changed today?**

I do not refuse, my friend, nor have I ever done so. No one denies the general truth that peoples who live together in one place for many ages naturally intermingle. Yet even after living and mixing together for so long, we see one group continuing to distinguish its language, conduct, dress, and customs from those of others and to claim them as superior.

It is on account of this attitude that we speak of Aryans and Dravidians. We chiefly consider ways of life and states of mind. Periyar said at Nagercoil near Cape Comorin last week that if a Japanese person declared there were no differences among people by birth and rejected systems founded upon such differences, he too would regard that person as a Dravidian.

To put it briefly and reveal the essential point: those who support varnashrama dharma are Aryans; those who say varnashrama must not exist and equality must prevail are Dravidians. Those who demand privilege for their own group are Aryans; those who demand equality for all are Dravidians. “Dravidian” is a signifying term used to indicate a particular ideal of life.

When we cite old texts as evidence, it does not mean we support the whole culture of that age. We use them to demonstrate the historical fact that Aryans and Dravidians existed as distinct peoples, not to demand that the country once again become exactly as those books describe it. Our purpose is not that the swords, spears, lances, tridents, drums, and battle-axes of that day should rule us today.

We say that most people who lived in this region once lived by the great principle that all people are equal, without caste, the distinctions attached to it, varnashrama, or the group that fostered and profited from it. They were called Dravidians. We say that we need their principle today. There is no reason to mock or recoil from this.

**2. What is the language of Dravida Nadu?**

Dravida Nadu is a federation. It will contain the countries of Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, and Kannada speakers. In each country, its mother tongue will govern. For the federal body, the Dravidar Kazhagam would at that time propose English, a language used by a large part of the world. If a majority of the countries in the federation agreed, it would be adopted. Otherwise, after learning their views, the language receiving the greatest support would be chosen. Under no circumstances would the mother tongue in each country—Tamil Nadu, Andhra, Kerala, or Karnataka—be deprived of the seat of government.

**3. The Tamil Arasu Kazhagam believes that the Dravidar Kazhagam prevents us from attaining Venkata as our boundary. Is that correct?**

No. That organization places the “boundary dispute” at the forefront. Our Kazhagam places at the forefront the task of ending the long-standing affliction of varnashrama. These are not contradictory fronts. There is no need for conflict.

**4. Was it right to give you the title `Thalapathi`, commander?**

No. It is not suitable today. If the title were corrected, I might be called `Thalarpathi`, the weary leader, because both body and mind are weary.

**5. If Dravida Nadu became an independent country, what would be the first reform law to come into force?**

The abolition of caste.

**6. What is the difference in principle between the Communist Party and the Dravidar Kazhagam?**

There is one fundamental difference. The Dravidar Kazhagam has principles of its own. The Communist Party has a policy of embracing and delighting in everything that happens to be gaining influence. Communists say it is enough to attend only to the economic front. The Dravidar Kazhagam holds that social reform is necessary and, even before that, reform in the minds of the people.

**7. Why should the Dravidar Kazhagam not use the tactics employed by other parties to gain public support?**

A tactic brings only temporary victory. It is not enough. The Dravidar Kazhagam therefore does not use it.

Rather than tactics, we may employ sound methods, and that work is already proceeding to some extent. Until now we did not have many journals; now there are numerous ones. Members are enrolling in unprecedented numbers, and branch organizations are being established. Like other parties, we have begun to employ methods capable of bringing victory. We do not use tricks of the kind our friend suggests, nor is there any need.

**8. Which party does the King of Comedy, N. S. Krishnan, belong to?**

He desires that people should attain equality and a just social order and live happily, and he seeks to do whatever he can toward those ends from within his own field. I do not know him to be a member of any political party.

**9. Will the Dravidar Kazhagam contest the Madras Corporation election?**

The Kazhagam does not yet appear to have reached any decision on this.

**10. Did you withdraw from the party, or were you pushed out?**

Circumstances compelled me to withdraw.

**11. You constantly abuse Brahmins, yet the Governor-General himself is now a Brahmin. Are you not ashamed at the sight?**

I shall answer in the manner used in the Legislative Assembly. To the first part of my friend’s question, the answer is “No.” To the second part, the answer is the same.

Now for the explanation. We do not constantly abuse Brahmins, nor is there any need to do so. We condemn a system, not individual persons. Just as the expression “Marwari shop” is used to signify all who charge excessive interest, when we condemn “Brahminism,” not only Brahmins but non-Brahmins who support varnashrama dharma become entitled to that label. It is therefore wrong to think that we abuse Brahmins. It is unjust to begin with that mistaken belief and accuse us on its basis.

Our friend asks whether we are ashamed that Achariyar sits as Governor-General. No. We know that his is the caste granted the titles and offices “representative of God,” “god upon earth,” and “divinity upon earth.” We shall not faint in astonishment to see him seated as Governor-General, nor shall we fear, envy, or feel any urgency to be ashamed.

There are, however, some who ought to be ashamed. Who are they? Those who climbed hills to proclaim, with condemnation and ridicule, that Achariyar was unfit even to be president of the Tamil Nadu Congress. They may well feel ashamed. They said that they had brought down the man they condemned through their valour, courage, and prowess. Yet he has now risen so high as Governor-General that they must request permission merely to obtain his audience. They ought to reflect upon that and be ashamed. We have no such necessity.

23 November 1947

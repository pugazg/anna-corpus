## English Translation

Six children! A loving wife! Ceaseless wandering! He had neither steady employment nor enough income for his family. He wandered about like an *annak kavadi*, a destitute drifter. He knew half-starvation well; borrowing money, worrying over it, and roaming about in search of another loan were all familiar to him.

Had he worked only for the comfort of his own family, there would have been no cause for hardship. He possessed matchless intellect and tireless industry. Had he devoted even one ten-thousandth of these gifts to his family, his life would have been peaceful, and those plated in gold would not have mocked him as an "annak kavadi."

He could have lived comfortably in some office, gladdening his loving wife and keeping his children happy. He watched even fools live that way. He saw simpletons purchase the services of scholars. He knew that people spoke of him contemptuously as a man with no means of making a living.

Nor was he an ordinary man. He possessed an entry ticket sufficient for any number of offices: he had studied at a university and earned a doctorate in philosophy. Yet he had no peaceful life. He did not seek permanent employment; that learned man continued wandering as an annak kavadi. Why?

Had he been concerned only with providing for his family of six children, he need not have wandered. He regarded all the poor people of the world as his family. He believed that life must be made to blossom in every one of those families.

It was this magnificent sense of responsibility that made him an annak kavadi.

But what kind of annak kavadi was he? He exposed in broad daylight all the glittering illusions of civilization seen in golden palaces and showed the poor people of the world a way to live. He gave them that golden volume praised by every nation, the book called *Capital*, and through it helped prepare the birth of a new world.

Yes, he made himself an annak kavadi so that he might instil the ideal that no one on earth should remain an annak kavadi. He was like a mother who takes medicine herself to cure her nursing child's illness.

Say even two words, knowingly or otherwise, about the poor person's hardship or economic distress. At once, someone who dislikes the idea will snap, "Oh! A great Karl Marx, are you?"

Karl Marx, yes, that was the name of this annak kavadi. He was the author of *Capital*, the man who supplied a compass for a new path. His own life was filled with wandering.

Three of his six children died, reducing the burden to that extent.

A newspaper had to be published to secure freedom for the dispossessed. Pamphlets had to be printed from time to time to condemn the conduct of various governments. Money was needed for travel. There was no chest, no strongbox, no inherited wealth accumulated by elders. Yet he could not remain idle.

His ideal stabbed and tormented him: "Work! Work! Work before the fallen people are utterly destroyed!" In this condition, Karl Marx struggled on many occasions. Once, a pamphlet connected with a communist case had to be printed. Money was needed. What could he do? He had a coat kept for use in the rainy season. He pawned it to pay the pamphlet's printing expenses.

Another heart-rending event occurred. One of his children died, and the body had to be buried. There was no money for the expense. It is said that he borrowed two sovereigns.

He received a little money for articles supplied to newspapers. That alone helped keep the family's life flickering, at least. There was no other means. Yet his thoughts remained fixed on what must be done to secure a "good life" for the world's crores of helpless, destitute, and impoverished people.

There might be nothing for that day's kitchen. He, meanwhile, was engaged in investigating how the growth of governmental wealth and the riches produced by commerce all arose through labour; how deceivers gave the labourer a mere drop of the wealth thus created and appropriated the rest; how labour was the world's principal and foundational wealth; and how a few sucked that labour and grew fat while the many laboured and consequently wasted away.

At the very age when the comforts of life were needed for his body to develop strength, poverty battered him for eight years. He endured it, but his health collapsed. By the age of forty, Karl Marx had become an old man. His body had no strength. There was hunger and toil. Lacking money, he bought the cheapest tobacco, and smoking it brought illness. He wasted away.

Marx, broken by the agony of poverty, was helped by his friend Engels, the son of a wealthy man. Engels gave him financial support that brought some peace to his life. But his health, ruined by eight years of hardship, could not sprout and flourish again.

The book *Capital* had plundered all his strength.

The ideas in *Capital*, born from the immeasurable intelligence of that annak kavadi, supplied the fundamental force for many revolutions in Europe. By sowing in people's minds the ideas shown by *Capital*, Lenin was able to conduct the Russian Revolution. Even today, every capitalist government regards that annak kavadi's marvellous volume as a dangerous instrument.

17 November 1946.

## English Translation

Dove! O dove! Poem of new strength!

Are you going to see the leader of the Brahmins?

To you as you depart, I shall entrust a message:

Tell him of the Tamils' courage, which despises

those who make a dead snake dance,

those who stand bearing a worm-eaten manuscript,

and those who, in their arrogance, ruin the condition of others.

People will say that the Brahmin leader is half-witted,

that his ears do not hear;

but that was when he served as a minister!

We know the present position

of the man who now occupies a splendid office

upon the soil of India.

Yet whatever position may be held

by fools who scorn our sweet Tamil tongue,

they are our enemies, and we shall oppose them!

Honourable Tamils will not lose their self-respect

in attachment to office.

However high their station may rise,

if they know how to prepare the way for ours to sink,

we shall not grow weary.

The capable Dravidians will not commit evil;

those who come to commit evil are themselves evil.

This is no tale, my friend, and no dream; it is truth!

Those who raised stones know the people.

Why, then, do you obstruct that path?

"I know everything," says the master.

I say this to you:

will the noble Tamil people agree, like a tortoise,

to withdraw and lose their rights?

Is it right to impose the language of the mean

upon a people who possess a language that raises them high?

Is it proper to imprison our Dravidian leader,

who declared that such imposition is wrong?

He is an elder of seventy years,

one who built an association that spreads like a banyan tree,

one who has said yesterday, today, and always

that if Aryanism rises, knowledge will perish;

one who has laboured so that the whole world may live

without the arrogance of "low" and "high";

one who serves as a staff of support for working people

against the crowd that sucks them dry.

Is there a prison in the country to confine him?

Did you come to rule for that purpose?

Even if you do not want wisdom, have you no love?

Has any government ruled by arrogance ever endured?

Is it not necessary to understand all this?

Did not the aspect assumed by this gathered multitude

give you a withered face?

You came to receive ranks, processions, and greetings;

instead, they gave you their rejection.

Everywhere you looked were black flags,

borne in the hands of valiant youths.

Governor-General! Yes, but what use is it?

Black, black, everywhere black!

Not the roar of ocean waves, but the cry of the young!

Not greetings, but abuse and vows: understand them!

Silk-clad maidens,

Vedic men who have memorised their texts,

and charitable elders who live through his favour:

are they the rare and great assembly of the people?

Look! It flies, soaring over every corner!

Do you not hear the solemn vow? Do you not hear the drum?

Say all this always, my precious dove,

sweet messenger of the Tamil of our delightful tongue.

Once, they told me a tale:

a prince named Sarangadharan

beautifully released a dove,

and it flew to a virtuous, vine-like maiden;

then he too went after it, they said.

They told such a tale, a sound pleasing to the ear!

Today you fly as the Tamils' messenger!

Carry a message of virtue; speak these words.

Having said this, I watched the graceful dove

without so much as blinking my paired eyes.

As though saying, "I shall now bear the message; watch me well,"

and as though the dove had listened to me,

it spread its two wings and flew.

On one wing was written, "Down with Hindi!"

On the other was the noble Dravidian movement's red-and-black flag.

The dove went forth to summon its mate;

then the prince followed it, the story says,

seeking the maiden with teeth like jasmine buds.

Today, you fly as the Tamils' messenger!

Stand firm upon one wing and speak these words,

I said, and watched the beautiful dove

without blinking my two eyes.

As though saying, "I shall now bear the message; watch me well,"

the dove too listened to me,

spread its two wings, and flew:

on one wing, "Down with Hindi!"

*Dravida Nadu*, 5 September 1948.

## Translator's Notes

The poem addresses Governor-General C. Rajagopalachari through a messenger dove and invokes black-flag anti-Hindi protests. Its source scan repeats the dove-and-prince motif near the close; the repetition is retained. One line around “Those who raised stones know the people” remains textually uncertain because the print and OCR are both damaged; it is translated conservatively and logged for review.

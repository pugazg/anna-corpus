## English Translation

The hero Thiru. Vi. Ka., who took part in national struggles and labour uprisings and earned fame as a fearless and powerful man, now does nothing. His health has failed and his inner vigour has declined; the printing press is his home, where there are many printed sheets. Do not imagine, however, that he has become a blind and silent man, subdued his thoughts, and sits idle reflecting that it is difficult simply to remain still. He too has work!

During the Gurukulam struggle he refused to enter the courtyard and ran outside. When the time came to go to prison, although his heart gave room for it, his body did not, and he cried, “O One who has Uma as one half!” Even so, he continues now and then to perform whatever work lies within his power. Up to that point we are satisfied.

Yes, even an aged grandfather whose strength to move has diminished, who lies upon the front platform with eyes closed but without sleeping, mouth shut, ears unhearing and body without oil, taps with his stick and imagines himself keeping perfect vigilance so that thieves do not enter the house. The young man of the house, knowing that this is how the old man thinks, pities his incapacity and suppresses the laughter that rises when he hears him speak.

In the same way, Thiru. Vi. Ka., withdrawn from heroic strongholds and resting in ascetic retreat, occasionally enters into one activity or another and passes the time, preventing himself from being overwhelmed by the longing: “I can no longer be as I was in former days; ruined are all that we learned and heard, saw and acquired.” It is a sight that arouses pity. Yet what can be done? A tree without fruit, a river without water.

A withered crop, a ruined family, an extinguished lamp, a broken painting, a veena with snapped strings, a musician with a choked throat, a song without meaning: these arouse pity. The state of the greatest heroes when they merely eat what is cooked and say whatever enters their minds is similar.

The Kaiser ruled Germany, shook the continent of Europe, and planned all manner of things with the intention of reducing England to dust. He moved through the battlefield like a fierce wind. Defeated, distressed and exiled, he entered another country, built a house, laid out a garden, fenced it, cultivated vegetables within it, and spent his last days there. The eyes that had watched affairs capable of changing Austrian politics, Italy’s future, Poland’s course, Belgium’s administration, Poland’s responsibilities and Russia’s direction, and the hands once used to reduce the crowns of many kings to a fistful of ash, came finally to be occupied with a garden crop: thought upon the plants, hands digging out tubers, and eyes looking at them. What a pitiable spectacle that becomes.

So too with Thiru. Vi. Ka., who once wielded the heroic sword in the Gurukulam struggle, in debate with an empire that conducted baton charges, in bitter battles between workers and owners, and in many similar conflicts. Driven by the thought that doing nothing today would give room for anguish, what work does he now perform? We shall tell you; learn to restrain your laughter before hearing it. His present occupation is attending guru pujas and delivering speeches!

Alas, fearless heart! National hero! Scholar of honeyed Tamil! Is this work worthy of you? Have you not heard people speak of the right person for the work and the right work for the person? Many will yearn to ask him this. What can be done? A man who ought to labour for the public struggle for rights is now useful to the “true devotees” gathered at guru pujas: he explains the hidden meaning in a Nayanmar’s sacred verse about the woman with a melodious girdled waist, and shows how the figure of the dancing deity may be viewed as a portrait of the destruction of imperialism.

It is like a king’s sceptre becoming an exhibit in a provincial museum; like a lame racehorse losing its place at the track and falling into the hands of a jutka driver who bargains, “Ten annas! Eight annas!”; like a fallen dry leaf swept aside; like a woman who has slipped into degradation glancing east and west and sustaining her body with money from those struck by her sidelong eyes.

Guru puja! A lecture on Kungiliya Kalaya Nayanar. A monastery! A speech on the firmness of Tilakavathiyar, her inner firmness rather than her body. Alas! Must a lord endowed with so much knowledge be used for this?

This thought must occur not only to those who know him but to Thiru. Vi. Ka. himself. The force of former days and the misery of today drag one another into combat. Carrying the ruined mind caught in that struggle, he places the burden upon the Supreme, uses the philosophy “life is illusion; becoming earth is certain” as a walking stick, staggers onward, gives Tamil, sees people’s faces glowing with the “fire” it gives, becomes frightened as though they were strangers, and says, “I do not want this street; I shall now go to another.” Thus departs the man of sweet speech.

From time to time, workers too invite the virtuous man who attends guru pujas to one place or another. There he says that the radiance of common ownership can be seen upon the head of the One with an ornamented ear, and that the doctrine of the great hero Lenin can be discerned in Mahesan, who wears the spotless moon and a garland.

Some people saw him in this condition at the guru puja of the Kaivalya Swami monastery in Tuticorin on the last Sunday of the previous month, the 25th. They are said to have been Self-Respecters. They are said to have presented questions and tried to cause confusion. That is the wording of the *Swadesamitran*, and we have repeated it.

Trying to cause confusion? Is it fitting? Is it proper? Let fitness and propriety wait. Was such an act even necessary? Why would anyone need to make a separate attempt to create confusion there? They presented questions. Was that not enough? Why try to create a disturbance? It was unnecessary labour. The question paper alone would have confused the speaker; the chair’s mind and the guru-puja followers’ minds would all have become confused. The moment they saw it, everything there would inevitably become confusion.

No further effort was needed to create disorder. A Self-Respecter does not act without cause; he would not have manufactured confusion, for that is not his occupation. Confusion would have arisen there, and it was not unexpected, but the Self-Respecter would not have caused it because he does not engage in needless work. Nor was confusion something someone brought tied up in a bundle. It was a feature of nature. Give a question paper to one who lacks the ability to answer, and his mind will be confused. Seeing his mind confused, the assembled crowd’s mind too will be confused. Confusion would therefore have arisen, but blaming the Self-Respecters for it is the mischief of small minds.

Now then, what did the confused Thiru. Vi. Ka. say? Did he speak? No, he railed. In what terms? “Oh! What do you take me for? I am a hero capable of the impossible! Am I not fearless? I shall climb to an upstairs storey and leap down! I shall enter the middle and disappear! I am afraid of no one!” He is reported to have hurled words in that fashion.

“I am not going to be afraid when people make noise.” This is Thiru. Vi. Ka.’s jeweled utterance. Very well, great happiness! Does he expect the people of classical Tamil Nadu to give him a painted prize because his mind does not flag at a noise? Anyone would feel like asking why he speaks as if this were some great achievement. To us, however, the anger of an elderly man customarily appears as an incident of comedy.

“Do not think me old. I possess immeasurable strength now. While I was speaking to a gathering of thousands of workers, the government began firing. Some died and many were wounded,” he is said to have declared in heroic excitement. What better example is needed to show that his contact with the public has disappeared so thoroughly that others will not know his glories unless he beats his own drum and tells them? Rationalists should consider whether answering a question by measuring out stories from “grandfather’s time” is a sign of learning or the ascendancy of fear.

We too know the period of that labour uprising. We know that workers were fired upon, as Thiru. Vi. Ka. says, and we know who trembled then. We know the champions who circled the white-robed Sir P. Theagaraya’s great mansion and sought the boon of a shield of mercy so that the government’s arrows would not pierce their bodies. We know the heroic pronouncements of those at the Sadhu Press.

We also know that whenever a heroic flame rose through the country and resolute people sprang up in every direction, leaping into the fire without considering hardship or loss, Thiru. Vi. Ka. alone left that gathering saying, “Sweet, sweet is solitude,” and went away. If one cannot answer, silence is best. Our friend abandoned it and measured out the chaff within him. Why? If he believes that impassioned speech alone can still obstruct the eyes of knowledge, he will certainly be deceived.

This is not an age in which a fox becomes a horse. It is not a time when, with the aid of “Namah Sivaya,” a stone could be made into a boat. The present is the age of the question “Why?” The young world of this age is itself a question mark. If it is tormented with sophistry or angry speech, that questioning young world will appear as a danger sign. We kindly tell him to recognize the time and change his hand. The single shield of being Periyar’s great friend will not always help. Heroic speech and conquering Tamil will not forever lead to victory. Our friend will certainly benefit by understanding the outlook and direction of the country’s people and acting accordingly.

Thiru. Vi. Ka. shouted all manner of things instead of answering the questions. Yet did asking those questions fail to produce benefit? No. They made a Puranic preacher utter modern ideas. It is remarkable not only that the hero unafraid of a gunpoint became terrified at a sheet of paper. He spoke scorching words against the Self-Respecters, and the very next day became a Self-Respecter himself!

How, you ask? On the following day, the 26th, he gave a lecture elsewhere. Here is a passage from it:

“No form has been prescribed for God. People cannot continue saying that their own respective religions alone are great. Taking up religious quarrels is ignorance. The monkey nature in human beings must disappear; religious fanaticism must vanish. Tamils study the *Tolkappiyam*. Are there caste divisions in it? Caste divisions exist in the monasteries operating today. We saw this recently. A college recently founded by one monastery has announced that it will admit only members of a particular caste. These monasteries must be described as a body of capitalists.”

The ideas our friend expressed are the very Self-Respect ideas of the group he had mocked as `Su.Ma.` Without his realizing it, the Self-Respecters introduced their principles into him. The Hara known to our friend is said to possess the nature of forcibly taking devotees into his service. Does this not make clear that Self-Respecters possess the power to stop those who walk wrongly and make them follow another path?

On the first evening came the question paper. On the next evening our friend declared that people must follow the great path of Periyar Ramasamy, must not speak falsehood, and must not be seized by the demon called religion. That is all we require. Such words of wisdom bring more good to the country and to friendship than his impassioned heroic speech.

Our friend had become king of the narrow circle that attends guru pujas, delighted in it, believed that delight permanent, and spat anger on the first evening. On the next evening, in a manner revealing a change of heart and showing the true method of guru puja, he supported Self-Respect principles. He cooled on the second evening the anger he had kindled on the first. Yet we refuse to be wholly satisfied.

Why? He is said to have thundered: “I stood without fear while the police fired. Today I possess immeasurable strength. Do not think me old. I shall not fear!” If it is true that he contains so much courage, why does he avoid intense struggles, visit monasteries that say a pouch of sacred ash and the chanting of *Thevaram* are enough, perform guru pujas, and pass the time?

There is work here for heroes, more and more work worthy of heroes: work to redeem the country; work to cleanse the stains in people’s minds; work to remove the contortions of antiquity; work to attack Brahminism; the struggle for rights. It is not work merely to shout loudly, nor work to proclaim the greatness of a rudraksha garland. Rights! Rights! In this work, where is this great hero, this hero who once stood near the muzzle of a gun? Why is he absent? Why? From whom can an answer be obtained? Why do we not see the hero? Hero, why are you nowhere to be seen? This is Tamil Nadu’s question. What is the answer?

Before our mind’s eye appears Pandit Thiru. Vi. Ka., his bowed head, weakened body and frightened speech showing a man caught in the grip of antiquity and struggling because he cannot leap into modernity. He is a Nayanar fallen into honey. What can he do? A pity!

11 March 1945

## Translator's Notes

The article is a sharply satirical criticism of Thiru. Vi. Kalyanasundaram’s later participation in guru-puja and monastery events, contrasted with his earlier labour and national activism. `சு.ம.` abbreviates `சுயமரியாதை`, the Self-Respect movement; the English expands it where clarity requires. Religious quotations, the “fox becoming a horse” and “stone becoming a boat” allusions, the Sadhu Press, Kaivalya Swami, Kungiliya Kalaya Nayanar and Tilakavathiyar are retained conservatively. “A Nayanar fallen into honey” reproduces the closing pun on Thiru. Vi. Ka.’s sweet Tamil and religious entanglement.

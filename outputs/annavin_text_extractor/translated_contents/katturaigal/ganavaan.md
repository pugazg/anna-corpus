## English Translation

Kamaraj has become a gentleman! Do not imagine that someone conferred such a title upon him or that he acquired an estate to administer. He has dared to fling about words ordinarily used only by lords. Until now he called himself a member of the working class, a servant of the people and a man unconcerned with personal comfort or suffering. Now he has found a new voice and rages like an earthquake. Wrong ideas and mistaken calculations are said to produce irritation. The mask is tearing. His speech carries the odour of lords, the cherished children of arrogance. He presents himself as a “great man.”

This lord has compared the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam to street singers and beggars. Beggars who stand with hands outstretched sing pleasing songs; a crowd gathers to hear them, and some listeners may even throw quarter-anna and half-anna coins. But does the crowd act as the beggars tell it? Likewise, he says, crowds gather to enjoy the speeches of DMK comrades, men and women, but depart when the speech ends and will not vote for the DMK.

So the DMK consists of beggars! The great man speaks from the lord's seat. Even the beggars he uses for mockery possess at least a sweet voice capable of attracting people. We do not know what this lord possesses that can astonish the public. Circumstance has given him a throne. Had the country's situation not assisted him, where and in what condition would this eminent man be?

He remained a Member of Parliament for many years. Tamil Nadu would know it only if someone mentioned it, because in the hall at Delhi he never opened his mouth. Did he ever speak, anywhere outside? He did not ask even a supplementary question. The sole result of his becoming an MP was that he sat silent with his mouth closed like a block of wood.

When anxiety arose over what would happen after the Congress drum was torn, they ran to Achariar to take up the sceptre of this State. They did not need this hero of heroes. Neither the high command nor those here thought of him.

Before he came to office, numerous situations and problems arose. His mind was of no use whatever then.

He became Chief Minister. Those who sang enchanting tunes and the crowd around him began circling him in expectation of their harvest. He believed them. The thought that no one could advise better than he took shape in his heart, perhaps giving him courage to speak in a bullying voice. This man from Virudupatti appears to think such browbeating is the mark of being a “great man.”

However one reaches a seat of political power, whether by skill or through side passages, one needs the generosity and good sense to respect others. If a ruler wishes the people to give him a good name, his only path is self-restraint and humility. Kamaraj cannot claim a distinguished political reputation, a long history as a tiger of administration, or a record of solving problems one by one. He may perhaps be skilful at postponing and evading them. When such a man beats his chest in conceit, people will think he is abandoning the path by which he rose and preparing to fall headlong.

The aloof hardness shown by Achariar wounded Tamil hearts. When Kamaraj was to succeed him, people felt glad because they thought an ordinary man, someone who enjoyed being among common people and shared their joys and sorrows, was coming to rule. They were not enchanted by Kamaraj's sharp intellect or by achievements he had produced. They welcomed him because he had presented himself as an ordinary person from among ordinary people. If that image now gives way to the prideful thought, “I have a commanding voice,” it amounts to striking at the people's forum itself.

Some may think or ask, “Did he ridicule the people? He attacked only the DMK.” But when a Chief Minister, seated in responsibility for the public good and harm alike, speaks extravagantly and treats a movement such as the DMK with contempt, what does it mean? Does it not mean that after coming to power he has acquired the courage to ask who can question him?

No one can deny that the DMK has grown into a political movement in which educated people participate alongside ordinary people. It may lack the resources available to those who attack it, but no one can therefore call it degraded.

They said Congress and the Dravidar Kazhagam had united, that the impossible had happened, and that in the next general election the two together would destroy the DMK. Kamaraj sought Periyar's assistance in the hope that this could be done. What did he find? The DMK demonstrated that it could win seventeen lakh votes in the general election. Although Congress possessed influence and wealthy supporters and had experience in many elections, it received only fifty lakh votes. Forgetting this and comparing the ideal of a liberation movement to a beggar's song is political ignorance and nothing else.

The “lord” beats his chest in the public ground. His commander, the hero of many defeats, Subramaniam, dons the robe of a political sage and lovingly appeals, “Give us your cooperation; only with all your cooperation can the country's plans be carried out.”

“We must not regard these as party problems, but as national problems, and everyone must work together. That is why I ask for the cooperation of all opposition parties,” the Finance Minister says tenderly in the Assembly.

Outside the Legislature, the Chief Minister tries to display his newly acquired status, while his chief disciple issues a loving invitation. Outside, Kamaraj calls us beggars; in the Legislature, Subramaniam says the help of those very beggars is needed. The lord says people listen to speeches and then leave; this new Buddha movingly declares that our cooperation too is required. Kamaraj performs Kathakali and Subramaniam performs Bharatanatyam. Here the Chief Minister assumes the form of the destroyer, while the Finance Minister becomes the embodiment of conciliation.

Members of the ruling party frequently say that they need the opposition's cooperation. I choose to believe that they ask sincerely, and so I state my views on the matter.

They invite opposition parties only to implement plans already prepared. When those plans were being drawn up, they did not ask us for a single suggestion. They did not even consider it necessary to learn the opposition's views.

They keep saying, “Cooperate! Cooperate!” without explaining what kind of cooperation they seek. As I understand it, one kind of cooperation is helping ensure that people act with respect for the law. If that is what they mean, no opposition party will hesitate to cooperate with those who govern.

But if the ruling party thinks we must neither point out the gaps in its plans nor criticise them, I respectfully state that in a democratic system it must not expect such cooperation from the opposition.

I know what the ruling party thinks of opposition parties. Recently, our Home Minister reportedly said, “None of the opposition parties in this State properly performs its duties.” What does it mean to say that and then demand cooperation? Nor is he alone in speaking so. At a public meeting our Chief Minister reportedly compared the DMK to beggars singing in the street.

When they speak in this fashion and then ask for cooperation, how can we believe the request is genuine?

Even so, whenever good measures or things necessary for the country are undertaken, we have voluntarily provided our cooperation. Both inside and outside the Assembly, the DMK has cooperated in making prohibition succeed.

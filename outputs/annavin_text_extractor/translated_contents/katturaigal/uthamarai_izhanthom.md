தோற்றம் : 3-10-1869

மறைவு : 30-1-1948

(திராவிட நாடு - 1-2-48)

## English Translation

A cry of grief throughout the world!

The Yamuna carries not merely the tears of the people of the Indian subcontinent, but those shed by all people throughout the world who possess humanity, good sense, and a longing for freedom. All those tears join and mingle with the sea. Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi’s life too has been forced into that same ocean of sacrifice into which noble souls who laboured to reform the world have been cast. He too had to follow the path travelled by all such noble souls.

The fire lit on the bank of the Yamuna at five in the evening on 31 January 1948 did not touch only Gandhi’s body. It touched not merely the very depths of millions of people throughout the world, but their hearts themselves. World history contains no other event so sorrowful, so convulsing, so shocking and bewildering. This grief will have transformed people in a manner they have never experienced before, not only throughout the land of India but wherever people of feeling and sound understanding live.

Wherever one looks, there is only bewilderment, tear-filled eyes, and anguish.

Each person will have known the pain caused by the death of someone who held the first and most important place in the heart of their family. Yet even that experience has no power to console us now.

This sorrow is somehow new and greater than any grief experienced before. Death brings sorrow even when it comes to a very old man. But was this death? No. He did not die. He was murdered by a great sinner.

It was evening. The noble man who had made an entire nation known to the world walked forward with his granddaughters on either side, towards an eager gathering awaiting his words. In one more minute he would sit upon the platform and lovingly counsel them, as he had on so many days, to preserve unity, remove mutual anger and hatred, and conduct themselves without destroying the nation’s good name. What an immense change that single minute produced! Merely to recall it creates a condition in which the heart might burst.

As he set foot on the steps leading to the platform, a villain, a beast moving in human form, a great traitor upon whom everyone in the land should spit, a sinner whose heart had become a vessel of hatred, fired his pistol once, twice, three times. The bullets struck the chest and abdomen. His eyes closed. He fell. The whole country sank into an ocean of grief. They carried him inside; those around him wailed. Life departed from him. At that very moment, honour itself departed from the country.

Without reflecting upon the magnitude of the ruin and treachery he was committing, that fanatic performed an act that would make even the Roman fanatics who crucified Jesus hang their heads in shame. At least the Roman imperial fanatics committed their cruelty because jealousy and hatred arose in them as the influence of Jesus spread and threatened their power. But this fanatic shot in the chest the very great leader through whom he gained dignity, his life gained a new status, and his country gained a new condition.

That demon who could murder his own mother fired when the leader came to offer wisdom to the people in the evening. He fired as the yellow sunlight fell upon Gandhi’s golden body and revealed his old age. He fired upon a man who came alone and unprotected, though the government of a vast subcontinent waited to run at his call. He did not pause even though another fanatic had thrown a bomb only days before; Gandhi had come without dwelling upon what might happen if someone tried again. Nor did the murderer recall that only days earlier Gandhi had fasted to the point of placing himself in death’s grip, striving thereby to remove the poison of fanaticism that had entered some among the people. He did not remember that for more than thirty years Gandhi had forgotten the very idea of a private life and laboured with the sole purpose of winning a new life for the country. He fired three times, until Gandhi fell.

The empire against which Gandhi had fought for thirty years did not dare do what the man whom he freed from that empire has now done. They say that if one rescues a cobra fallen into a fire, it will bite its rescuer. This sinner’s act was crueller still. Not even one year had passed since the nation gained a new standing in the world. Even if Gandhi had died naturally within that year, sorrow would have followed. But he did not die. He was murdered by a cruel man, said to be a young Hindu.

In the sixteenth century the world knew only that there was a land called India where cardamom and cloves grew, where gold and goods abounded, and where many ignorant people lived. India then gradually fell into the British grasp. The world spoke of it contemptuously as a fine hunting ground for the English. Though efforts towards freedom began to appear like the morning star in Tilak’s time, only after Gandhi entered the Congress could the world learn that a country called India possessed awakening and resurgence. The world came to see India through the radiance of Gandhi’s fame.

Gandhi alone could create in the country a spirit of struggle devoid of selfishness and unconcerned with results. Until then the nation had remained in the hands of intellectuals who petitioned for freedom. Neither his appearance, physique, speech, nor conduct seemed capable of creating a military spirit, yet he awakened in millions a heroic feeling capable of standing against armies. How many struggles there were! How many millions of young people took part! What deeds of courage and sacrifice occurred in his time! To the leader who gave all this, that traitor gave three bullets. Even crows and vultures, dogs and jackals, would recoil from eating the body of that great sinner. Such was the magnitude of the young man’s treachery.

This is a time surrounded by darkness because of that fanatic’s act. Since it happened without anyone expecting it, no amount of talking can bring consolation. This grief cannot easily be wiped away. A whole country faces a great crisis.

With darkness gathering, hearts pounding, and tears overflowing from our eyes, consolation is exceedingly difficult. But what can we do? One fanatic’s betrayal has shaken the nation itself. Our minds tremble. When the minds of the people are in such distress, the country’s leaders must offer comfort.

By taking a share of the grief upon themselves, by bowing their heads at the thought that we have witnessed such savagery in our country, and by remembering how Gandhi worked without fear until he fell, we too must gain the strength to bear this unbearable sorrow. Though he knew fanatics roamed Delhi and had even thrown a bomb, he came to that immense gathering without seeking protection. We must display something of that nobility.

The fanatic shot down not the radiance of Gandhi’s fame, but only his body, worn out by labour. Let us therefore wipe away our tears and bring our anguish under control. Let each of us understand that serving the people during our own lifetime is the respect and offering due to the great leader who forgot himself. Let us turn the fire kindled on the bank of the Yamuna into a fire of sacrifice and stand united. That was what he desired, and it was for that he worked. He repeatedly told us to remove anger, drive away hatred, and serve the nation. He was murdered by a great sinner while engaged in that very work. May his passing not stop with causing anguish, but give our minds new resolve and reveal a new truth.

This noble man created the firm conviction that even without weapons one could oppose and defeat an empire possessing immense military power and diplomatic strength. At the moment when even imperial power had bowed before him, some fanatic shot him. Neither this nation nor the world will forget it.

In our lifetime we saw the country win freedom. Before we died, the disgrace of being enslaved to England was removed. Our people stood with heads erect, rejoiced, and proudly declared our generation magnificent. Today our hearts are torn apart by the sorrow that we are also the people who lived in the age when a great sinner murdered the Mahatma.

During his thirty years of service there was not one harsh word, one dishonest act, or one selfish design. The influence created by his service spread India’s light and Asia’s fame so widely that it could frighten the European powers swollen with racial arrogance. Heads of foreign governments hurried to hear him speak. If he stayed in a mud hut, kings and emperors considered it an honour to go there. No other leader anywhere caused an entire land to tremble as it did on hearing of his death.

Buddha, who founded a new path; Ashoka, who enlarged India’s fame first through valour and later through wisdom; and kings who bound and ruled great empires became known only to the educated. The Mahatma was known throughout the land by scholar and commoner, and by members of governing assemblies; more than that, he found a place in their hearts.

He constantly insisted that the country embrace the twin principles of truth and nonviolence. Even when fraud and massacre occurred in politics, his heart suffered, but he never ceased to uphold those principles. He held to them until the end. The storm of pain caused by the thought that the country did not follow that path was immense. For several months he had spoken of that anguish.

He went where others thought it impossible to go and lacked the courage to proceed. In Noakhali and the Calcutta region, where massacres had taken place, he walked from village to village along narrow footpaths, over field embankments, and into the smallest hamlets, consoling every wounded heart. He transformed brutality and brought about a new change of mind among the people. Many feared that these were dangerous places roamed by ignorant and violent men. His gentle smile answered them all. “Do not worry about my life,” he said. He walked upon a volcano and the fire did not touch him. Yet injustice occurred in Delhi, where the seat of government itself stood close at hand.

Many leaders, including Kripalani, had said that accursed Delhi was a graveyard of empires and ought not even to remain our capital. Truly, the great empires formerly established there all collapsed through conspiracy, massacre, and the betrayal of sinners. A graveyard, yes, the graveyard of empires. Now it has become something more: a city of treachery that killed the noble man who laboured to reform the world.

When future generations across the world ask what is in Delhi, how can we and our descendants answer? With bowed heads and trembling voices we must cry, “That Delhi is the place where a great sinner killed the Mahatma.”

One hour before the Mahatma was murdered, Sardar Patel had been speaking with him. Noticing time passing and remembering that people had gathered to hear his loving counsel, Gandhi took leave of the Sardar. When Patel saw him again, he saw the Mahatma’s chest pierced by bullets, his face still serene, and life departing from him.

In our piercing sorrow, let us bring them for a moment before our inward eye. Could the hearts of Sardar Patel, Nehru, Rajendra Prasad, Rajagopalachari, Sarojini Naidu, and Devadas bear this news? What ties they had, what kind of ties, and for how many years! Their lives had mingled in hardship and happiness. What must their hearts have felt when they saw his body lying in Birla House? They have borne that sorrow with a sense of duty to wipe away the country’s grief. Let us stand beside them and bear this unbearable sorrow too.

In every city of the world, people’s hearts trembled. A cry of grief rose in distant America; lamentation sounded through many countries. Never before had people everywhere cried out in anguish at such a dreadful event.

The funeral procession that began at eleven in the morning reached the bank of the Yamuna at five in the evening. It travelled through an ocean of humanity and past wailing people. The route is said to have been five miles long, all of it filled with crowds. Yet the journey of grief took place not merely along those five miles. In every city and village throughout the thousands of miles of India, the people made that sorrowful journey. We did not all see the Yamuna, but with tear-filled eyes we saw many rivers of tears.

There they lit the pyre at five in the evening. Here, the moment we heard that the murderer had killed him, a fire was lit in our hearts, a fire that cannot heal or be extinguished. A man driven by rage set fire to the very life of our country. When we think what cruel treachery that wretch committed by placing fire upon the body of the noble man who made us known across the world, the fire in our hearts blazes still more fiercely.

We have suffered a very great and immeasurable loss. We have lost a noble man who stood above parties, restraints, desires, anger, and hatred. This grief has demolished the walls of party hostility and bound all the country’s people together. The same conviction is clearly heard in the consoling words of many leaders. This is the time to control the mind. If we fail, grief will turn people to stone, for it has struck that deeply.

Foreign ambassadors in India hurried to see him for the last time and stood beside him in lamentation. Burmese representatives, still grieving for their peerless leader Aung San, recently lost to an assassin, were also there. Governors stood at his feet. The Viceroy wept aloud at the sight of him. Statements of sorrow came from every great power. Yet all these things seem only to deepen the grief within us, not lessen it. Whatever consolation we seek, sorrow pulls us beyond it and strikes again.

There is only one way to escape this terrible condition: transform this very grief into a force that binds us together. Though we stand with eyes that have wept, let us place this immense task before us and remain faithful to the path of righteousness. If we can bear so great a hardship, no later hardship in our lifetime can gain enough power to torment us.

He has passed away, but the world will not forget him. Only if we acquire even one-thousandth of his firmness of mind will the world look upon us and say, “These are the people of the country in which he lived.” We must learn to restrain the beast that dwells within human beings and emerges whenever an opportunity arises to commit frenzy. The whole world shares our sorrow. We must use this resolve to preserve the new standing our country has attained.

We have lost the holy man who tirelessly laboured to shatter the fortress of imperialism, open temples that had remained closed for ages, and establish a new dignity among the people. Even after many generations, nothing can compensate for this immense loss suffered within our lifetime.

He had undertaken the work of removing the evils that had accumulated and become deeply embedded in Hinduism, using his pure life, spotless teaching, and new philosophical interpretation. He wholeheartedly believed that through this rare work the path of love would flourish and the truth that all are children of God would shine forth.

Alas! We had said that neither inward nor outward purity could reform the religious fanatics whose hatred had accumulated and grown over a long period. From the very place where he expected the path of love, a fanatical Hindu arose to drink his life. It is said that this man sought to establish Hindu religion and Hindu rule and had taken part in the Hyderabad State satyagraha. His name was Nathuram Vinayak Godse.

Addressing the world and our own hearts, we must all declare, not only in words but in deeds: “A wretch has struck down a noble soul. We have lost his body, but through his noble principles he will live forever. On this day of grief he has become part of every one of us; no one can now separate him from us. On the bank of the Yamuna we burned his mortal remains, but before returning home we found that he had taken his place within our hearts. He is here. He continues as the source of our virtue and good conduct.”

Born: 3 October 1869

Died: 30 January 1948

(Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 1 February 1948.)

## Translator's Notes

The article was published two days after Gandhi’s assassination and preserves the immediacy, repetition, and emotional intensity of a contemporary obituary.
“உத்தமர்” denotes a person of exceptional moral nobility; “noble soul” is used in the title and related passages according to context.
The source’s “யமூனை/யமுளை” are OCR or print variants of “யமுனை” (Yamuna). They remain unchanged in the Tamil source; the translation consistently uses “Yamuna.”
The scan-backed final page, `015-8f0e56cf93.png`, was absent from the original OCR Markdown. It has been restored to the canonical Tamil source and supplies the conclusion, Gandhi’s dates, and the publication date.
The source uses the period terms “வைசிராய்” (Viceroy) and “சர்க்கார்” (government); the translation retains their historical sense.

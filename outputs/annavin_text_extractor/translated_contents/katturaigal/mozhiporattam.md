## English Translation

We heard that the soil of East Bengal, which gave birth to patriots who shed their red blood, had itself turned red during the past few days, and our hearts grieved. Mother Bengal, proud bearer of many bloodstained episodes in the political history of the Indian subcontinent, saw her “world of the future” floating in a pool of blood. The blood of college students was spilled. In that land of sacrifice, the student world rose like a raging sea to remove the humiliation inflicted on its mother tongue, only to become prey to batons and bullets. As reports reached us that “the bank of the Ganges was reddened” by this heroic struggle of youth, we trembled.

The fire lit by the students has grown. The heroic cry raised in Dacca on 21 February has begun to echo throughout East Pakistan. As a result, reports say:

Twelve people were killed.

One hundred and fifty were injured.

Professors were arrested.

Members of the Legislative Assembly were arrested.

The military ran riot.

All this occurred within a few days in East Bengal. Shops observed a *hartal*. Dacca University was closed. Twenty-eight students were arrested. Police entered every room. A former League secretary was arrested. Newspaper editors were imprisoned. The army patrolled. Section 144 and curfew were imposed. All these things happened in East Pakistan.

Every one of these reports stings the mind like a scorpion. They happened for the Bengali language.

An upheaval has arisen for Bengali, a language rich in vitality and prosperity. Bengali has fostered national courage, given wealth to literature and produced countless poets. This is the plain that has borne children devoted to their mother tongue. Yet there was such horror there, such violence: twelve lives, military marches and the roar of bullets!

Why did all this happen? To stop those who came forward to protect their language, and to impose upon the public something they did not want. The Pakistan Government had previously proclaimed Urdu as the official language. From the day that decision was made in Pakistan's Constituent Assembly, East Bengal had continued to seethe.

The people demanded that Bengali, like Urdu, also be made an official language. They sought equal status for their mother tongue. Two-thirds of Pakistan's population spoke Bengali, yet Karachi did not accept their demand.

Bengali students rose like tigers to oppose this measure. Declaring, “This is a danger to Bengali; Bengali too must become an official language,” they set out. To establish their right, a strike arose at the university and the drum of protest sounded in the colleges. Once this language struggle blossomed, professors, legislators, university people, Dacca lawyers, the public and traders all assembled together to prevent humiliation of the mother tongue. Many prominent Muslim League figures participated directly. The fire spread. Shops closed. Students stayed away from colleges. Cries of “Do not degrade Bengali!” began to rise.

No one can say this language struggle was deliberately contrived, or dismiss it as a storm stirred up by a few people out of hatred, because the whole of East Bengal joined this protest movement without party distinction.

East Bengal's total population was six crore, of whom four crore spoke Bengali. Who could endure contempt for the mother tongue of such a great majority? When language is regarded as one's mother, would her children hesitate?

East Bengal's Chief Minister, Nurul Amin, treated this consciousness of rights with indifference. The agitation therefore grew stronger and student ranks gathered everywhere. To suppress the language struggle swelling like a storm, the East Pakistan Government trusted troops and guns. On 21 February it turned its guns upon the student procession that advanced like a surging sea. Bodies fell, and 150 people lay wounded.

Even after such disgrace, peace has reportedly not returned. Arrests, Section 144 and military patrols continue.

Only after blood and bullets had fallen and scattered did East Pakistan's legislators accept the people's demand. They reportedly passed a resolution declaring that Bengali should become an official language and sent it to Pakistan's Constituent Assembly.

This decision, reached after so much pain, is nevertheless welcome.

A language struggle is resistance to autocratic arrogance. Among those who rose in East Bengal demanding respect for Bengali were Congress members; the five legislators imprisoned were Congressmen. They sacrifice themselves to prevent the domination of another language over their mother tongue. But here, in our Madras, in delightful Tamil Nadu! To the nationalist comrades here who support Hindi, we point to East Bengal. Look at the drops of blood shed there for language. See the khadi garments stained with blood. Look at the valiant people sacrificing themselves for their mother tongue!

Language struggles will arise everywhere. East Bengal declares that an attempt to enslave a flourishing language and make an unwanted language common cannot succeed.

We honour the sacrifice of those noble young people who rose for their mother tongue and gave their lives. The fire kindled by those student jewels has today assumed a mighty form. Those heroic sons who fell in the streets dedicated themselves to the glory of Mother Bengal. We believe the blood they shed in a brave struggle for their mother tongue will not be wasted.

We want the Pakistan Government to respect the voice of rights. It must protect itself from succumbing to autocratic arrogance as the Indian Government has done. It is dangerous for the desire to subject people to such authoritarianism to sprout within Pakistan too.

We therefore want those in Karachi to act responsibly. That Bengal's soil was reddened by a language struggle is a stain.

The people of East Bengal accepted the partition that created Pakistan and laboured for the splendour of the new country. They rejoiced when Pakistan blossomed because their community had obtained a separate nation. If people filled with such joy and hope were driven to oppose their own government, it shows that love of the mother tongue sounds through the very nerves of youth.

The present language struggle is the result of Karachi's failure to understand this truth. Such shameful events do not befit good government.

Those who dream that linguistic domination can be spread must understand this. To lose one's mother tongue...

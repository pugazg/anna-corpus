## English Translation

## A Thunderbolt Against *The Hindu*'s Torch-Bearing Service

## Who Is the Communalist? An Explanation

“Come! Come!” we invited affectionately. Nine years passed. We grew weary on seeing many friends refuse the invitation and disparage our moral struggle, but we did not lose hope. We knew that anyone who sat calmly and reflected would ultimately find our principle just.

The sense of rights does not arise only during a conflict between white rulers and our people and then decay when that conflict ends. If some within the country use caste, religion, wealth, military power or any other cause to dominate and suppress others, that sense will not remain asleep. We knew that once the “national struggle” ended, the struggle for social justice would certainly begin. We therefore retained confidence that friends would heed our invitation.

Nationalist comrades often asked: “Why fear Brahmins or envy them? They are only three in a hundred, while you are ninety-seven. Why should a majority seek protection from a minority?” Each time they asked, they admired their own intellectual brilliance.

Numerically Brahmins are few, while non-Brahmins are so numerous that their very breath could suffocate them. But Brahminism does not depend upon numbers; it depends upon the power supplied to it through many channels.

A warrior may terrify a whole village, yet in a trance he fears the small bunch of neem leaves in a magician's hand. The greatness attributed to the leaves and magician produces that fear. Likewise, although Brahmins are few, innumerable texts have long taught that they hold the keys to heaven and hell, measure divine grace, possess a birthright to other communities' service, and that pleasing them pleases God. These ideas were mixed into the people's blood over centuries, enabling influence wholly disproportionate to the community's size. Our leaders' statement explained precisely this.

## The Bridle Is Small, But...

A bridle is small, but everyone knows what a horse suffers beneath it. A nose-rope is not even as thick as a bull's tail, yet a bull caught by it submits. If extraordinary greatness is attributed to a small community, its influence inevitably grows. Even a town of nine thousand fears one aircraft circling overhead because it may drop a bomb.

When the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms were introduced, our leaders therefore fought to include protection for non-Brahmins and reserve a defined number of legislative seats. Our people received many benefits as a result.

No similar limitation was included in the 1935 constitution now in force. Our leaders did not agitate, believing Brahminism would recognize its error and subside. What resulted? Brahmins, three percent of the population, would proportionately receive seven legislative seats; today they possess forty-nine. Though proportionality would deny them even one ministerial place, they hold four of ten. The chief ministership and assembly presidency too appear effectively theirs. They also pull the strings of legislative proceedings, and their dominance has spread through many fields.

If one small community acquires such dominance, where is the place for others? Have they no right to govern and prosper? They pay taxes and serve national welfare, yet struggle without room in higher fields. Can anyone honestly call that just? Is it justice for one person to toil and heap up produce while another consumes it without shame or question? If those who were *mahants* in religion alone become leaders and ministers in the “national” field too, can that be democracy?

## Why Did They Rise in National Life?

As the leaders' statement showed, because we elevated Brahmins socially and accepted them as religious guides, we came to accept them as gurus in national life. In religion, contradiction of their word was forbidden and hell used to intimidate the mind. Likewise, criticism of Congress policy, plans, conduct or government was said to obstruct imminent self-rule, and critics were abused as traitors and servants of the white man.

Just as those exposing injustice committed in religion's name are branded atheists and subjected to hate propaganda, those exposing harm beneath nationalism's screen are made objects of public suspicion. The misguided Tamil who regards Congress as salvation glares, threatens, flexes shoulders and hands, and even reaches for stones when seeing another party. Such propaganda can alter humanity itself. It caused Tamils to misjudge Tamil leaders; only the tireless work of Periyar and others opened most Tamil eyes.

The leaders therefore said safeguards were necessary to stop a small community from using argument and strategy to bewilder candid people and seize most offices.

All this is said because the country requires genuine freedom. Should we swim across the river only to fall into mud?

The self-rule desired by non-Brahmins must not permit one community to dominate another, a minority to languish under a majority, or a majority to be destroyed under a minority. It must be good government in which all possess equal rights. Everyone reasoning with knowledge will accept this. Bharati too sang:

> Gone is the age when the Brahmin was called master;
> gone too is the age when the white foreigner was called lord.

He sang it, but it has not yet come true. Our party's work is to realize it. For that we lovingly call, “Come! Come!”

This article explaining the issue and our resolve appeared in *Viduthalai* in 1939. We were mocked: should a majority fear a minority; should the sea fear the Cooum? We were warned that communalism was disgraceful and uncivilized. Intellectuals supposedly would not even raise such talk; it was dismissed as antiquated agitation for office. Some threatened imprisonment for inciting communal hatred. Without fear or anxiety, we continued saying what conscience judged right.

Nine years later, when friends once seated in the opposing camp say we were right, we feel genuine pride and delight.

The weekly *Kandibam*, issued from the *Dinasari* office, wrote:

> Dissolving communal organizations alone will not eliminate communalism; communalists' attitudes must change. Consider Tamil Nadu. Fortunately, Hindu-Muslim feeling is not as pronounced as in the North, but Brahmin/non-Brahmin feeling exists and leads toward communalism. We must examine why it arose.
>
> The minority Brahmin community dominated every field, and over time that dominance became a monopoly because of special opportunities. The non-Brahmin majority rebelled and created the Justice Party. After entering government, it became a party of ceremonial turbans and later died, but the Brahmin/non-Brahmin movement, though weakened, did not die because neither side developed a willingness to yield.
>
> Who must yield? Those who possess dominance. That is the world's way. On this principle, the Madras government introduced communal representation in appointments and education so that no single community monopolized posts or educational facilities.
>
> This system has existed for a long time. When Rajaji was premier, some demanded its abolition as contrary to Congress policy. Rajaji warned it could not be removed so easily, and the agitation subsided. The late Srinivasa Iyengar supported communal representation and went further, saying communalism would disappear if Brahmins refrained from seeking employment.
>
> That agitation has now resumed. *The Hindu*, while preaching grand nationalism, leads it with a torch, elegantly publishing readers' letters asking whether communalism must enter even education.
>
> We do not believe the giant bandicoot *Hindu* is unaware why colleges use communal representation. Let it compare the numbers of Brahmin and non-Brahmin students before the system. Have disadvantaged castes not advanced through it? Are these people not a majority over Brahmins? Does *Hindu* dharma require that majority to remain below? Could the country prosper so? Even a fool would say no.
>
> *The Hindu* instead asks whether there is no place for merit. It is profoundly dishonest to say merit belongs to some and cannot arise in others. Given equal opportunities, everyone can become capable. To call one caste inherently talented is wrong. Keep one community below and it will appear merely animate; raise another to the pinnacle and even its stumble looks like an accomplishment. All this shouting about “merit” is a screen concealing naked communalism.
>
> Readers may be startled by another fact. Observe how these patriots, who prattle about merit and non-communal nationalism, staff their own offices. One wonders whether they are progressive offices or sheds maintaining a sacrificial fire. They appoint only people of their caste and subsect. If pencils and building lime had caste, with Vadakalai and Thenkalai divisions, they would purchase only their own sect's pencil and lime. Asked why, they call it private business. Their policy is made from these people and their mentality; how can inseparable elements be separated?
>
> Nor do these virtuous sons practise what they preach. They oppose caste division while working to elevate one caste. Instead of saying this openly, they proclaim a universal, planetary nationalism connecting them even with inhabitants of Mars, while acting according to the Jinnah model.
>
> This communal mentality must disappear wherever it exists because it endangers the country. Let these masqueraders, who falsely cry “merit” and “universalism,” reform themselves at least after seeing Parliament's law. Let them cease asking applicants to their offices which sect they belong to.

That article appeared in the 16 April 1948 issue.

The age has passed when people mocked: “What use is envying Brahmins? It is like a cat branding itself after seeing a tiger. See their sharp intelligence, energy and diligent study. If you lack their ability, carry loads or drive carts instead of wailing that the Brahmin advanced. It is futile hatred and the cry of incapacity. Have you no shame?”

After nine years, our principle has begun to settle in their minds. *Kandibam* writes that Brahmins rose not through exceptional intelligence or labour but special opportunities.

When we asked whether it was right for three percent to monopolize every office and what would become of the greater community, they reddened, bristled and argued: “Capable people enter high positions. Why suffer jealousy? Administration works well only when talented people hold important offices.”

Now observe how *Kandibam* pierces the target:

> “It is profoundly dishonest to say merit belongs only to some and cannot arise in others.”

> “All this shouting about ‘merit’ is a screen concealing naked communalism.”

Though *Kandibam* took nine years to aim and release its shaft, its piercing power is tremendous. Both weapon and enthusiasm are new.

“*The Hindu* carries the torch.”

“The giant bandicoot *Hindu*.”

“Offices resembling sacrificial-fire sheds.”

“The masqueraders' false cry.”

There are many such pungent expressions. Nine years of patience were not wasted. A mocked principle has been accepted; former critics have resolved to defend it. The truth we long proclaimed has gained new strength.

Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 25 April 1948.

## Translator's Notes

The scans confirm the title as `கணபாடி கவசத்தைக் காண்டீபம் துளைக்கிறது`; OCR had damaged `கணபாடி` and `கவசத்தைக்`. `கணபாடி` is an older term for a learned Brahmin or Vedic scholar, while `காண்டீபம்` evokes Arjuna's bow and is also the title of the quoted weekly. “Kandibam Pierces the Ganapadi Armour” preserves the title's weapon metaphor.

The final scan gives the publication note as `25.4.48`; OCR rendered it `23.4.49`. The embedded *Kandibam* article is separately dated 16 April 1948. Both canonical Tamil copies were corrected from the visible scans before this bilingual file was built.

The article and quotation use historical caste-political labels and caricatures, including `பெருச்சாளி` (“giant bandicoot”) for *The Hindu*. They are translated as documentary rhetoric, not endorsed descriptions. `வகுப்புவாரிப் பிரதிநிதித்துவம்` is rendered “communal representation” in its period-specific administrative sense.

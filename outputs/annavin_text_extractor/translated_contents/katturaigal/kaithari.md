## English Translation

Handloom Week was celebrated with pomp, not merely in one part of Tamil Nadu but throughout the region.

Dance, drama, musical performances and other artistic programmes were mixed together and served. Explanations of Congress policy were supplied too.

Whoever a Congress speaker may be, whether a hero scarred in the August struggle, a person who entered midway and rose to prominence, a minister or anyone else, the whole country knows how many people will come merely to hear him speak. The rulers have made not one or two but many attempts to hide this well-known truth.

One such effort is to mix their offering of ideas with artistic programmes. At the handloom celebration, the whole country saw the “skill” with which they concealed the lack of skill!

We are not so blind to ideas that we would say artistic programmes are unnecessary at festivals of this kind.

Those who organised Handloom Week may have thought that dance, drama and similar events were needed to attract the people's interest and attention. There is nothing wrong in that.

Yet the success or failure of the festival depends upon the idea that arises in the minds of the thousands who see the celebration, hear its speeches and finally enjoy its artistic programmes before leaving.

If those returning from the festival discuss only the dancer's ability, the precision of her poses, her skill in expression, the rolling of her eyes and the bending of her waist, can Handloom Week be considered successful?

If, instead of speaking about the elegance of handloom saris and the quality of dhotis, they investigate whether the gypsy dance was good, the peacock dance captivated the mind, and whether the dancer's talent appeared more in the snake dance or Bharatanatyam, how can Handloom Week become useful?

One admirer announces what he enjoyed: “If you call anything dance, this is dance! Magnificent! She spins like a top.” Another, eager to display his connection with dance, replies: “Why do you say that, sir? Was this dance? It was complete nonsense. She does not know how to hold a pose and is an absolute zero in expression. My brother-in-law's daughter Ambuja, you know, she can dance. Just watch her...”

A young bull paints his excitement in words: “What a performance! What a performance! Those rolling eyes alone deserve crores. Ah, that light in the eyes, the elegance of her gait, the curve of her waist...” Another leaps to the next stage: “A waist? Was there really one?” Many among those returning from the dance speak in this fashion.

Artistic programmes occupy far more space in their minds than handloom cloth.

If that is so, what benefit is produced by thousands attending Handloom Week and returning?

This does not mean every festival containing an artistic programme must meet the same fate. If anything is done with a sense of proportion, such danger can be avoided.

But because the organisers of Handloom Week did not remain within limits and instead gave artistic programmes primary importance, the good that should have resulted from the festival slipped away.

For many years we have attended to the need to protect the handloom industry, remove the misery of those engaged in it, wipe away their tears and make their lives prosperous. With the right earned by having offered whatever assistance lay within our ability, we are duty-bound to examine the festival's results, express our view and ask that its defect be corrected.

No one can regard as ordinary the efforts we have undertaken to seek support for handloom cloth throughout the country or the benefits they produced.

At the same time, not only we but the whole country asks what efforts our ministers have made to relieve the distress of handloom workers.

They say, “We too support handloom.” From time to time, they advise: “Produce new varieties; only then will the industry grow.” Wherever they go, they offer a list: “We have opened emporiums, constructed colonies and established cooperatives.”

We find no shortage of words in the ministers' speeches. We do not deny or undervalue their right or ability to offer advice.

But what efforts and measures have the rulers taken so far to obtain permanent support for the handloom industry and remove the workers' misery?

They offer small forms of assistance, as though feeding milk to a child with a spoon. Where have they provided permanent support?

If a newborn child writhes in hunger, milk may be fed by spoon. What does it mean to apply the same method to adults?

Handloom is a very large industry. Many thousands of families depend upon it for life, and many lakhs of people are engaged in it.

When that is the position, how can giving occasional minor assistance and concessions amount to protecting the industry?

Small forms of aid will neither develop handloom nor make the lives of its many lakhs of dependants blossom.

Already our pitiable ministers repeatedly go to the rulers in Delhi crying, “Bhavati bhiksham dehi,” seeking a rebate for handloom sales. Those who know the matter say that even this “alms” may soon be stopped.

What, then, is the handloom industry's future? What will our rulers do to eliminate competition between handloom and mill-made cloth? Which will receive greater importance and government support: handloom or the mills? Or will they leave both to remain exactly as they are?

An opinion already prevails among Congress leaders that the handloom industry cannot endure or obtain protection for long. Otherwise, why do they seek support for the Ambar charkha? Why do they try to introduce it? Without the courage to say openly that handloom is unwanted, they are single-mindedly engaged in an attempt to destroy khadi. What other meaning can this have?

The decline of handloom is no ordinary problem. If the industry decays, the lives of lakhs will decay and unemployment will arise.

Our rulers possess no plan to provide them with new occupations, industries and opportunities. There is emptiness not only in the government treasury but in its thinking.

That is why they conduct festivals, introduce entertainments and diversions, and turn attention elsewhere instead of entering any useful undertaking.

Recognising this problem, Acharya submitted a scheme, and people belonging to several parties accepted that it was necessary.

Yet Delhi's rulers threw away his scheme, offering the strange reason that it was impracticable.

I am not going to say that the great Congress members treated rejecting everything Acharya proposed as their policy.

The proposals I am about to present were supplied by our Finance Minister:

> Developing handloom while also developing the mill industry is contradictory. Concessions granted by reducing the tax on mill cloth severely affect handloom.

> Certain varieties, such as particular saris and dhotis, must be reserved exclusively for handloom.

Anyone reading this may feel that it contains an independent idea.

Let us leave aside whether the goods are domestic or foreign, because the country knows this is a reprint of Acharya's scheme and needs no explanation from us.

The country knows our Finance Minister too as a man who finds fault with everything said and complains that statistics have not been supplied.

If he speaks at length about this problem, must we not assume that he has discussed all the statistics?

No one denies that statistics are firm evidence.

But what action have he and the ministry in which he serves taken so far to develop handloom, remedy its shortcomings, and improve the lives of the many lakhs engaged in it?

No useful action appears to have been taken so far.

Why does he not present in Delhi the evidence he showers upon us and the explanations he gives us, and argue his case there?

How often does he travel to Delhi? When speaking to us, he displays the courage to say that he fears nothing and no one. Had he argued before Delhi's rulers with even one-hundredth of that courage, could no result have been obtained?

Why does he stop all his chest-thumping speech at the borders of Tamil Nadu?

More than anyone else, he has every opportunity and facility to speak about this problem. He can shower statistics.

What the country expects from him is a way to solve the problem, not statistics.

Thus what the country expects from him and the government is a useful path to a solution, not empty figures or artistic programmes that melt the heart.

But what can be done? Their attention is devoted to searching out eye-catching spectacles and offering a feast to the eyes, not to wiping away the handloom worker's tears.

22 January 1959

## Translator's Notes

`கனம்` is a shortened honorific used for ministers (“Honourable”) and is placed in quotation marks sarcastically. `பவதி பிட்சாந்தேகி` represents the Sanskrit begging formula *bhavati bhiksham dehi*, “Madam, give alms.” `அம்பர் சர்க்கா` is the Ambar charkha promoted for decentralised yarn production. The OCR wording naming sari and dhoti varieties in the Finance Minister's proposal is damaged (`போட்ட புடவை, போட்ட வேட்டி`); the English remains generic rather than inventing a textile classification. The opening's geographical phrase is internally repetitive, so it is translated conservatively as a celebration throughout the region.

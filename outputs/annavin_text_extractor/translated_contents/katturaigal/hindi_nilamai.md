## English Translation

According to a resolution of the All India Congress Committee, which met in Bombay in June 1939, India was to be divided on a linguistic basis. The following nineteen regions were specified:

| Region | Language | Capital |
|---|---|---|
| Andhra | Telugu | Madras |
| Assam | Assamese | Gauhati |
| Bihar | Hindustani | Patna |
| Bengal | Bengali | Calcutta |
| Bombay City | Marathi and Gujarati | Bombay |
| Delhi | Hindustani | Delhi |
| Gujarat | Gujarati | Ahmedabad |
| Karnataka | Kannada | Dharwar |
| Kerala | Malayalam | Calicut |
| Mahakoshal | Hindustani | Jabalpur |
| Maharashtra | Marathi | Poona |
| Nagpuri | Marathi | Nagpur |
| North-West Frontier | Pashto | Peshawar |
| Punjab | Punjabi | Lahore |
| Sindh | Sindhi | Karachi |
| Tamil Nadu | Tamil | Madras |
| United Provinces | Hindustani | Lucknow |
| Utkal | Oriya | Cuttack |
| Vidarbha | Marathi | Akola |

Even by the Congress resolution, it is clear that only four regions, Bihar, Delhi, Mahakoshal, and the United Provinces, had Hindustani as their language.

The other fifteen regions possessed different languages.

Is it proper, therefore, to impose the language of these four regions upon the others?

Can the claim that Hindustani is spoken by most of India's people withstand statistical scrutiny? Even by the Congress's own calculation, only four of nineteen regions had Hindustani as their language.

How then can it be India's common language?

(*Dravida Nadu*, 47.48)

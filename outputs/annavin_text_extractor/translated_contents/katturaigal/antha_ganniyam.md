## English Translation

Many have praised him; countless people have placed garlands of fame upon him.

The Queen upon the throne congratulates him. Political scholars applaud him. The people's cry of “Long live!” reaches the sky. No newspaper has failed to portray his fame. America calls him “beloved leader”; France addresses him as “honoured great man.” English intellectuals call him a rare sovereign of speech, and the literary world celebrates him as an outstanding king of the pen.

“Even then I did not experience such joy. But the scene I witnessed here now has melted my heart. This is a day I can never forget in my life. Today witnessed the summit of the astonishing events in our country's history. The world will be amazed when it hears of this and ask: ‘Can a politician receive such a magnificent welcome?’”

His heart reportedly melted, his voice trembled and tears of joy sprang forth. The sight of him bowing to others and taking leave as he departed the chamber is said to have shaken the mind.

British newspapers write in this fashion about the tribute paid in Parliament to Prime Minister Winston Churchill, who recently reached the age of eighty.

Churchill is no ordinary man. During the last war, with a single lion's roar, he made Britain—tossed like a splinter upon the waves—plant the flag of victory. No one fails to praise his ability. An old veteran of service, his voice, though tinged with mockery, sounds like music to the people of England.

There is no surprise in so many garlands of fame and tributes being offered upon his birthday. But why did he say in Parliament, “Only now, and only here, have I received a joy those things did not give me”?

Many members of Parliament are his followers, people who acclaim him every day. Their praise may therefore have given him little elation. All manner of gifts must have accumulated before him. Why, then, did he say that the gift and words offered in Parliament melted his heart?

Nor was the gift some wonder capable of astonishing the world. It was a painting—not a treasury of matchless imagination, but a portrait of Churchill himself. Yet Churchill called that painting a blessing he had received and described it with joy as a privilege not granted to others.

The words spoken by the presenter were not a ritual of “O genius! O wisdom! O fame!” like the praises others offered Churchill. They contained a little spice: “Mr Churchill, you must leave behind some enduring achievements in your lifetime.”

Even so, he exulted that he had received a happiness not supplied by Queen Elizabeth's greeting or his political comrades' cries of “Long live,” asking who else had received such joy.

It is true that this “blessing” received by Churchill does not come to many in political life. The praise and gift came not from his colleague; if they had, he might have regarded them as merely one more among a thousand, offered his customary thanks and departed.

The giver was Attlee—the Leader of the Opposition, who daily criticised Churchill and his government sharply in Parliament.

It was upon seeing Attlee's praise and gift, and the respect shown by every opposition member rising to their feet, that Churchill's voice—ordinarily scattering ridicule and sarcasm—grew thick and tears of joy appeared in the eyes that daunted his opponents.

“I have received joy; I have witnessed an event that has never occurred anywhere in the world; Attlee has congratulated me.” Thrilled by that fulfilment, the aged veteran bowed as he left the chamber.

We know Churchill's policies, the aristocratic course he upheld and the obstacles he placed before Indian freedom. Yet when we learn of the dignity he displayed now, our hearts genuinely marvel for a moment.

He could have said a few words: “Who is this Attlee? Will this man's praise keep me alive? The entire world knows me. Why do I need this gift? If I asked, I could receive not one but a thousand astonishing portraits framed in gold and painted in varied colours. Why this one?”

Such words would not have damaged the crown of fame he already possessed. There are lords who would have rejoiced and published news in every style: “Attlee was struck upon the head; Churchill taught this Attlee the sense he needed.”

Churchill did nothing of the kind. His heart melted, his face blossomed, and joy surged in his eyes and gait. He marvelled that this was a rare blessing.

Such dignity is no light thing. Living among people who warn that they will carry khadi flags and create a disturbance if someone goes to unveil a statue of Gandhi, we feel genuine delight when we contemplate that scene.

For people in political life, a small bouquet willingly offered by an opponent is worth a crore more than rose garlands placed by their own party. That is why Churchill said, “I have been blessed.” His dignity deserves great praise.

At the same time, Attlee was Leader of the Opposition and Churchill the head of a government pursuing policies Attlee rejected. Yet Attlee possessed the magnanimity to recognise that dangers to the country had been removed through Churchill, that he had reached the age of eighty and that his service too must be acknowledged—and put that generosity into action. Attlee's dignity is astonishing.

Had Attlee remained silent while many others offered praise, no one would have thought anything of it. But Attlee congratulated him—though with some spice—and presented a gift. As Churchill said, this was no ordinary event but a note for history.

In this country, the wretched belief that political civilisation consists of abusing opposition parties and leaping forward to strike them has become a contagious disease.

There are many small-minded people who keep some object in mind and rush to inflict cruelty, calling opponents traitors, gamblers, outcasts, murderers, enemies and undesirable teardrops.

Others roar: “If they are the opposition, we shall settle their account. Do they praise me? Whom do they have worthy of praise? That is why. Did I ask them to praise me? They are idle. There must be some conspiracy.”

We have always condemned such political incivility. Though standing in the opposite direction in the political sky, we have never hesitated to praise the good done by another party. Nor, for that reason, do we excuse its faults.

Whether it was Achariyar or Kamaraj, the Pandit or Kripalani, this has remained our policy until today. That is why, when the DMK arose, we proclaimed the three words: dignity, duty and discipline. We have asked the Kazhagam's warriors to travel along that path.

Only if this course blossoms will the country live and humanity endure. That is why we rejoice in Attlee's magnanimity and Churchill's dignity.

More than that, the veteran Churchill has displayed his dignity in another way as well—a dignity that the Pandit said to radiate light throughout the world must understand; that Achariyar, who seeks to live by intimidation, must understand; that those creating abuse and quarrels in the political sky must understand; that those who say whatever they lay down is law and whatever they command is binding must understand.

It is a dignity that those who fling the words “nonsense” and “barbarian,” and then wish to parade like lions despite knowing that the wounded community has roared like a tiger, must understand.

Churchill displayed it recently. Speaking at a meeting in his constituency, he released news that startled the world. During the last World War, he said, he had telegraphed the then commander Montgomery to collect and retain weapons captured from the Nazis because they might later be needed to attack the Russians.

The news produced great agitation throughout the country. Had Churchill associated with Russia while secretly contemplating conspiracy? That question arose. Attlee announced that he would bring a motion of censure in Parliament.

There was such commotion and condemnation everywhere. Churchill was no ordinary man: fearless, energetic, a treasury of mockery and a great statesman. More than that, he had spent the greater part of his life and stood in its final season.

Do you know what he did? On 1 December, in Parliament, he expressed regret for making that statement. Before a great number of members, he admitted that what he had said was wrong:

> I am genuinely sorry.
>
> I spoke under the impression that I had sent such a telegram.
>
> I habitually teach others that before speaking they should consider what they are going to say.
>
> I deeply regret that I failed to follow that advice myself.
>
> I accept the correction with which *The Manchester Guardian* exposed my error.

That is what he said. He expressed regret. This dignity captivates our thought even more.

Churchill is a great speaker, capable of turning words into sword strokes and reducing enemy forts to dust. He could have tried to end the issue with a little anecdote, humour or mockery. He is a great man, but rather than concealing it, he recognised his mistake and expressed regret. There lies the rare dignity worth seeing.

That is political principle—the loving principle that political life exists for the people.

Churchill behaves so there. How do our rulers behave here? They vilify, accuse and abuse. When told they are wrong, they ask, “What can you do?” They disregard even the legislature's decision and crush the democratic voice.

Churchill respects the country and sincerely expresses regret. These people think only of their own prestige and thus appear arrogant.

Wherever their political camp may be, they must acquire this character of respecting the country and the dignity that prevents injury to it. Churchill has demonstrated that dignity to everyone in public life. We therefore rejoice greatly and point it out to those here.

Though he is an enemy, the opposition acknowledges the good he did.

Though it comes from another party, he receives its gift joyfully in his hands.

Though a very great man, he sincerely expresses regret when what he did was wrong.

Such dignity must arise in public life. Whether it is a grove of common ownership or a nationalist forest, every party must develop the character to respect people, the maturity to praise the benefits they brought the country, and the nature to admit and regret mistakes sincerely.

Here, however, these virtues are as rare as horns upon a horse. Those who wield the sceptre have forgotten the very character of respecting other parties. One or two opposition parties regard opposition to everything as their sole work. Ministers who commit mistakes never recognise and regret them. Yet they never fail to speak of democracy, the people's age and new life.

Politics exists to refine human life, not cultivate incivility. Politics exists for service to the people, not for fattening the human body.

Churchill expressed this in two lines while responding to the tribute offered him: “I earn my living with pen and tongue.” He also said he had never used politics for personal profit and therefore wished, as Attlee had urged, to leave behind some enduring deeds during his life.

We draw attention once more: his path may be different, even opposed to ours. But everyone must respect the sincere desire to accomplish enduring things for the country before departing.

If hearts of that kind appeared even in the fascist camp of Congress, the country might become a flower garden. What does it matter who acts, so long as the country lives?

Will such magnanimity arise among the great men here? Will dignity blossom? There is not even a sign of it.

*Published in Dravida Nadu*, 5 December 1954

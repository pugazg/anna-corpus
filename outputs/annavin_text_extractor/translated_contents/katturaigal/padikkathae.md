## English Translation

“What was that sound just now?”

“We were talking.”

“No. I heard the sound of reading. Do not hide the truth. That is what you were reading, is it not? Quietly and respectfully hand over the rolled manuscript. Otherwise trouble will come upon every one of your heads. Beware!”

“Why are you angry? No one here was reading. We were merely talking, for amusement...”

“Amusement, is it? What marvellous amusement did you find in it? That rogue mocks our gait, gestures and manners. He drags us into the marketplace so ragged fellows can ridicule us. Without honour or spirit, you read that book which degrades us?”

“It was amusing. Moreover, many things in it are true. He has described very well the conditions found in noble households.”

“Enough! Shut your mouth, fool! That manuscript does not merely insult us. If it spreads among ordinary people, we shall be disgraced, and our nobility itself will be endangered.”

“But the people may already have read it!”

“No. The public can read it only if printed copies exist. A few people somewhere may have secretly obtained handwritten copies, but the idea cannot spread rapidly through them.”

***

“Come now, stop exaggerating and praising it as something magnificent. I already know enough about all the scandals of these nobles.”

“How?”

“How? Go and read the manuscript that exposes their affairs!”

“Which manuscript? Where is it?”

“Do not shout! The police may hear. Come tomorrow night at twelve. I will take you to the place where it is read. It must remain secret.”

***

“Tell me without lying. Did you read that manuscript?”

“No, sir!”

“Were you standing nearby listening while someone else read it?”

“No, sir!”

“Do not lie!”

“No, sir. I did not even know such a manuscript existed.”

“You did not know? Then why did you look at me mockingly when I put on my silk garments and ceremonial attire? All of you have read what that sinner wrote ridiculing us, and that is why you behave without knowing head from foot.”

***

One manuscript, and throughout the country so many kinds of conversation about it! Some spoke with fear, some with disgust, and many danced with joy. In the nobleman's mansion, the worker's hut, the marketplace and the flower garden, everywhere people spoke only of that manuscript in whispers. This continued not for one or two years but for many years.

And after all, it was merely a play, not even published in print. Yet in that condition it made the whole country talk. It shook people's hearts.

The Tsarist state possessed the power to confront enemy armies, domestic conspirators and revolutionary bombs without fear and suppress them through harsh action. Yet one manuscript caused that regime such distress.

The Tsarist government issued a prohibition: “Anyone who reads, performs as a play, or prints this manuscript in the country will certainly be punished.”

Alexander Griboyedov wrote it. Through that one manuscript he drew the gaze of Russia toward himself. As soon as its contents became known, the Tsarist government raged, nobles trembled and officials grew busy. The command “Do not print it!” went forth.

The offending manuscript was a drama, so a supporting order followed: “Do not perform the play!”

It was also a book suitable for reading, and thus came the command: “Do not read it!”

Do not print it! Do not perform it! Do not read it!

Under the mighty Tsarist regime, the point of a pen created such terror. The order remained in force for thirty-three years. The author never saw his work presented as a play. Only thirty-three years after his death was the drama he created performed.

“Do not perform it,” said the Tsarist government, and the country did not perform it. “Do not print it,” ordered the Tsarist government, and no one printed it.

But the command “Do not read it!” flew away like cotton. Everywhere people gathered secretly and read it. Before it was printed as a book, the public had learned it by heart.

The great work was *Woe from Wit*, which the Tamil account calls “Harm Caused by Wit” or “Harm from Mockery.” Its wit truly could cause “harm.” It portrayed the conduct of nobles praised as leaders of the country who lived by tormenting the poor and simple. The attitudes, actions, gait, gestures and manners of those who moved through palaces were all mocked. It explained well that defects in society prevented Russia from advancing.

The work filled the nobles with fear. That is why the Russian monarch prohibited its performance. Its wit brought danger to the nobility and ultimately threatened the regime itself.

***

Alexander Griboyedov could portray the splendid lives of the nobles so aptly because he was born into a noble family and could know that class's way of life closely.

He was born in Moscow in 1795 to a wealthy household. While he was at college, Napoleon invaded Russia in 1812, and Griboyedov joined the resulting war. He later worked in the Russian diplomatic service. Even while holding that post, he remained deeply interested in literature and wrote many works.

He completed *Woe from Wit*, the work that shook Russia, in 1823. It was not printed, but its ideas spread across the country.

In 1826 Griboyedov was arrested on an accusation of being a revolutionary. He was released after an inquiry and returned to the diplomatic service. Although he was freed, the ban upon his work was not lifted.

In 1829 Griboyedov was killed in Tehran by opponents of Russia. Even after his death the prohibition remained. Thirty-three years later, in 1862, the ban was lifted. The book was printed, people began reading it openly, and the play was performed.

Famous actors longed for even a single opportunity to play any role in it. Even today, an actor who has appeared in the play earns distinctive fame and respect.

The manuscript that provoked a government's anger captured a nation's heart and gained a place in world literature. Many of its lines became proverbs.

Such an excellent work was kept from the public forum for thirty-three years by the Tsar's rule. By fearing it so greatly, the Tsarist regime, which treated the strength of the people as mere straw, itself taught the world how much power writing possesses.

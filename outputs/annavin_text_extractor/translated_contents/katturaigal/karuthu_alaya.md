## English Translation

"In Greece, caste divisions among the people ruined the country. Through ignorance and arrogance, the group that considered itself high-born regarded the rest of society as an 'inferior order' and showed discrimination by refusing to dine or associate with them. This brought immense harm to Greece."

The lecturer paused for a moment. Calling to a student who had not raised his bowed head, he said, "Hello, black suit! Pay attention. I am discussing an important subject: Greek history, and especially the root cause of the destruction of that splendid empire. At such a moment, why are you staring at some powdered face in *Movie Mirror*? Attend to the lesson!"

The history professor was indeed discussing an important problem. Why the ancient Greek empire, a land fragrant with art and filled with valour, fell was a historical question that the people of this country ought to know, and one especially important to students. The teacher was explaining this rare lesson skilfully. A mere teacher? A professor! At that very moment, if a student comrade was delighting in the picture of a cinema star whose coloured cheeks, visible not through glass but on paper, had wounded his heart, how could the lecturer fail to become angry? He did become angry.

He thought of confiscating *Movie Mirror* from the student, at least temporarily, but that thought brought a fear with it. Not that the student would create a disturbance; the lecturer was slightly afraid that once he took *Movie Mirror* from the student, his own attention might leap toward its colour pictures! He therefore took no further action, hurriedly departed Greece, and went to the Roman Empire.

There he concluded: "Not only the Greek Empire, but the Roman Empire too, more extensive, renowned, and home to great heroes such as Julius Caesar, suffered immense harm because of the division between plebeian and patrician, lower and upper classes."

After making some general observations, he said: "For a state to endure, a powerful army alone is not enough. The growth of art and epic literature alone is not enough. Expanding commerce and prosperity alone are not enough. Society must contain neither division nor schism. It is the duty of intellectuals to remove such divisions and schisms. Ordinary people and those enamoured of the past will not readily give up caste feeling, clan pride, and ritual observances. Scholars must come forward. Young people in particular must undertake this work."

The students were delighted to see the professor conduct the class in such a rare and engaging manner, combining historical knowledge with a sense of the occasion. The professor rejoiced at the delighted students.

Singing softly but sweetly, "There are no castes, little child," Arunagiri walked out of the college with the gait of Ashok Kumar. His song travelled by special post. There was a girl named Pappa in his class. She belonged to another caste! Arunagiri therefore sent Bharathi as his messenger. On that very day, had not the professor explained how social division, discrimination, and caste cruelty destroyed entire empires? Hearing it gave Arunagiri fresh resolve, enthusiasm, and hope. Had not Pappa too listened to the same lesson?

The class ended. Arunagiri entered the students' hostel. What had he heard in class? That lesson had even given him new determination to win the enchanting Pappa near the doorway. Yet the hostel stood in complete opposition to the lesson.

Had the professor not said that ordinary people, lacking clarity, were unwilling to abandon caste division? Aruna was in the Intermediate class at college. There was Jagath, tossing away his cigarette, singing a romantic song, and opening room number seventeen: he was a B.A. student. Kannusamy wandered outside in a vest, freely exhibiting his well-built body for others to see: he too was in the B.A. class. Thus, room after room contained Intermediate and B.A. students! College students who had learned in class that caste divisions within society were a principal cause of the fall of great powers. They were not ordinary illiterate people, nor people of antiquity, but young bulls who had entered college for degrees.

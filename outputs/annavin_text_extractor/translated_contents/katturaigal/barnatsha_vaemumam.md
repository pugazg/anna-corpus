மட்டுந்தான் தெரிகின்றனவா, அந்தக் கால்களும், கரங்களும் !

## English Translation

"Seven arms! Who counted them, you madmen? Does the great god have eight legs? Are those legs and arms visible only to you? Am I blind in one eye? Have you ever seen the god Pan-ku? Yet you speak as though you have! Believe, if you wish, that there is a noble being who created the world. If you desire a little more intellectual clarity, say that some 'power' must have existed for the world to take shape, but that we cannot discover the nature of that power. Speak in wonder and pay it respect. That much you may do. Instead, you listen to tales spun by all manner of people, claiming that the god Pan-ku created the universe and possesses seven arms and eight legs, and believe them with your eyes shut!

"You worship the earth. When asked why, you say the earth is a god. To you everything is divine: the sun, the moon, and all else. Dull-witted man, do not confuse your mind so! The earth and sun need not be worshipped; they are not deities. Do not forever brood upon heaven either, wasting your time as prey to the pleasure of empty fantasy!"

Thus spoke the great Chinese thinker Lao-tzu. Ordinary Chinese people who had believed in the god Pan-ku for ages trembled and raged. They reviled Lao-tzu as small-minded, arrogant, a blasphemer, and an enemy of scripture. That pure man regarded their abuse not merely as trivial but as the babbling of madmen, and continued to teach his great path. Later, the common people followed his way.

The "way he laid down" was not the Chinese people's final path. Many other great thinkers appeared after him to cleanse religion of its stains and bring renewal. At every renewal, however, ordinary people were bewildered: they opposed it, mocked it, raged, and cursed. Yet they could not stop the renaissance.

"You deny the god Pan-ku. Very well, then how did the world arise? There must be a first cause. What is it? There must have been a primal being. What do you say of him?" As their anger dissolved into bewilderment, ordinary Chinese people began to ask these questions. Lao-tzu replied that before the earth took shape there must have existed something formless, profoundly mysterious, inexhaustibly powerful, and distinct. It might be called the mother of the world. Not knowing what name to give it, he called it Tao.

Tao means the Great Movement.

Lao-tzu rejected the imaginary god Pan-ku. Yet because science was still embryonic in his time, he could not explain how the world came into being. He perceived and said only that some immense power had operated and that the earth was its result. He did not bind his conjecture to imaginary tales. He asked people to abandon the imaginary conception of God inherited from earlier generations, and he succeeded.

Others did not rest content with the light he shed. Confucius, another great thinker to arise in China, sought and offered clearer explanations of human life. He neither accepted imaginary stories about heaven, earth, celestial light, and stars, nor hesitated to tell the Chinese that these were petty paths. Rather than worrying excessively about deities, he said, people must think about human beings and work for humanity. The theists of his day regarded his inquiry too as atheism. Today Confucius is placed among the great sages, but when he first offered his conclusions, bewildered common people regarded him as a great sinner.

When his disciple Tzu-lu asked, "How should one serve the spirits?" Confucius is said to have replied, "You do not yet know how to serve human beings, and you ask how to serve the spirits! Serve humanity first!"

Even cleansing religion to this limited extent was extremely difficult then, so dense was the fog covering people's minds. It was an age in which fantasies were believed to be truths.

Not only in China but in countries throughout the world, before scientific explanations became available, ordinary people explained every sight and event according to their limited abilities. They treated stories invented by various persons as philosophies, built many forms of worship and ritual upon them, and believed the result to be their religion.

In Peru there was a tale that the sun had a moon-goddess. Today we can call it a tale, but for ages the people of Peru revered it as religious truth. They believed thunder and lightning to be messengers of the sun. There was a god named Huancan, giver of immortality, and another named Apachita, giver of mercy. Such ranks of gods existed in Peru. People performed blood ablutions to remove sin, and even practised human sacrifice. It took a long time for Peruvians to realise that this "religion" was not born of reason.

The Indigenous people of Canada worshipped a creator god called Olgans Oki. This god supposedly shot an arrow to split the earth. The earth opened, and men and women were born from it. That was their sacred story. Women worshipped the sun-god, while warriors danced in the evening to worship their lord. No one in Canada worships Olgans Oki today; he has been forgotten. Yet there was a time when Oki Mahesan was celebrated as the true God.

People passed such fabricated tales from generation to generation, multiplying one into ten. Poets later embellished those same stories and gave them artistic polish. Many epics were composed, and consequently the stories gained still greater prestige.

When persons with poetic sensibility turned some tales exchanged by ordinary people in markets and rest houses into epics, those tales became fit for mansions and palaces. Fabrications became accounts of divine glory. Temples were erected, and the characters named in those tales were installed there in state. Temple-car festivals, mantras, and rituals arose. Supported by those captivated by epic beauty, mere fabrications became sacred stories endowed with grandeur. Later, when intellectual growth led some to doubt or mock them, those people were condemned and punished.

Many stories that lacked the polish of epic beauty gradually disappeared from people's minds. We can observe this truth here too. Vishnu is said to have taken ten incarnations, each for a different purpose. Yet most people know the stories of Rama and Krishna much more clearly and extensively than the other eight. They know little of incarnations such as the Fish, Tortoise, Boar, and Dwarf. Even those with some knowledge do not praise, worship, or honour Matsya and Kurma in the same manner or measure as Rama and Krishna. Large temples belong chiefly to these two incarnations; the other eight hear little drum and fanfare. According to devotees, all ten are incarnations of the Supreme Lord. Why, then, do most people know little of eight?

Epics about the Rama and Krishna incarnations arose in Tamil, the language of the majority here. Through the epic-polished stories called the Ramayana and Bharata, Rama and Krishna were introduced to the people generation after generation. There is no Kurma-puranam or Matsya-ayanam in Tamil. That is why those incarnations did not gain an equal place in the popular mind.

Likewise, because no great epics were composed for Chinese gods such as Pan-ku or Peruvian deities such as Apachita, people could forget them easily and quickly. Divine tales protected by the armour of epic could be demolished only after the most powerful assault by the forces of knowledge. Countless thinkers had to lose their lives merely to forge that intellectual weapon. The noble people killed in that assault are beyond counting. Men who memorised fabrications, earned the distinction of being blessed by God, and assumed the title of guides to salvation tortured clear thinkers.

Those thinkers did not necessarily demand that religion, belief in God, morality, or noble character be abolished. It was enough for them to say that stories presented as divine sports confused the mind, that investigation exposed them as farces, or that their incidents were obscene. Calamity would immediately descend upon the speaker. Priests would howl, kings threaten, and the people rage; prison or the gallows would summon those "atheists."

Even a devotee who continued to praise God and cried, "Lord, what injustice is this!" would receive the label of atheist and the ruler's punishment merely for doubting fabrications that had acquired the dignity of religious texts. Such religious frenzy has greatly diminished today, but only after how many ablutions of blood!

Only now do people here attach some philosophical meaning to every story, whether it fits or not. Thirty years ago, merely rejecting a story was enough to seal one's fate. Our people still do not properly know the efforts and sufferings of thinkers in other lands who challenged fabrications bearing names such as "divine sports" and "incarnations," stories that had flourished in mansions and royal palaces, and tried to dispel the people's delusion. They have not been told.

Those noble people said that one need not believe the sun has seven arms and eight legs. Their countries once possessed ranks of gods: one god for wealth, one for strength, one deity for daylight and another for night, one god for agriculture and another for food. They tried to drive these temple-honoured divine hosts from the minds of their people, and lost their lives. We have not yet shown them to our country.

If people knew that magnificent history, cunning minds and exploiters could incite them with all the uproar of ritual observance, yet they would refuse to attack rationalists. In many lands there once existed numerous Pan-ku deities as embodiments of the primordial sound. They no longer exist because an intellectual revolution intervened everywhere. Ordinary people here do not know this because no one has informed them.

"Our literary teachers must deeply examine the Puranas and sacred fictions from a new perspective. We need many Bernard Shaws who will investigate Christ and the Bible in the spirit of modern science and discover in them an imperishable newness." These words appeared not in a journal of the Self-Respect Movement but, a few days ago, in a journal of the nationalist camp that regards the Self-Respect Movement as a cyclone.

We need a Bernard Shaw! Valmiki and Vasishtha, Kamban and Kalidasa are no longer enough. Connoisseurs who memorise and recite fabrications are not enough. A Bernard Shaw is required! Why?

Even orthodox believers must now preface their old tales by saying, "Listen, I shall tell you something; believe it or not as you please..." The speed of the times has done that much work.

Among themselves, orthodox men and lovers of the Puranas lament: "When we tell the sacred tales handed down by our elders, today's youngsters mock them, say they will not believe them, and ridicule us. They ask whether we measured Hanuman's tail, counted Indra's eyes, or saw Rambha dance. What can we do? They demand a reason and meaning for everything, and challenge us to show evidence!"

First they panic, then they are bewildered, and finally they themselves laugh. "Yes, as these fellows say, the Puranic tales insist upon being unbelievable. What are we to do?" they reflect, and laugh.

Seeing this situation, even those who rage against the Self-Respecters now think the Puranas must be rewritten with new meanings. They seek Bernard Shaws! We are somewhat pleased that they have at least opened their minds this far. Are we not witnessing this change in our own lifetime? The age that threatened, "Fabricated tales must be believed with closed eyes; the questioner, doubter, or mocker is an atheist and must therefore be crushed," is fading and dying. Those who once knitted their brows now stand smiling and attempt to give the Puranas new meanings. They summon Bernard Shaws, in a cry of doubt!

(Published in the Dravida Nadu issue of 5 December 1948.)

## English Translation

The gates of Paris stood wide open. German forces poured in without obstruction. Hitler invaded and easily conquered France. France fought, but could not defeat Germany. Even when Hitler's invasion began, France did not tremble into action. Its armies moved, swayed and ran, then finally stopped. France surrendered and its people became German subjects.

This is one of the strangest episodes of the Second World War, an inexplicable puzzle to the world. At first no one believed France had fallen. Victory and defeat are ordinary in war, but France did not appear to possess the conditions of a defeated nation. Distress lay in Germany, while prosperity seemed to fill France.

After the First World War, France stood foremost among leading countries. Clemenceau's voice shaped many lines of the Treaty of Versailles and echoed through the peace conference; rich America and famous Britain stood behind France. It acquired new territories, developed colonies and benefited from defeated Germany's tribute. It possessed a mighty army, admired navy and enviable air force. Yet this golden-age nation fell beneath a foreigner's feet in a week.

The conqueror could match none of France's advantages. Despite seemingly invincible forces, wealth and a victorious tradition, France lost. The hero became slave to the worthless; the milkweed became king in the rose garden. The land of the Maginot Line became a hunting ground for German fanatics. France, which had offered the world liberty, equality and fraternity through revolution, became enslaved.

The cause was neither inadequate armed force nor lack of money, but France's internal condition. While war approached, political magnates in theatres, pleasure halls, drinking rooms and private chambers mocked it beside intoxicating cups and alluring companions. They ignored a blaze capable of enslaving the country because the real foundations of democracy were dying.

Parties multiplied like mushrooms and party fever rose. Personal rivalry and jealousy became daily political festivals. Who was greater, who should hold power and receive more honour became political doctrine. Bribery, lies, fraud, conspiracy, exploitation and deception became teachers of politics. Corruption ruled, confusion occupied the crown, and weak programmes and purposeless parties replaced public welfare. Anyone wielding a stick became a collector of dues. New ministries appeared daily with lifeless plans. Political order had collapsed and ability declined. How could France avoid defeat?

The fear now reaches its height that France's tragic history may be written in India. Delhi is becoming Paris, a sign of danger. Connaught Circus rivals Parisian shopping streets, and India resembles France before the German invasion. The country once praised as Bharat Devi, golden land of the Ganga and abode of wisdom, where British departure supposedly promised abundant happiness, now accumulates the same mountains of political pollution.

Public dissatisfaction grows while rulers discard what they said before taking office. Once Nehru became minister, employment went to relatives. His sister became India's representative abroad; close kin, favourites and friends received posts and foreign appointments. The foreign service filled with his circle, many lacking competence. Vijayalakshmi Pandit could not even secure an interview with Stalin in Russia. Asaf Ali returned from America for want of suitability, only to become Governor of Orissa. V. P. Menon temporarily filled another assignment, being acceptable to both Nehru and Patel.

Princes who understood Patel's smile retained status as provincial governors after accession. The ruler of Bhavnagar, Patel's favourite, became Governor of Madras. A Railway Minister's relative became a manager under a newly fashioned arrangement, and railway administration became a realm of favour.

Economic administration swayed like a creeper. Ministers came and went: Sir Shanmukham resigned, Matthai left, Bhabha stayed briefly and Deshmukh now struggled. Under Jairamdas Doulatram the Food Department filled with disorder and faulty files. Instead of sending an incompetent minister home, they made him a provincial governor. Munshi followed and became not minister of food but minister of famine.

The Education Department became merely a series of art conferences. Azad's closeness to Nehru, rather than fitness for the portfolio, kept him there. Health Minister Amrit Kaur spent little time in India, frequently going abroad for her own health. Thus ran the central administration, and provincial ministries followed Nehru's example. If the father robs by torchlight, the son robs in daylight. Nehru began the error; those following his path completed it.

Prakasam's own accusations against Madras ministers suffice: jobs to relatives, cement and petrol permits to intimates, children sent abroad to study at government expense, bribes and payments for favours. If ministers acted so, subordinate officials came to treat every signature as the opening of an iron safe. When the elder brother jumped eight feet, the younger jumped sixteen.

The article then cites a departing Congress figure, whose OCR-damaged name appears as Karand, identifying two English expressions for the week's political accounts: one meaning preferential treatment of relatives and another denoting growing interference in administration. The first had no adequate native word because it was presented as a dictionary gift from British rule to present rulers. Declaring both practices to be increasing, he left Congress.

Those were precisely the conditions that made France unable to resist Germany. We fear the same environment in India. Several had left before him, but their departure from a party did not remove the evils from the country. Many abandon Congress to prevent Delhi becoming Paris, yet have not closed the route by which India may become France. They must realise this and act together.

Karand left the party; Nehru announced withdrawal from committees. Congress stands beneath Nehru's shadow and lives by his name. Gandhi called him his heir. He is praised as learned, high-minded, worldly, India's heart and Asia's light. Nehru is Congress and Congress means Nehru. Yet he withdrew from the Central Parliamentary Board and Working Committee.

He may return and people may sing that the crisis has ended. That is not the issue. Congress has decayed enough to make Nehru himself threaten withdrawal. Before us stands a broken veena, snapped bow and curdled milk. A stringless veena gives no music; a broken bow cannot take a string or shoot an arrow; curdled milk cannot regain its taste. Congress has become so. Nehru is one clot in the curdled milk, perhaps a large one; Tandon is a drop of cream floating in it.

Their quarrel forced Nehru's announcement. Tandon asked how Nehru could retain Kidwai, who had opposed or left the party, as minister. Yet the central cabinet included Ambedkar, who did not belong to Congress; Sir Shanmukham had served, Matthai was not a Congressman, Bhabha came from outside their camp, Deshmukh was no Congress devotee, and Sir Gopalaswami had long opposed Congress. Why then did Tandon raise a dispute specifically over Kidwai and Jain?

An ordinary observer might call it anger that they had left Congress. A sharper one asks why a president tolerating long-standing opponents as ministers refuses yesterday's Congress members today. Tandon invoked party rules and called Nehru's act wrong; Nehru did not release his furrowed brow. The truth lies beneath: today's flame grew from long-buried personal rivalry.

Tandon and Nehru both came from Uttar Pradesh and rose in the same party. Tandon had led the provincial Congress, served as legislative speaker and briefly as minister. Nehru, the “prince of Allahabad” and cherished son of Anand Bhavan, was born, raised and lived there too. They were like two kings of one country, but one rose to become a mountain while the other took long to move beyond the province. Tandon repeatedly failed in attempts to become Congress president.

Though party colleagues, they differed deeply. Tandon's beard and Nehru's cap symbolised more than appearance. Admirers called Tandon *Rajarishi*, comparing his persistence and anger to Visvamitra; hostile Congress journals now called the same “royal sage” a plague. He championed Hindi imperialism, stubbornly pressed Hindi numerals, spoke communally of Muslims, stood as uncrowned king of orthodoxy and commander of the varnasrama army, and defended antiquity and Hindu social degradation.

Provincial obsession filled his mind though he spoke daily of an undivided Bharat. He did not condemn Uttar Pradesh's grave betrayal of Dravida country: during famine and starvation, when parents sold children, that province sold rice to the South at an extortionate price exceeding a crore of rupees. Nehru too remained silent, avoiding conflict with Tandon over provincial actions despite being Prime Minister. They leap when foreign countries withhold food but do not condemn one province exploiting another. Tandon's provincial attachment thus exceeds his concern for a single India, even as they abuse us for seeking our own country.

Nehru had never willingly accepted Tandon's leadership. Two camps formed during the Congress presidential election. Patel marshalled forces for Tandon, while Nehru favoured Kripalani. Opposing Tandon therefore meant opposing Patel, so Nehru tried to conceal the camps and oppose indirectly. Patel completed every arrangement for Tandon's victory, and Tandon became president.

Thereafter Tandon watched for chances to realise his old ambition and acted contrary to Nehru whenever possible. Nehru and Patel's relationship steadily weakened; newspaper reports of visits and long conversations dwindled and disappeared. At this point Rajaji was summoned to Delhi as minister without portfolio. Whether Nehru or Patel caused it matters less than that both needed him. Though without a department, he effectively received the portfolio of carrying messages between them.

Rajaji was no ordinary man but a descendant in statecraft of Chanakya. If Nehru and Patel became close, his messenger department would have no work. They remained too distant to build a bridge, and Rajaji became their envoy if not their bridge.

Then Patel died. Tandon knew his supporting strength had broken but believed the Congress presidency still enabled him to confront Nehru and perhaps create another Patel. Though newspapers did not report them, conciliatory conversations occurred between Tandon and Rajaji. Rajaji became Home Minister.

Anyone who understands this older history can see why Tandon launched a major agitation over Kidwai. The stated issue is not the true cause; it is the latest form of the long struggle between Tandon and Nehru. The milk curdled and the pot broke.

## Translator's Notes

The Tamil OCR source is retained verbatim. No publication date is visible in the surviving source. Several lines and names in the administrative-resignation section are badly damaged, especially the person rendered “கரந்த்” and two English political terms that OCR reduced to unusable strings; the English describes their recoverable meaning without inventing spellings. “பால் திரிந்தது” is translated “the milk curdled,” preserving the article's metaphor for Congress's irreversible decay. Historical accusations, personal judgements and administrative claims are translated as the author's contemporary polemic and have not been independently fact-checked. Period forms such as Tandon, Kidwai, Kripalani, Rajaji, Bhavnagar and Asaf Ali are normalised only enough for identification.

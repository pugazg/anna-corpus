## English Translation

1938! How can the Tamil people ever forget it?

It was in that year that the anti-Hindi struggle took place in Tamil Nadu—a struggle that left the two heroes Thalamuthu and Natarajan dead and sent thousands into cruel imprisonment.

Before the moral struggle began, an anti-Hindi conference was held at Kanchipuram on 27 February 1938. Sir K. V. Reddy, a distinguished scholar from Andhra who later served as Vice-Chancellor of Andhra University, presided. Krishnan Nair, Kerala's scientific luminary, inaugurated it.

The conference stood as a sign that not Tamil Nadu alone, but Dravida itself, opposed Hindi. The cry raised then by those departed great men still resounds in our ears. Even at that time they answered many questions being raised today.

To draw the attention of many members of the growing Dravidian camp to those answers once more at this moment, we reproduce portions of their speeches.

The present government's stated reason for imposing Hindi is that a sense of unity must arise and that one language is needed for intercourse among the provinces. Sir K. V. Reddy offered the following view:

> If any reason can be offered for making Hindi compulsory, it can only be that a common language is needed to unite the provinces and princely states, create a sense of unity among the public and develop the feeling that all belong to one country.
>
> That single reason may at least appear plausible at first sight. Looked at in this way, however, the scheme is revealed as one introduced for a political purpose, not as something connected with educational principles or aims.
>
> Is a common language indispensable for developing patriotism, strengthening national unity or bringing people together? History declares the opposite truth.
>
> Consider Canada. It has a federal political system, and both French and English have continued to be spoken there since the Act of 1791.
>
> Both French and English are used in the Quebec legislature. Ever since constitutional changes were introduced, the requirement that both languages be used has been demonstrated in practice.
>
> Consider South Africa. Two peoples live there and two languages exist. English remains the language of the English, while Dutch is used by the Boers.
>
> Switzerland contains three peoples speaking three languages, but it remains one country and possesses national unity.
>
> Under the federal constitution completed in 1874, German, French and Italian were established as national languages.
>
> Considering all these examples, it becomes clear that India does not need a single common language. Nor is it practicable to spread and compel a new language throughout provinces and princely states where hundreds of languages are distributed.
>
> Let us next consider the position that will arise for this province in its relations with the others if Hindi is introduced as a compulsory subject.
>
> When the Central Government of India was in Calcutta, Bengalis occupied the whole government establishment. After the capital was moved to Delhi twenty-five years ago, the official establishment passed into the hands of Punjabis and residents of the United Provinces.
>
> Madras, however, has never had anywhere to turn. Northerners regard the Madras Presidency with contempt.
>
> They have inflicted many injustices upon Madras. During the Montagu–Chelmsford reforms, for example, under the scheme called the Meston Award, the Madras Presidency poured 350 lakhs of rupees into the Central Government every year.
>
> The present Congress government does not understand how extensively language shapes human thought.
>
> People's mental development follows their language. Children's thought develops, and ideals arise later in life, through language, art and knowledge.
>
> Where is Tamil, and where is Hindi? Can the two even be compared? The *Tolkappiyam* is a work of grammar. A language requires a grammar only after it has attained substantial development.
>
> This shows that Tamil had already attained exalted development two thousand years ago. Besides being ancient, Tamil is a language filled with charm and beauty. Hindi can never approach its feet. Dr Burnell says that “in developing a language suited to the composition of poetry, Tamil is unequalled even by Sanskrit.”
>
> We of the Madras Presidency possess no influence in Delhi, where our feelings are not respected. Bengal in the past, and Punjab and the United Provinces today, have treated us as their slaves.
>
> We must rise for equal rights. We too must possess a right and share in the administration of the Government of India, just like the other provinces.
>
> If we yield to Hindi, it can only be understood as another example proving that we are meek people who submit to the northern provinces.
>
> The assertion that the majority of Indians speak Hindi is unsupported and utterly absurd.
>
> Bengalis pay no heed to Hindi. Muslims oppose compulsory Hindi and have always preferred Urdu.
>
> Kannada speakers and Marathis in the Bombay Presidency strongly oppose the imposition of Hindi.
>
> Sindh, Assam and Punjab too will inevitably oppose such a method.

Our ancient saying declares that a person has three mothers: the mother who bore him, the motherland and the mother tongue. One who neglects his mother betrays her; one who fails to honour the mother tongue becomes a traitor who hands it over.

Let us call the betrayer of the mother tongue a Judas, who betrayed Jesus for a little money. Jesus was killed because of Judas's conspiracy.

Yet Christianity endures. May the Dravidian languages likewise endure!

Some threaten: “If you do not study Hindi, you will find no work tomorrow.” Krishnan Nair answered them—not today, but nineteen years ago. He also explained which language would be needed if such a language were indeed necessary, and why Hindi was in no way fit for the purpose. Here is what he said:

> It is claimed that Hindi will help educated and unemployed young people from the South travel north and find work. As stated earlier, by the time southern youths reach the age when they go north seeking employment, they will have forgotten the Hindi imposed upon them in school during childhood.
>
> Even if we assume they have not forgotten it, there is no guarantee that graduates educated in Hindi will obtain employment in the North. The claim that southern B.A.s will find work there if they know Hindi is utterly absurd. Anyone who reads Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru's address, “Education and Unemployment in India,” delivered at a meeting of the East India Association in London on 31 May 1937 under the chairmanship of Lord Lothian, will understand this well.
>
> English made it possible for Congress to meet in many places and for numerous conferences on social and industrial research to be held at important locations throughout India. Even in many Congress-governed provinces, proceedings in the legislatures are not conducted in the mother tongue; they are conducted in English.
>
> For various reasons, English has come to our country as an honoured guest. It possesses the capacity to remain permanently. It is a developed and attractive language in which even the most subtle ideas can be expressed beautifully and clearly. It is a language where many priceless and varied jewels, such as science, shine.
>
> They say English has destroyed patriotism. Without properly understanding the doctrine of patriotism, they cry, “English is a foreign language; let us cast it away.” There is no reason to demand, contrary to public wishes, that Hindi—which prevails only in one part of the country—be made the common language of all India.
>
> Nor will that project succeed. The Madras ministers differed over whether cricket could be adopted as a national sport.
>
> Some ministers thought cricket was a foreign game and should therefore be hated and rejected. My friend, the Honourable Dr Subbarayan, strongly differs from his colleagues and argues that cricket should be adopted as India's national game. I wholeheartedly support Dr Subbarayan. Likewise, although both are foreign languages, I trust he will accept my argument that English should be made India's common language.
>
> English is our honoured guest; the mother tongue is sweeter than our own mother. However developed, attractive and beautiful a foreign language may be, it cannot become the language of ordinary people.
>
> English cannot obtain the place held in this province by Tamil and the other Dravidian languages.
>
> The mother tongue is the language in which our mothers sang lullabies and raised us, and in which they caressed us as infants. It is the language of our babyhood—the language that helped us form and express thought as children before our ideas became clear.
>
> However much scholarship we later acquire in a foreign language, it is the mother tongue that helps us depict emotions such as love, affection, anger and grief. It is the language we use upon our deathbed to communicate the agony of death. Nothing equals such a mother tongue.

*Dravida Nadu*, 13 October 1957

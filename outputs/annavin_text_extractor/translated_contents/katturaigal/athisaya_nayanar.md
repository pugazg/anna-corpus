செய்தார்களாம். தோழர் மனோகர்லால் அவர்களை அவ்வாறு
செய்ய: வேண்டாம் என்று தடுத்தாராம். சில நேரங்கழித்துப்

## English Translation

Among the orthodox, we find a belief that if an object is to acquire a greatness and fame it does not naturally possess, one must imagine either that it has powers contrary to nature or that it causes supernatural events. Though this imaginative tendency may be found among religious traditionalists in every country, it has existed from ancient times in a particularly abundant form among those living in the Indian subcontinent.

To confer greatness upon God and religion, devotees and religious preachers felt compelled to connect many rare events beyond human powers with God and religion. Hence every devotee claims that his own gods performed particular miracles and possess particular powers, thereby seeking fame and greatness for them. This is why wonders imagined to have been performed by every god have been fashioned into Puranic stories and set in circulation among the people.

People generally observe closely the natural events occurring in their surroundings and gradually define their characteristics. As far as possible, they learn the fundamental causes connecting one natural event with another. When a cause cannot be known, they begin to conjecture what it might be and become eager to discover it.

It is precisely at this point—where people stand bewildered because they cannot identify a cause—that religious preachers bring God forward. They declare that mysterious powers found in God are the underlying causes of events whose causes cannot be discovered.

When an object is placed before the people as possessing powers beyond the ordinary, wonder arises towards it. Wonder produces pleasure; pleasure produces desire; desire produces affection; affection produces attachment; attachment produces enslavement; enslavement produces fear; and fear produces devotion. All these arise in succession. Believing and worshipping whatever is exalted as being beyond human capacity and natural power has become an inherited custom.

Many miracle stories said to have been performed by God are presented to the people to confer greatness and fame upon him. In the same manner, to create distinctive fame and greatness for particular people, the orthodox have long attached supposed miracles to them.

Whenever they wish to separate and elevate a few individuals above ordinary people, they undertake to assign many extraordinary events to those individuals and do not fail to seek methods of doing so. Some people use abilities that are present in them to an unusual degree to deceive others, make others imagine that they possess immeasurable power, declare themselves great beings equal to God, and arrange to be called *mahan*, *rishi*, sage or Alvar. People without the power to think believe all this. Have they gained any benefit from such belief? No.

Abilities found only in small measure among many people may be concentrated in a few. Because particular individuals possess rare and great abilities, they naturally acquire a name and reputation and are praised as great people. Yet the foolish practice of attaching miracles to them in order to increase that fame was widespread in this country and continues even now.

Gandhi created political awakening in the Indian subcontinent. He led the army of the freedom agitation, encouraged the people in the struggle for rights, laboured to remove divisions among them, and counselled them on living well. For these reasons people throughout India came to love him.

Gandhi possessed a great power to draw the people's affection towards himself. Therefore the country called him a noble man, a guide, and “Mahatma,” meaning a great soul. Owing to these genuine causes, his fame rose here to heights others could scarcely imagine.

Some people have now abandoned the worthy task of establishing Gandhi's reputation upon good, elevated and appropriate reasons. They have entered the orthodox world's “sacred service” of associating many miracles with him. Perhaps the single belief that large numbers of people remain ready to believe anything described as miraculous encourages them to continue.

An “extraordinary event” in which a black cobra circled Gandhi's memorial and paid homage was reported in *The Hindu* on 1 June 1949. The person announcing it was comrade Manoharlal, watchman of Gandhi's memorial structure at Rajghat in Delhi.

One recent morning, Manoharlal reportedly went before dawn to pay homage at the memorial. A large black snake entered the structure. Without disturbing it, he watched in wonder to see what it would do. The snake slowly made one circuit of the memorial and stopped near the steps leading up to it.

By then the sun had appeared and people had arrived to worship. Many tried to kill the snake. Manoharlal prevented them. After a while, the snake moved quickly away, entered a nearby bush and disappeared. Manoharlal supplied this account to the United Press of India, and it appeared in newspapers such as *The Hindu*.

The ordinary event of a snake coming to Gandhi's memorial has been made into a miracle. This is exactly what orthodoxy has done from generation to generation.

The report leads people to imagine that the black cobra understood Gandhi's greatness; that it felt a duty to worship at the memorial of such a man; that it knew worship meant circling once and standing in reverence before it; and therefore that it came there that morning for this purpose.

When two unrelated incidents coincide by chance, people identify the coincidence and present it as a wonder. This habit exists not only in this country but elsewhere too. The proverb about “a crow sitting and a palmyra fruit falling” describes precisely such a conjunction.

A snake may have come to Gandhi's memorial. That is an ordinary possibility. The snake and the memorial are two separate things. When they happen to meet, a miraculous fiction is constructed.

It is proclaimed in a manner intended to make people believe that the snake came to see Gandhi's memorial and that the event deserves religious devotion as a miracle. Yet snakes sleeping upon tombs, dwelling among memorials, lying coiled around them, leaping across them or resting curled before them are neither impossible nor unheard-of events. We see such things not merely at Gandhi's memorial but near many ordinary tombs throughout the country.

Consider how profoundly ignorant it is to dream that a snake arriving by chance came specifically to pay homage. Exaggerating such events confuses people who lack the power of reflection and prompts them to believe.

Earlier, when Gandhi travelled on foot through Noakhali, a snake reportedly appeared on the path, raised its head, looked at him and moved away. In recounting it, people said that the snake had come to behold Gandhi.

Even when we ourselves walk along, we commonly see a snake raise its head, look towards us and then run in another direction and slip away. When something that happens to ordinary people also happens to Gandhi, how can it become a miracle?

In many homes, while a child sleeps in a cradle, a snake climbs up, looks at the child, and sometimes slides down and escapes without doing anything. Should we say the snake blessed the child? Children thus “blessed” by snakes do not all become great saints in later life; they remain very ordinary people.

When many people sleep in beds, snakes come and lie near their feet. When the sleepers rise, the snakes look towards their faces and flee. Should we say the snakes performed morning worship for every one of them? Snake handlers make snakes move in all the ways they command. Must we call all these miracles? Examine how irrational it is to regard such occurrences as supernatural.

Why had the “Snake Nayanar” who has now come to worship at Gandhi's memorial never visited earlier? More than a year has passed since the memorial was built. Why such a delay? Why did it feel no desire to see Gandhi while he lived or when he died? Can anyone even promise that this “Snake Nayanar” will henceforth return regularly to pay homage?

People are enamoured of miracles. Therefore the merchants of orthodoxy manufacture them.

Some months ago, *Swadesamitran* printed a picture and praised a “Rooster Nayanar” said to have come to worship Murugan at the Kachaleeswarar temple in Madras. Through their credulity, people treated the rooster's conduct as a miracle. Where is that “Rooster Nayanar” now? In whose stomach, and as what kind of curry, did he attain salvation? Did *Swadesamitran* later show any concern for him?

Like this “Rooster Nayanar,” there is the “Bandicoot Nayanar” that comes every day to lick and drink the oil upon the lingam; the “Bat Nayanar” that deposits worms and urine on it; the “Ant Nayanar” whose congregation swarms upon the honey; and the “Frog Nayanar” that shelters beside the lingam during the rainy season.

We would not be surprised if *Swadesamitran*, *The Hindu* and other publications created many such Nayanars. Their conduct remains at that same level.

Like the Snake “Nayanar” that has now “seen” Gandhi's memorial, many “Nayanars” are said to have been visited or worshipped by animals. They include the horse, spider, eagle, monitor lizard, monkey, jackal, ant, insect, honeybee, beetle, elephant, lion, peacock, swan, pigeon, owl, pig, tortoise, hare, indragopa insect, parrot, curlew, snake, scorpion and rat. All these creatures are said to have attained liberation.

As for Gandhi, whenever he counselled the people of the country, he repeatedly said that he was not an incarnation, that he performed no miracles, and that he was only an ordinary human being.

The people can advance through intellectual clarity. Stories about such Miraculous Nayanars can never create a path of progress; they can produce only superstition.

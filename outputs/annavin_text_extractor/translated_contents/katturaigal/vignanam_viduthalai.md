## English Translation

“People praise the telephone and television as marvels of science. But do not the torpedo and tank destroy human beings today? Are they not science's wicked children? People boast that the aeroplane and ship are wonderful scientific inventions, but can anyone deny that explosives and poison gas are also its products?

“Science serves destruction. Society's abode of peace is not found in science's dwelling; tumult, ruin and injustice issue from it in volleys. Science is used for the degrading cruelty of destroying humankind by inventing ever more efficient instruments of devastation.”

Many honourable people here speak this way, shed tears and call scientific development the ripening of an age of destruction. Science has advanced elsewhere in the world; it would be proper to regret that it lies here like a person paralysed. Instead, we find the belief that denouncing science is a sign of genius.

The glow of a firefly-like lamp, a dog barking at the sound of footsteps, a farmer worshipping his plough, his wife sleeping at his feet, ponds in which buffaloes wallow, shrines to Madan and Katteri, the neem tree beside them, children rolling beneath it and the rustic refrains they sing: these are considered praiseworthy here. Such village life is presented as the ideal, and fulfilment of that ideal has been given the sacred name Rama Rajya.

There are people here who say that Westerners, spending their time in intellectual work, have become impoverished in the spiritual field of discovering God. Eminent religious exponents lament that Westerners may have developed science but do not know even the alphabet of Vedanta; that they regard this world as all-important and have forgotten to think of the next.

A coward calling a brave man a brute, a pauper abusing a rich man as a slave to murderous wealth, or a householder forced to wrestle with his wife beginning to sing the purity of renunciation may all be called thoughts suited to circumstance. Likewise, when some people here belittle and abuse science, their denunciation merely conceals their own condition. It resembles the fable of the fox declaring that the fruit was sour.

No other body of knowledge has helped relieve the world's suffering as science has. From medicine for disease to mastery over wind and sea, and from hearing at a distance to seeing afar, science made the difficult easy and the rare accessible to everyone. It removed foolishness such as the magician's threats, priestly fraud and alleged demonic activity. It dispelled fear and broke the bonds of slavery fastened upon knowledge. That glory belongs to science.

To condemn science because weapons employ scientific methods resembles a crooked-minded man striking his head against the granite intended for a fortress and then wailing about the stone's cruelty.

Science may produce many discoveries, but it is not responsible for the disposition of those who use them. The strings of a veena yield music beneath a musician's gentle hand; an ignorant person may use them as a noose. The fault belongs not to the veena but to the fool. Calling science evil is like proclaiming the veena a monstrous instrument that drinks life.

Science assumes responsibility for inquiry, the advancement of knowledge, discovery and the reshaping of an age. It is unjust to burden it with blame for destruction caused by a madman's conduct.

Science states clearly: “I am the friend of knowledge and servant of inquiry. The result depends upon the character of the person who uses me.” There are frenzied men who maul the lips they wish to kiss and scoundrels who beat with a stick the woman they ought to embrace. Likewise, madmen use for destruction the science that should serve human happiness.

Does this occur only with science? No, it occurs with everything. Human intelligence, ability, beauty, education and wealth, as well as every object of nature, can be made either beneficial or harmful.

Indeed, if we tried to conduct the world through nature alone without human intelligence, nature would cause more suffering than pleasure. A natural flow of water may become a great flood and destroy a town. When human knowledge and science build a dam, bridge it and direct its course, that same water irrigates fertile fields, nourishes beautiful gardens and supports a graceful life.

If nature is left unrestricted and allowed to wander freely, it produces more injury than comfort. Science does not share this defect; it possesses the great virtue of transforming even nature's cruelty. Young people must undertake the work of welcoming it.

Nobody advertises science as omnipotent. Nobody says that its conclusions cannot change or be corrected. It grows and possesses a generous nature.

Those who idly argue that explosives and poison gas are scientific products must answer another question. In the creation of the supposedly all-responsible Supreme Being, why do we find a flower garden and the venomous serpent hiding within it; an unfailing river and the crocodiles rolling in it; honey and scorpions; flowers and filth; fragrant eaglewood and civet beside storms and volcanoes: objects producing both comfort and injury?

Why would the Supreme Being give sinful thought to human beings and then send teachers of the virtuous path? Why create poverty and afterward create charitable lords? Why create orphans and later incarnate as their saviour? If not an atom moves without Him, why did the Creator fail to restrain his own creation so that injustice and wrongdoing did not arise?

Science neither claims omnipotence nor calls itself the final conclusion. It describes itself only as one part of human intellectual growth. Is it just to direct against science the abuse that ought to be cast upon those who use its products for evil purposes?

The Viceroy recently expressed this idea very well at a scientific conference. Speaking to scientists, he explained science's greatness and necessity and the valuable work it must perform in the Indian subcontinent's future.

A group here considers itself intellectually eminent but finds science bitter as neem. Its geography, history, mathematics and knowledge belong to ages long past. It declares science the enemy of wisdom, a delusion opposed to divine grace, and an obstacle to happiness in this world and the next. It is not content merely to say this; it ensures that not a trace of the old-world smell leaves the minds of ordinary people.

This policy profits the group. Its comfortable life, dominance and elevated position stand upon that foundation. It is therefore unsurprising that it spends intelligence repairing its methods so they can withstand each new attack.

In a country where even elementary education has not spread adequately, it is unsurprising to hear lazy sayings such as, “Would they say it if it did not exist?” and street-corner objections such as, “Does this fellow alone know everything?” In this setting, *Swadesamitran* even criticized indirectly the Viceroy's praise of science.

It said in substance: “Science is being used destructively today. Had it been used properly from the beginning, would the world now be in this condition? Many of humanity's sufferings could have been eradicated.”

The outcome of scientists' service depends upon the methods and objectives of those who employ it. The guilty are therefore not scientists but those who misuse their priceless intellect. To condemn science for that is foolishness.

We ask those virtuous people who blame science rather than the users who turn its products toward disaster: how many people live according to the commands of religion, praised as the path to happiness in this life and the next, as morality and righteousness, and not merely ordinary speech but divine revelation?

Religious tales adorn Siva with the destructive glory of burning the three cities to ash through the light of his eye. Expositions of Vishnu's stories proudly recount which demon he destroyed in each incarnation. They swell with pride over the vast armies Murugan is said to have annihilated.

The Supreme Being ought to have been portrayed as the spring of love and grace and the home of righteousness and reconciled wisdom. Instead, religious advocates celebrate destruction upon destruction, one deity competing with another, one declared greater, and one seeking another's aid.

When these people malign science, an innocent form of new knowledge, it is not merely impudence. It is anger that science has exposed their barren boastfulness in broad daylight.

The Purana claiming that Vishnu took the boar incarnation in Hiranyaksha's age and rolled up the earth like a mat is a cosmic falsehood, a fabricated scroll constructed by a selfish group to plunder people's property after first plundering their reason.

That fabrication could remain a channel filling the priestly group's stomachs only while scientific knowledge failed to spread among the people: knowledge that Earth is spherical, one among many planets, with a measurable extent, particular resources and a knowable history of formation. Once the new knowledge that Earth is round arose, the Puranic deceiver had to pack up his fraudulent livelihood.

Likewise, all the falsehoods invented here to deceive ordinary people will scatter as mist before sunlight when science develops. Fear of that result makes those who treat selfishness itself as scripture condemn science.

The world of knowledge will not heed them. The time has arrived for the life of fraud to be burned away and truth to shine. Science will not permit the orthodox zealot's livelihood to continue. It will become a sledgehammer against fortresses of selfishness and cut down the dominance of those who rise against knowledge. It will remove mental delusion and relieve people's suffering.

Science will continue transforming everything: drawing gems from earth and pearls from sea, fashioning vessels from wood, changing what lies in the womb of matter, and ascending into the sky. The nature of planets and their relationships has become an elementary lesson. The kinds of disease and methods of cure have reached the ordinary shop. America and Africa have been brought into our rooms.

Thayumanavar sang that the rutting elephant of the mind could be brought under control, but finally said that suppressing thought and remaining still was difficult. Yes, thought truly cannot be suppressed into stillness here.

Poets spent, and still spend, their time singing of the deer and axe held by the Lord of Chidambaram and arranging disputes between Siddhanta and Vedanta. They speak arrogantly as though nothing else is needed.

From then until now, except when borrowing English knowledge, remedies for illness have remained Mariamman and neem leaves, worship of the nine planets and gifts to a Brahmin. From then until now, people have been told that Earth is flat, the sun's chariot travels with magnificent speed, Saturn rides a crow, Chandikeswarar is deaf, the goddess appears directly to Sankaracharya, sighting Garuda on Sunday brings great benefit, seeing Arudra grants fortune for six births, fasting on Vaikuntha Ekadasi assures boons, and bathing in a tank erases sins accumulated from one's great-grandfather's time.

That old intoxication has not cleared. Westerners, refusing to suppress thought, mastered wind and sea, subdued disease and derived nature's benefits. That learning is why science has become the West's wheel of command.

Here, Puranic storytellers govern with a tale that, in the divine rule of old, cow and tiger drank at one riverbank and a divine discus would roar forth and sever the tiger's head if it misbehaved. In reality, we see a wheel that lets tiger and cow drink at the same place even in an ordinary circus. We need the scientist's wheel of command.

The chief reason this Indian territory remains a foreigner's playground and hunting forest, and the reason we remain slaves to slaves, is that our people allowed their thought to leap toward Sivaloka, trusted miracles and abandoned substantial knowledge.

If this condition of slavery is to change, the road to liberation lies only beneath the vigorous rule of science.

May science flourish!

*Dravida Nadu*, September 1944

## Translator's Notes

The closing scan prints the date as `9/1/44`; the OCR renders it ambiguously as `9//44`. The translation therefore gives September 1944 without asserting a precise day. `விடுதலை வீரன்` is literally a warrior or champion of liberation.

The article distinguishes scientific inquiry from the moral purposes of its users. Its references to tanks, torpedoes and poison gas reflect wartime publication. The Viceroy and scientific conference are not named more precisely in the surviving text. `ஆக்ஞாசக்கரம்` means a wheel of command and also evokes Vishnu's discus; Anna contrasts mythic control with technological command over nature.

## English Translation

“Those who will not abide by the decisions taken by the party may leave. There is no place here for those who will not follow them. They may withdraw!”

From whom has this warning come? From the present president of the Congress, Mrs Indira Gandhi!

It is said that, before receiving the title of president, she belonged to the group desired by the Congress organisation. Her present speech also carries the scent of what the “leftists” said in those days.

We do not know whether she is going to change the course of the “official Congress” or whether she too will travel along that same road. Nevertheless, this speech is welcome, if only because such words have now been uttered.

“Those who dislike it may go!” After Nagpur, differences of opinion have arisen within Congress. Some with clear convictions have come forward to reveal their position. The lady president expresses herself with such firmness; speed can be heard in her voice. Those who now issue statements, object and complain that certain measures are improper could have demonstrated their opposition at Nagpur itself. Those whose thinking was pure and who possessed the resolve to say what had to be said should have acted in that manner. Perhaps they wondered how they could speak while standing before Pandit Nehru, whether their voices would be heard and what he might think. Whatever the reason, no one asked why and no one spoke.

The resolutions were passed at Nagpur. Those holding views contrary to Pandit Nehru remained silent at the time.

People had said that someone would oppose them and speak differently. But once Nehru stood up, he may have wondered where all that talk had disappeared and may even have exulted. Many must have hidden themselves thinking, “Why should we get involved? Why should we oppose Nehru?” Rather than invite trouble and hear his abuse, they must have considered silence a thousand times better.

Nehru likes everyone immediately to accept whatever he says. Once they have agreed, he will not easily refuse whatever they later request, and they can get things done through him. Even resolutions passed with passion and enthusiasm may gradually be enlarged, reduced, bent or twisted by him for those he favours. All he requires is that no one raise an opposing voice and say, “That is wrong; it must not be done,” when he first makes a proposal. Afterwards, Nehru will do anything for them; those who move closely with him know this secret and know how to make him change his mind.

Whether resolutions are implemented or are working is not even particularly important to him. At every conference he must appear the supreme hero and establish his “victories.” That is all.

Did not emperors of old think that an opponent must first surrender and fall at their feet, after which he might become a friend or even a commander within their realm? Nehru's manner may be described as almost the same.

Those familiar with this characteristic of the Pandit may think: “Let us say yes now. What loss can result? Tomorrow we can speak to him privately and have it corrected.”

There are also those who ask whether their only task is to nod. Some among them speak courageously, and some have indeed spoken. It is to them that the lady president now issues her warning: “Remain if you can say that you accept it; otherwise leave!”

The issue now is not who stood where in the past. That much is clear from her speech.

One feels inclined to ask her: “Madam, how did those who possessed minds opposed to Congress policies enter Congress in the first place?” How did these irritated opponents arrive? Did they slip in while the party was unguarded, or were they praised, celebrated and invited inside during election season? The truth is that eight out of ten were brought in amid ecstatic songs of praise and numerous encomiums.

The bearers of the Pandit's palanquin in every locality, including major leaders, cast nets everywhere and dragged people in merely to fill the basket. The lady president ought to learn whom they brought in and from where.

Victory! Victory! The election must be won somehow. Most people admitted to Congress were recruited under the rule: whoever he may be, will he spend money and can he win? If so, drag him in.

There was no requirement that they understand Congress policies. It did not matter if they had never touched khadi or had opposed Congress until the previous day. Could they win an election? If yes, bring them in. They might regard socialism as a discordant noise. Let it be. What of it? They might consider the paths held sacred by Congress no better than dust. Never mind. Are they suitable fighters for an election? Bring them in, garland them and welcome them. This is what occurred in many places.

Did Nehru not know that all this was happening? He knew. What did he do after knowing it? Did he condemn or restrain his commanders? He too cared only that the news should come that a greater number had won the election, no matter what happened.

During the ten years that have passed, the Congress “agents” celebrated. They seized people, dragged them in and celebrated victory with chests thrust forward.

We are not saying that no new person should ever be admitted to Congress. We merely wish to point out that people with no concern whatever for Congress socialism or its policies were dragged in and inserted.

Will this strengthen Congress?

Many imagined that they were hiring Congress for themselves.

Many wondered why they should oppose Congress when it pleaded with them to join, bestowed praise upon them and stood ready to provide every facility.

Those waiting for an opening through which to enter, and for an opportunity to pocket whatever they could seize, thought: “Here is a great party built by martyrs, possessing the influence created by people who performed distinguished service. When it is available, why hesitate? Let us enter and acquire it.”

If the lady president wishes to know the details, she need not conduct an inquiry elsewhere. It would be enough to examine once the list of MLAs and the histories of Congress members in municipal elections. Everyone knows that they were selected not because of devotion or affection towards Congress, but because they declared that they would win.

The election authorities may ask, “How can we drive away those who wish to come into Congress?” One cannot reject everyone who comes. But did these people arrive in ordinary times? They were invited and installed. That is all. They do not recognise khadi, know nothing and understand nothing except elections. Such people have now gathered inside the Congress tent.

Is this the way to develop a party? Can its principles grow through them?

If Indira Gandhi examines the record of those who have worked for the party, difficult though that task may be, she will learn that Congress has become a useful gambling board for self-seekers and gamblers.

The lady president is angry with those opposing the Nagpur decisions. Is she not the party president? No one will think that wrong. Yet openly voicing condemnation and saying that one dislikes something is not evil; it should be welcomed, heard patiently and answered appropriately. No danger arises from that.

Today Congress does not have many people of that kind. More numerous are those who, instead of speaking openly, devote all their attention to secretly digging pits. At the right moment they know whom to praise and how to pull and push people, and through this they profit in many ways. Political morality, however, cannot remain unharmed. Such people have injured the very organisation of the party.

If the lady president truly desires to strengthen the party, no benefit will come from pouncing upon opponents or issuing warnings. Only people such as Professor Ranga will thereby leave. Her attention must be directed towards those who were “made into Congressmen.”

What decision will she take about them?

She can take no decision. If she begins identifying such people and expelling them all, the danger will be grave. Her father may find himself without admirers or friends and may suffer, perhaps even become angry!

12 April 1959

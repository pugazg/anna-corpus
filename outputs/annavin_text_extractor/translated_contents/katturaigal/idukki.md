## English Translation

The first of May is the workers' New Year's Day.

From 1 May 1946 to 1 May 1947, the world of labour has completed another year. Bearing many attacks and hardships, it grows and advances.

The struggles conducted during this year shook all Tamil Nadu. They revealed both the workers' growth and strength and the economic order that is decaying away.

The all-India railway strike planned for the final week of June 1946 deserves mention: when the central union decided to abandon the strike, it was the South Indian Railway that remained in its support.

There was the complete strike by fourteen thousand Buckingham Mill workers when the white proprietor refused their demand to dismiss fifty-three Gurkhas. Then came the postal and telegraph strike, conducted with complete success in the Madras Presidency; the powerful agitation of ten thousand Madras sanitation workers that began in August; and the South Indian Railway workers' strike, a magnificent struggle waged with their blood that a future historian of labour must inscribe in letters of gold. There were also the strike struggles at Tuticorin and Coimbatore.

Beyond these stood the Travancore labour uprising against repression and the struggle of the Tanjore cultivators, who held their heads high and declared, “Whether he is our landlord or the ruler commands it, we will not give even half a measure of paddy from our share.”

Such great struggles did not remain within factory walls. They reached the gatherings of elementary-school teachers. From government employees to policemen, people took part in strikes. The strike had a proper year's work!

The Prakasam ministry inaugurated last May did not shine. Now it beholds this ministry of the intermediary Omandur.

Strikes have become the ministry's nightmare. The situation does not appear to be improving, and fear keeps growing. If Tamil Nadu's impoverished economy persists and Congress continues to absorb the blows meant for the factory rulers, conditions will worsen.

Even after the sun of freedom rose, the trumpet of righteousness sounded and songs of conciliation rang out, the darkness in the worker's life did not lift. Trying to crush strikes through repression without examining the worker's poverty is equivalent to blinding oneself.

The ministry and the newspaper world vividly portray the harm caused by strikes. But what causes the strikes? Their easy answer is that opponents of Congress, troublemakers and Communists incite them. Financiers give this answer during strikes, and honourable ministers give it at question time. The whole truth is not merely that workers have been incited, but that they live in conditions capable of being incited.

Scolding a tenant who complains, “The wall of the house is collapsing,” will not keep the wall upright. The fault lies in having neglected repairs for many years. Ignoring cracks in the wall while planning another storey and announcing that plastering will soon begin is not merely foolish; if carried out, it is dangerous. To suppose that a surface coating will suffice once cracks appear is a failure to understand the condition. Not only the builder but we who live in the house must recognize that the wall collapsed because it lacked a sound foundation.

The economic history of the Madras Presidency reveals a condition worse than outward appearances suggest. One worries over its ruin as a doctor might say: “He complained of a stomach ache. I gave medicine for two days, but it did not improve. Finally an X-ray showed his stomach full of lesions. Only immediate surgery can save the patient; otherwise the disease alone will survive.”

**White domination**

The white man came to this country in the guise of trade. For more than two hundred years, the Indian subcontinent became his exclusive estate. Tamil Nadu and the other regions fell victim to imperial exploitation. After two centuries, Tamilakam had been turned into a dry wasteland.

In the Indian subcontinent imperialism employed a method of double profit. India provided a commercial market in which finished goods from English factories could be sold, while the raw materials required by those factories were also sent from here. Western governments obtained more profit than they expected from ruling colonies.

Suppose scented soap comes from France to Madras. The finished soap comes from France, but France does not produce the groundnuts used as its raw material. A French trading company buys groundnuts in South Arcot and ships them to France. The same ship returns to Cuddalore carrying scented soap. The Cuddalore trader who sold the groundnuts for a quarter anna per measure then pays a quarter rupee for a cake of soap.

Tamil Nadu's economy was ruined by this pincer attack. Small industries declined and were crushed. No large factories were built to replace them because imperialism did not permit it. Export, import and commercial power fell entirely into English hands, destroying the country's wealth. Famine and poverty became ordinary sights. There was not the slightest industrial growth.

Although the north faced imperial restrictions too, factories gradually arose there. Indian-owned textile mills came first, while jute and tea industries grew with British support. Imperialism allowed nothing to develop freely, yet growth occurred little by little. Here, even seed was not scattered.

Despite restrictions, many northern industries began lifting their heads in the twentieth century. Banks and insurance companies appeared and worked vigorously to establish factories. One may ask why industry did not grow in the south at the same time. Imperial restraint was joined by pressure from northern capital, and southern attempts were plucked out at the sprouting stage. The one or two factories that appeared as rarely as the flower of the fig tree belonged to white capitalists or were mortgaged to northern moneylenders. No independently functioning industries arose.

Even when war broke out in 1939, this was the position. Cotton was available and yarn was spun in Coimbatore, but the yarn was sent to Bombay to be made into cloth. Leather was tanned in Madras but not manufactured into goods here; it went to Kanpur or Liverpool. This condition continues.

The great war that began in 1939 subjected imperialism's political and economic structure to a severe test. As the war intensified, many British factories producing consumer goods were redirected to war matériel. What they produced was needed domestically, while naval warfare and enemy sea defences also prevented shipment to India.

Northern Indian capitalists consequently gained first place in the Indian market, and goods from northern factories commanded high prices. The south had nothing capable of competing. Nobody should imagine that imperialism allowed this out of goodwill. Trade becomes imperialism only after selling off goodwill first. Necessity compelled the concession. Indian production of consumer goods increased, but India was not permitted to manufacture war equipment. Such was imperialism's fear.

The Government of India bought clothing, food and other supplies for the defence forces from Indian capitalists, producing enormous profits for them. It need hardly be said that more than ninety-nine out of every hundred of these military contractors were northerners. Capitalists sought profit by charging high prices even for goods essential to the people. Food, firewood, cloth and similar necessities therefore suddenly became unavailable to the public.

As industrial expansion turned lakhpatis into crorepatis, the number of labourers also increased. The source introduces statistics for worker growth between 1939 and 1944 to show that this wartime expansion benefited the north far more. The surviving OCR of the table preserves only fragmented headings and no reliable figures. Its stated conclusion is that the Madras Presidency had been crushed industrially and that opportunities for restoring it had not been allowed to produce growth.

**After the war**

A boy employed in a shop grows up, learns the tricks of trade, saves a little money and opens another shop. At first the proprietor disregards him, but later reflects on the loss caused by the boy's departure. The proprietor has grown old and rheumatism has disabled him, so he can no longer work hard. Seeing the former shop boy struggling nearby with a few goods, he summons him and says: “Why should there be a quarrel between us? When we already have one shop, why another? You take care of this shop. Let all receipts and expenses pass through you, and we shall take a share of the profit. I shall change the shop's address into your name.” Thus the old owner invites the man who is becoming a new proprietor.

British imperialism has reached such a position in India. It has entered into an agreement with the growing northern capitalist group. The shop's signboard has been changed to “Independent India.” The old proprietor has not yet moved away from the cash box; instead, he has found a suitable man to guard it.

Northern Birlas and British Nuffields will now conduct business together. It has begun already. What follows will be the exploitation of the Madras Presidency.

Had factories multiplied during the war, or had wartime factories been converted to peacetime production, or had the enormous wartime expenditure been redirected toward new factories and useful industries, the suffering of unemployment could have been reduced. In the north at least, factories may relieve some distress through expanding trade. Madras remains severely backward in industry.

The source next presents a badly damaged comparative table of factories. Recoverable entries refer to glass, leather, tea plantations, coal, aluminium, paper mills, chemical products, engineering, cement and a tannery, with Travancore named beside aluminium. Though the figures and column alignment are no longer reliable, the paragraph's declared purpose is to illustrate the contrast between northern industrial development and the south's scarcity.

Besides those listed, many different factories flourish in other northern provinces. Bihar contains what the article calls the largest iron and steel works in the British Empire, and it produces eighty percent of India's mica. Mica is reportedly present even in Nellore, but that knowledge has not been put into practice. The north produces and grows. Bihar also has thirty-three sugar factories and cement factories.

Assam, home of the tea industry, is said by the damaged OCR date to have had 1,726 tea gardens and sixty-three tea farms.

The south lacks the north's industrial growth. Even its one or two factories remain in others' hands. Their machinery is worn down from prolonged wartime operation. The capitalist class, despite earning vast profits, is ready to close the mills. This is the first stage of the unemployment crisis.

Those released from various occupations and those returning from the war will number more than five lakhs in the Madras Presidency. If each supports a family of four, the article calculates that twenty lakhs of people will be left without support. The rulers hesitate to protect them and behave as though they do not notice.

Many ministers understand neither the stagnation that strikes cause in industry nor the anguish of unemployed people who cannot find work. During an economic crisis these problems will assume a terrifying form. Those who have come to power must confront them beforehand. Later, even if they wish to do so, they will not be able to face them.

*18 May 1947*

## Translator's Notes

`இடுக்கித் தாக்குதல்` literally denotes an attack that squeezes from both sides, hence “pincer attack.” The article applies the image first to imperial extraction of southern raw materials and sale of finished imports, then to the combined position of British and northern Indian capital.

`படி` and `காலணா` are historical units of measure and currency. `அத்திப் பூத்தாற்போல` means something appearing with extreme rarity. `லட்சாதிபதி` and `கோடீசுரர்` denote possessors of lakhs and crores. “Nuffields” renders the damaged `நப்பில்டுகள்`, apparently referring to British industrial interests associated with Lord Nuffield.

Both statistical tables have suffered major OCR loss. On Image 6, almost all numerical rows are absent. On Image 7, headings, figures and columns have collapsed into fragments. The prose conclusions and recoverable labels are translated, but no missing statistics have been invented. The source reads Assam's date as `1989`, which is impossible in a 1947 article and likely an OCR error; it is therefore described rather than silently corrected. The final arithmetic appears as `29 லட்சம்` in OCR but five lakh workers each supporting a four-person family implies twenty lakhs; the English states the calculation while the Tamil source preserves the printed OCR reading unchanged.

The article's regional economic claims and statistics are translated as presented and have not been independently validated.

## English Translation

"All right, my boy! You speak in an unbroken torrent and never tire, but what is the use? Speech! Speech! Your friends proudly call your speech a heroic trumpet call; others say that someone is merely shouting. Apart from that speech, what else is happening? Have you ever done any concrete work? Have you ever thought of doing it? Will that thought arise at least now?" Out of friendship, and therefore rather sharply, a comrade named Ramasamy of Kagaveri put this question one day a year ago to K. V. K. Ramasamy, a comrade in our movement from Rasipuram.

Rasipuram is a small town in Salem district. Kagaveri is a hamlet next to it.

"Concrete work! Have you done any concrete deed apart from speaking in a loud voice?" This question began to torment our friend K. V. K. Ramasamy. What that friend said was true. We spend our time only in useless speech. Enthusiasm, fervour, awakening, effort and labour are all being wasted. This state must not continue. Something concrete must be done; useful work must be performed. Our aim is to spread sound knowledge in the country and illuminate the people's reason. Ramasamy of Rasipuram decided that a concrete undertaking founded on this aim had to be carried out. Today that thought stands before us as a beautiful, spacious and large school.

In a tiny village near Rasipuram, he erected at a cost of about thirty thousand rupees a school building for the primary education of children. He handed it over free of charge to the District Board and thereby rendered rare service to schoolchildren. Neither that tiny village nor the many villages around it possesses so large a building, especially for a school. The place may be called a town, but it is really countryside. With help from friends and villagers, he built this structure in such a place.

Its opening ceremony was conducted splendidly at four in the afternoon on the tenth, before more than ten thousand people. Comrade Ramasamy, principal of Salem College, presided. I opened the school building. The Tamil teacher and world worker unveiled a portrait of the noble Gandhi, and Pulavar Kuzhandhai unveiled a portrait of Periyar.

The college principal said: "Members of every party agree that education is deficient in this country and must hereafter spread more widely. Unless the people's intellectual condition advances, the country cannot attain a new condition. Those who have come to rule must perform this noble duty without interruption. They say that they desire to do so but lack sufficient money. What use is it for rulers to speak like this? They came to power after promising good government, saying that the evils in the country would disappear under their rule and that the people would live happily. If, after beginning to govern, they now say 'there is no money' whenever anything is proposed, what are we to do?

"This is the same old song the British sang. At that time these men told the people that if foreign rule were removed and self-government established, happiness would follow. Now they themselves sing that old song. Is this proper? They say prohibition has caused an enormous financial loss, that revenue has fallen by several crores of rupees, and therefore there is no money to spend on education. I do not know whether the prohibition scheme is a success or a failure. But I will say one thing: it is wrong, a very great wrong, to proclaim prohibition of liquor while imposing prohibition of reason. Until reason spreads, this country will command no respect in the province. If our country is to obtain a high place in the world, the rulers must build temples of knowledge in every town and every village.

"Education Minister Mr Avinashilingam says he has three desires. When I say three desires, do not imagine desire for land, gold and women. Those are not what the minister means. He is said to desire nationalism, Gandhism and Tamil. He tries to fulfil all three simultaneously, and his scheme becomes one great confusion. He has one scheme for one moment and issues another order the next day. In an exemplary land such as Russia, more than ten languages are national languages; no one language has been made the compulsory common language everywhere. In Switzerland, four languages possess the status of national languages. Are these men going to build a greater state than those countries? Why then is compulsory Hindi needed here as the common language?

"Primary education has not spread in the country, and they say there is no money for it. Why incur fresh and needless expenditure on Hindi in this condition? Hindi, they say, is for the nation; Tamil for the country; and English for the world. If three languages are loaded upon young pupils in this way, can they bear the burden? I ask why this pointless trouble is necessary. I have about twenty-five years' experience in education. I therefore say that the government's language policy is wrong and that its thoughtless, unconcerned attitude towards education is gravely wrong. If they ask what else can be done, my answer is this: if you cannot do it, leave office and hand the government to those capable of running it. One must not remain in charge of government and fail to perform the work required by duty.

"This country must have compulsory, free primary education. No amount spent on it would be wrong. Money must be found and sources of revenue must be discovered; that is the responsibility of the rulers. The public, too, must recognise how terribly deficient education is in our country and offer whatever help they can to spread it. The school about to be opened here has been founded, at great expense, through the generous help of the public. This disposition to perform useful work must grow in the country. Knowledge must spread."

I then spoke:

"Ordinarily, ministers are invited to conduct the opening ceremonies of schools such as this. I, however, am a man whose name appears on the list prepared by ministers of those to be arrested. Inviting me to open this school is therefore a somewhat daring act.

"I convey my gratitude to the friends who built so large a school in this small and beautiful hamlet, and who invited me to perform the noble task of opening this educational association. I had grown somewhat weary of public life and lay dispirited; this fine duty gives me fresh enthusiasm. I have always been especially concerned that we need concrete work, useful service and institutions that establish our principles, and that our comrades must train themselves to create them. Many times I have wondered and grieved: are we without the opportunity or ability to do anything concrete? Do we lack strength, or have we failed to feel the necessity?

"We know that irrationality and ignorance hold the country in a very powerful grip. We told the country and continue to tell it. Yes, we must continue that work. But should those of us who perform this noble task be satisfied with that alone? Why should we not establish educational associations and primary schools here and there so that knowledge may spread, irrationality may be defeated and ignorance may disappear? Their form and benefit would be visible; they would help each locality; and they would be institutions founded on our lofty principles. Who but those of us who seek the spread of knowledge would feel concern for establishing such institutions, schools and reading rooms in every hamlet and village? Are we not the people who repeatedly insist that the country needs sound knowledge? We ourselves must establish the educational associations through which that knowledge spreads.

"I have long felt pain at the thought that we were not doing such concrete work. When friend Ramasamy invited me to open the temple of knowledge he had built in his tiny village at a cost exceeding thirty thousand rupees, it was like honey flowing into my wounded heart. Here, I thought with delight, they are acting; concrete work is taking place; speech is not stopping with the form of sound but taking shape as action.

"I have known friend Ramasamy for many years, yet I truly did not expect such a concrete undertaking from him. He speaks rapidly, takes part in all party meetings with enthusiasm and speaks rather sternly. I thought that was all. I never imagined he would become a man of action, change the mind of an entire village, obtain its help and carry out such a work. I have now seen this attractive building and take pride in my friend's great service. Its size and quality would do credit to any city.

"This is a rural educational association. My friend, the king of comedy N. S. Krishnan, sings amusingly about a village school in his *Kindanar Kalakshepam*: 'It has one window - the termites have eaten it.' Village schools are often just like that, with cactus and weeds, snake mounds and a ruined well in the backyard, a collapsing compound wall and a leaking roof. In such a place they install the 'Queen of Education' upon her throne! Can education spread there? By contrast, this temple of knowledge is the loveliest place in this village. It is the common property of the village, the wealth of people of character, and the training ground that will shape the country's future. I thank the friend who created it and all those who helped him.

"Our country is one among the many countries of the world, but in education it is more backward than any of them. It is said that only ten or twelve people in a hundred are literate. Among even these, if we count those who have not forgotten what they learnt, those who use what they learnt, those who believe its benefit should serve public welfare rather than only themselves, and those who wield knowledge as a weapon in a righteous struggle, it is difficult to find even one or two in a hundred. If this is the condition of the country, how can we gain respect in the world?

"It is not enough to point only to the mountains, forests, rivers and fields of our land. It is not enough to offer only its ancient greatness, poems and spectacles as evidence. If the world is to regard and honour our country as one of its leading nations, the level of education must rise. We need compulsory, free primary education, and knowledge in general must spread. Those who rule must make this their chief responsibility, duty and ideal.

"It is useless to repeat the refrain that there is no money. Chief Minister Omandur Ramasamy is a villager, a friend of the people, free of intrigue and self-interest, and a simple man. All that is true. But what use is it if, while holding the office of Chief Minister, he sings a mournful *Mukhari*? He says sorrowfully that a proper educational scheme for the country requires forty-six crores of rupees, that the government has no money, and asks what he can do. Is this what we should expect from a Chief Minister? Any priest at a shrine on the village boundary can say as much. The former advisers said it; under the white rulers we grew tired of hearing that there was no money. Is he too to repeat it? Is the office of Chief Minister needed for that?

"When money is lacking, those in power must investigate why it is lacking, where it exists and how it can be obtained. Imagine fields at one end of a village lying cracked and dry while a great lake stands in another part. Would the farmer sit on the bank and weep, 'There is no water in the field! O lake, give water! Come to the land! Cool the cracked fields! Make red paddy and grain flourish'? No. He would cut channels, lead the lake water into the fields and irrigate them. A farmer who understands the matter would act. A useless crowd would claim to summon rain through prayers to Varuna and deceive fools.

"Does the Chief Minister not understand this? Very well, he and the government have no money. Why should they not discover where money does exist and how it may be used for education and the country's welfare? Is that not their duty? The Chief Minister merely laments that he needs forty-six crores and asks what he can do. Is that right? Is money truly absent? I invite the Chief Minister: come, and I will show you the places where money exists! It disappears only when education seeks it. For what other purpose has money ever been unavailable? There is money to compensate zamindars. There is money for the durbars of mitta mirasidars. There is money when ministers increase their salaries, for aircraft and for festivals. Only education cannot obtain money. Who does not understand this secret?

"I tell the rulers: if you cannot find and spend money on education, and do not know the way, entrust the education department to the Dravidar Kazhagam for five years. At the end of those five years, see what condition education has reached in the country. We shall demonstrate where money comes from and in what great quantities. We shall not even go to ordinary people or rich men to ask for it. We shall appeal to the very 'gods' whom these people accuse us of telling others not to trust. We shall explain our grievances to those gods and obtain their help.

"We shall go to the Lord of the Seven Hills, enduring the pain in our hips, and say: 'Lord of Tirumalai who cures incurable karma! The people of the country do not have sufficient education, and its rulers have no money to spend on it. Therefore, Lord, temples of knowledge must be established everywhere. Graciously give us money, O stainless one! Devotees have laboured with divine money and presented you a diamond crown costing thirty lakhs of rupees. Give us that crown; many colleges can be founded with it. The brilliance from a diamond crown is indeed no ordinary thing, but if it is converted into money and spent on education, the light of knowledge spreading through the country will be brighter than the glitter of your crown.'

"The Lord of Tirumalai cannot refuse. He might disregard the words of those worthies who consider themselves protectors of theism, but he cannot reject ours. He will think: 'These are good children after all. Why were they abused as atheists? Their request is just. Should not the light of knowledge spread through the country? Only when knowledge spreads can people understand me. Are knowledge and I different? Have not learned men called me the God who is knowledge? They ask for my diamond crown in order to spread that knowledge, so I must give it. Is money that cannot be used for this good work really money? Am I merely a stand bearing the weight of ornaments, or an advertising doll helping people with equal measures of devotion and money introduce themselves to the world? Shame! Knowledge deserves support.'

"Having resolved so, he will give not only the diamond crown but his many ornaments and heaps of gold, and lovingly send us away saying, 'Take them! Spread knowledge! Establish educational associations in every village.' Five years will be enough. The Chief Minister asks for forty-six crores; we shall generously raise it.

"We shall go to Chidambaram and say: 'O Lord of the Cosmic Dance! The people have earthen vessels in their homes, while yours has tiles made of gold. In this land where poverty dances, there is no money to spend on education. O Lord Rudra, give us those golden tiles.' We shall ask and receive them. Madurai Meenakshi, Sriranganathan, Kanchi Varadan, Kachchi Ekamban, Kalathi Easan and Aruran: how many such deities possess lakhs and crores? We shall create a mature means by which that money reaches education. As channels carry water from a lake to the fields and make crops flourish, we shall release the treasury locked up in the name of God and spend it on the primary task of opening the people's eyes of knowledge. We shall bring a new condition, strength and radiance to the country.

"Are the rulers ready to accept this challenge? If they lack the ability to act in education, let them hand the department to the Dravidar Kazhagam for five years and inspect the country afterwards. They say there is no money. It is not money that is absent; courage and resolve are absent. Where there is a will, there is a way.

"The Chief Minister appeals to the public's spirit of service, charitable feeling and generosity. A few days ago he spoke movingly: the government has no money to spread knowledge in the measure and manner the country needs, so those who possess money should give charity to this worthy cause and offer the gift of education. The man who governs speaks with emotion. But before him many people more learned than he - Vedantists, Siddhantists, Alvars and Nayanmars - have made even more moving appeals. That is not the service we expect of him. Many people can conduct religious discourses and preach without a monthly salary or a ministerial office. We do not need a Chief Minister for that; it is not the method that will open the iron strongbox.

"Even if charity and compassion grew in more hearts and to a greater degree, that alone could not accomplish the country's necessary public works. Compassion does not simply keep growing. The feeling takes shape according to surroundings, associations and calculations of income and expense. Anyone can test this. A person standing at a bus stop with money in his pocket gives the first beggar a coin before being asked, grieves over the unfortunate man's condition and wonders whether there is no way to redeem such people. Moved by feeling, he melts. The second beggar asks and receives a coin. The third asks, and the man pretends not to notice; if someone else is watching, he gives with irritation on his face. He curses the fourth as a dog or devil, and the person beside him joins the abuse. That is all the heart can bear. Can we trust this charitable impulse to establish and continuously operate schools?

"The Chief Minister appeals to charitable men while forgetting the many places where wealth lies covered in moss. Let him summon charity as an ally, but he must first undertake a fascinating inquiry: what is charity, what kind should be performed, and what kind do people today regard - and have been taught to regard - as good?

"Our people are deeply imbued with the belief that deeds done in this life serve the next, and that charity performed here earns merit and gives benefit in the upper world. The Puranas and epics have fed this idea. Ask why charity should be done, and people answer that it is to secure benefit in the world to come. Charity has become a price paid here for a place and comfort in another world, a strange form of trade. Old texts also explain what counts as charity: building temples, performing consecrations and chariot festivals, renovating shrines, building rest houses and shelters, and giving gifts to Brahmins. These are the acts people have largely accepted as charity. The texts explain not only charity and its rewards but also who is a worthy recipient, and say that giving charity to Brahmins alone produces religious merit. This idea has been implanted through the Sastras, Puranas and epics. Consequently our people can imagine charity only as a gift to Brahmins.

"Thinking thus, our kings and wealthy men performed gifts of cows and land and conducted temple consecrations. As a result, the Brahmin community could live without labour and subjugate other communities. The Chief Minister now asks people holding such a harmful and socially destructive conception of charity to help education and give so that knowledge may spread. Will they abandon their existing belief? Will they decide that what he says is the true nature of charity? Only then will they help educational associations.

"They must indeed think anew. For that to happen, they must understand that their previous belief is wrong: the doctrine that what is done here is for the next world, and the belief that Brahmins are earthly gods and that gifts to them produce merit, must be rejected. The old blind faith must disappear for new knowledge to arise. What has Omandur Ramasamy done, and what will he do, to remove it? He must explain that charity does not mean a fee paid to the man carrying *darbha* grass or the serving of a ritual feast. It is neither a bribe paid here to obtain pleasure in the upper world nor a commercial transaction. Charity means virtue, and virtue means duty. But that would become Self-Respect propaganda. Will the Chief Minister acquire such courage?

"The culture of the ancient Tamils did not permit charity to become a commercial device. They were not 'merchants of virtue who perform deeds in this life for the next.' They regarded virtue as the tracks and signs marking the path taken by good people. Foreign speakers and people of a foreign civilisation who came here in the intervening age degraded charity from that pure state into a method of trade. If this truth is explained and the people's mistaken idea of charity removed, the Chief Minister may obtain some of the help he expects. Yet even that will not be enough.

"Every month festivals are held in our temples. People with some means and income spend whatever remains in their hands on these festivals. During the month of Purattasi, trains are overcrowded by whole families from villages going to Tirupati at a cost of five hundred or a thousand rupees. Calculate the travel expense alone during that month. After returning, there is the further expense of treating illnesses brought back from the holy place, and the cost of goods purchased along the route. If all this money were not wasted on such purposes, those families could improve their living conditions or respect the Chief Minister's appeal and contribute to educational associations.

"But just as people regard charity as a way to pleasure in another world, they regard visits to holy places and attendance at festivals as meritorious acts. They therefore waste their surplus money in this manner and even borrow to do it. So long as this belief remains, how can our people answer the minister's emotional request for contributions? They will grow weary and ask how many different demands they can satisfy. There is reason for that weariness.

"Suppose someone sees this school, becomes eager to establish a similar educational institution in his village, and approaches wealthy people to collect money. What will happen? One of them will say: 'Money certainly ought to be spent on education. It is a good work and must be done, but I have no means. Last month Guru Kuppu came for the consecration of the Bhuvaneswari temple, and I sent three hundred rupees. It was the goddess's affair; could I say no? On Friday Vedantachariar came because his daughter was to be married. Could I refuse a Brahmin affair? I gave him fifty. Then there is the regular expense for Monday worship in our local temple. Besides all that, this year there was one great expense: they are making a silver chariot for the Kamakshi Amman of our village. I paid a thousand rupees. I struggled and bargained, asking for it to be six or seven hundred, but our Ellappa Mudaliar insisted he could not accept even half a paisa less than a thousand. What could I do? He is a great man whose word cannot be opposed. Since all these expenses have become enormous, I cannot do much for the good work you propose. Take these five rupees.'

"Only if this condition changes can forty-six crores be found. What is the minister doing to change it? Apart from imposing Section 144 wherever the Dravidar Kazhagam conducts the propaganda of reason needed to change this environment, what has he done?

"Money lies immobilised in religious institutions. The people's money is continually wasted in large sums on festivals. The wealthy possess a mistaken conception of charity. All these obstacles lie in the Chief Minister's path. The important question is whether he can cross them. The basic meaning of charity must first be changed. The tendency to waste the people's money on useless purposes must end. Immobilised wealth must circulate in the country. Then not merely forty-six crores but many crores more will be freely available for education.

"The duty of a government is not merely the accountant's task of listing income and expenditure. It must say: this essential service requires this amount; we have devised these means of obtaining it; we have stopped money being wasted in these ways; we have begun these new revenue-producing undertakings; and this much new revenue is now available. Those who govern have a duty both to say this and to act upon it.

"Even after money has been found for education, we cannot say education and knowledge have arrived. The educational plan itself must be radically reformed. We need education that leads our people into the realm of knowledge; education that enables them to understand the state of the world and explains the greatness of science and the distinction of industry; education that cleanses the stains in their minds, removes the degeneration caused by distinctions of caste, religion and clan, and gives them the strength of heart and integrity to demonstrate in action, rather than merely in speech, the ideal that all are one community. We need an education of knowledge that removes illusionism, mental delusion, the means by which arrogant men make a living, and blind ideas, and gives our people the qualities required to occupy a high place in the world.

"The present educational system, syllabuses and textbooks cannot produce this condition. In the fourth standard a pupil begins with the Puranic lesson, 'What does Kannan eat? Kannan eats butter.' By the time he reaches the B.A. class, the same companion has grown into the episode of Draupadi's disrobing. Lakhs and crores are dissolved in leading students towards the land of the Puranas, and then an account is presented claiming that so much has been spent on education. It is useless. Such teaching does not assist intellectual growth. There is little difference between the camphor burnt in a temple and the lamp oil students burn to obtain this education. We therefore cannot be satisfied merely to find money, spend it and establish educational associations; the educational plan and method must be reformed.

"However proudly we speak about this country, whatever evidence we offer, however many hymns we sing and sky-high towers we build, we cannot conceal one truth: through our intellectual advancement and skill, we have not yet given the world even one object or convenience needed for human social life. On the contrary, we conduct our lives using the conveniences and articles discovered and supplied by the intellectual clarity and scientific ability of other countries. In lighting we have advanced no further than the traditional standing lamp, not even as far as the hurricane lamp. In sound we remain at the stage of claiming that it reveals the sacred syllable 'Om, Om.' A Saivite devotee teaches worshippers the glory of that syllable through a loudspeaker, the marvellous instrument furnished by foreigners!

"Consider whether in even one field we have discovered instruments or ideas for the modern world. Are we at least travelling along that road? Our educational plan must be redesigned with this purpose in mind. Only then will expenditure produce its full benefit and our country advance.

"The foundation of education must be changed with the noble aim of spreading knowledge. It is no use if education serves merely to secure a livelihood or increase folly. Building a few more schools without reforming the system is not enough. In the present condition, one spectacle occurs in our schools that does not occur even in a devotional hall. In an old, ruined building, bricks loosen, turn to dust and lie scattered. When rain falls, water flows through the brick dust; the water takes on a saffron colour, the dust becomes sludge and the water is spoiled. It merely looks red and stains clothes; it never becomes a true red dye. Our education is in the same neither-one-thing-nor-the-other condition: a little new knowledge is sprinkled over old ideas, producing a useless mixture and an immature method. Such education cannot help and must certainly be reformed.

"Will the ministers carry out this task? It demands exceptional courage. Do these sons of Bharata possess it? I doubt it. I see in them a tendency to obstruct the spread of new knowledge. My associates and I are not great poets. Nevertheless, we found faults in the course of the story and in the fundamental issue of justice in the *Kamba Ramayanam*, and we told the people. Pulavar Kuzhandhai, a member of our movement who is present here, wrote a work called *Ravana Kaviyam*. If it contains faults, harmful ideas, errors or things contrary to reason, these ministers could explain that to the public. If they consider such a task beneath their dignity, their numerous disciples, attendants and soldiers could have been made to speak. Did they do so? No. They banned the epic. They obstruct new knowledge. How can reform in education be expected from people who protect old ways and ban new thought? The ministers are becoming temple preachers.

"Friend Avinashi tells the tale of the squirrel building a bridge. The moment the play *Porval* spreads new knowledge, it too is banned. How can rulers who impose such bans dare to make revolutionary changes in education? Yet knowledge cannot grow through education without reform. Even if the rulers refuse to understand this, you who made them rulers must understand it.

"I will not enchant you with the tempting claim that education guarantees a livelihood and great wealth. You and I know that people unable even to sign their names have become immensely rich, while a man with M.A. and B.L. degrees changes houses every year because he cannot pay the rent. Sivaji and Akbar, who never saw a college, created and splendidly ruled great empires. I therefore do not say education is needed merely because it alone can gather money and obtain a degree.

"Education is a collection of the thoughts of wise people. Through education we learn those thoughts; knowing them, we think; through thought we form further ideas; and then we offer the world those ideas, the actions based upon them and their results. A scientist invented the telephone. Does that object remain today in his own house as hereditary property? No, it benefits everyone everywhere. He is a great benefactor. We need an education that gives us the ability to confer through our knowledge and action at least one drop of benefit upon the world.

"Compulsory free primary education alone is not enough to fulfil this aim. Vast numbers of people above thirty or forty years of age are illiterate. It would be farcical to seat them in primary schools and teach them 'squirrel' and 'goat,' but neither may they be excluded from the realm of knowledge. If that great body continues to wander in the old world, a vast gulf will open between it and the newly educated population. It will become a cruel division equal to caste distinction. We therefore need a programme of adult education, and mere literacy is insufficient. World knowledge must be conveyed to adults through song, drama, story, conversation, speech and visual display. This can be done without great expense.

"Most people today know the stories of the Ramayana and Mahabharata. Did they read them? No; they learnt through hearing, generation after generation. Through educational associations we can use the same method to spread knowledge of the new world. In large village gatherings like this one, we can recount to people long accustomed to hearing the lives of the Nayanmars the life histories of the heroes, thinkers and scientists of our own times, and plant new ideas in their minds.

"There are known objects of his anger. He is angry with teachers, angrier still with Tamil teachers, and angriest of all with Tamil teachers who embrace the doctrine of self-respect. A man possessed of such anger wields power. In this condition, how can we expect our demands in education to be fulfilled? Nevertheless, I have said these things so that those who govern may at least understand what kind of education the people desire.

"I am immensely happy to see the people of this village gathered in such a great multitude for the opening of an educational association. Ordinarily people assemble in this way only for festivals. I regard the present thirst for knowledge in the country as the cause of this vast gathering. Today, if one visits an ordinary village and asks what makes it notable, people usually speak with pride of the sacred power of some neem tree or the greatness of a holy man staying in a roadside rest house. They do not say that their village possesses a large school, a good clinic or a reading room.

"By erecting this splendid educational-association building, your village has become a guide to other villages. I ask the people of other districts also to show such enthusiasm for intellectual work. I thank the friends who brought me to this ceremony, and I declare this school building open."

(Published in *Dravida Nadu*, 18 July 1948.)

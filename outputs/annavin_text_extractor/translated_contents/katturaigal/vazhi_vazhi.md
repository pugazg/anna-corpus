## English Translation

The old man may have lost his teeth, but that young man's smile, revealing all thirty-two like jasmine buds, cannot equal the old man's toothless grin! The young man laughs because his son, walking with a kingly gait and holding a stick of sugarcane as his sceptre, chases the mother who bore him while she cries, “Listen to what your father says! Listen!” “My son, coming to my defence!” the young man laughs thunderously. Though he knows that she is the lamp of the family whose precious womb carried the child, he does not even seem to notice the old man smiling nearby.

Perhaps he thinks the old man's own story is already over!

But the old man smiles precisely because the story is only beginning. He is thinking: “That young man is my son, and that lion cub is his son.”

Prosperity that passes from generation to generation possesses an incomparable sweetness.

Those who reach a condition in which they must say, “I lived, but no one received life through me,” have not created a history. It means only that they have passed their time.

The Tamils made domestic life a virtuous life because they understood that continuity must be secured and one life must become the seed of another. Only when domestic life becomes virtuous can a home contain the toothless old man's smile, the ringing laughter of the young man, the woman whose dimpled cheek speaks her delight, and the little king who carries a stick of sugarcane as his sceptre.

Consider instead a young man prematurely wearing the aspect of old age, who says: “Father boarded a ship and left; ten years have passed. Mother went to another town to run an appam stall. The woman I married is lamenting. The red scorpion born to me in a free hospital has crawled into some hole somewhere. Here I remain, though I have not died.” He is no example of prosperity secured from generation to generation.

Not merely the family, but the country itself must prosper generation after generation. Only then can the distinction of the wealthy, the elevation of those who possess, and the advancement of those who live be properly ordered.

It is natural that the dignity of a family flourishing through the generations should become more clearly visible on the new day of Pongal than on any other day. There is a special joy even in undertaking the effort required for it.

If one gained the opportunity to say, “My country has excelled and flourished through successive generations,” no festive feeling could be more majestic.

But our ability has not been sufficient. Whether home or country, we see only a dwelling place for people who yearn when they remember what existed before and tremble when they wonder what tomorrow may bring.

Home and country must be places of life not only for us, but for everyone who comes generation after generation.

That condition cannot be obtained merely by thinking about it. It can be secured only through pure labour, and after many defeats, disappointments, hardships and losses.

“Grandfather, murukku!” says the little fawn as she toddles forward to give him one.

Watching her totter along, her mother rejoices as though that fawn has won the medal in a world dancing competition.

“For me, dear? A murukku, darling?” asks the grandfather. He takes it, breaks it into tiny pieces, and feeds it back to the very sprig of the family who brought it. Watching her munch it, he imagines that the little girl has accomplished something no one else could achieve, kisses her cheek and delights in her. He cannot eat the murukku himself; he has no teeth! Yet he was the one who long ago bought the tool used to make that murukku when he carried a bundle to sell at the market. Today the murukku produced by that tool is not for him, but for those who came after him! Even so, when he reflects that another generation has arrived, his youth returns. And if the young man has understanding, he sees his future in that scene.

It is natural to think that what exists and what is obtained is not for oneself alone, but for one's family. One day long ago, someone gave the old man a handful of beaten rice. He put a little into his mouth where the giver could see, tied the rest in his cloth and brought it home. He gave it to the young man who now pretends to scold the graceful peahen, saying, “Is it still not ready? Hunger is pinching my stomach!” At that time the young man was a boy of seven or eight. Today the old man sees him bring his own son a flute, a rocking horse, a singing bird and a running deer. The order of generation following generation becomes visible.

But if this continuity is to be secured for a family or a country, no decline must intervene.

Not merely a decline in material resources: there must be no decline in thought.

If an old man takes a younger wife and the son born to her lives separately, the old man will flounder between “Here or there?” He will worry whether to carry things from here to there or bring them from there to here, because cross-lines have fallen across the continuity of the generations.

If these “lines” broaden until they become a chasm, matters will end in: “Look after your own path! I shall look after mine!”

Therefore, if generation after generation is to flourish, neither material decline nor a breakdown of harmony must occur. There must be no decline in thought.

Compared with other countries, the families of our country have achieved considerable success in preventing such a decline in thought.

But as industrial development and the bustle of life accelerate, this condition may be seriously affected in the future.

Although this continuity is being preserved to a noteworthy degree within the family, we cannot say that the country has attained prosperity passed down through the generations.

The moving verse comes to mind:

> In that month long ago,
> beneath that white moon,
> we had our father,
> and no stranger possessed our hill.

We cannot proudly declare that prosperity through the generations has been safeguarded in the condition of the country.

Why? Have we lost wealth? I do not mean material wealth. What if the flowers fall, so long as the vine does not wither? I am not speaking of lost possessions. We have lost the wealth of ideas. Through the decline that followed, the country has lost its generational prosperity.

> All living beings are equal by birth.

> Humanity is one community, and God is one.

> May the whole world receive the joy I have received.

> Every place is our town; everyone is our kin.

We have lost so very many treasures of thought, and we see decline attacking us.

We must recover those intellectual treasures. We must remove their stains, clear away their dust, and put them to use.

When thought becomes excellent, advancement follows; we shall attain the dignity of prosperity continuing from generation to generation.

There is no day more fitting than the new day of Pongal on which to contemplate this idea.

On this day, therefore, we must probe and discover why our country lost all the colour and abundance inherited through successive generations. We must then consider how this condition can be corrected, resolve to labour, and begin the journey.

There stands the lovely woman, cutting ginger. You cough to signal, “Come here.” When you look to see whether she understood, that mischievous eye says “yes,” but her spear-like gaze says that this is the time to finish the work.

For those engaged in labour, the work itself is the guardian that issues commands! It is not work merely for ourselves, but work for the country.

The ancients called this pure labour `thondu`, service.

The measure of success achieved by those engaged in this service determines the continuity from generation to generation that the country can celebrate.

The victory gained by members of the movement who seek joy through such service will become a great victory that restores the country's radiance.

But decline must not enter midway.

Look there! Milk Pongal! The woman knows it will reach the right consistency in ten seconds and says so with her eyes. In his agitation, her husband overturns the vessel he picks up. Water spills, the fire goes out, and smoke spreads everywhere. What happens then? The milk Pongal will take longer. The woman must clear the smoke, add fresh firewood, raise the flame and cook it again.

Therefore, to obtain benefit from service, agitation must diminish and calmness must be joined with determination.

I do not know whether we possess the ability to engage in this pure method and win. But I do know that those participating in this good endeavour are not numerous; we are only a few.

Our responsibility is therefore very great, and our goal very sweet. Our purpose is to labour so that we may attain a condition in which successive generations prosper, and so that the home, too, may gain that environment.

This good purpose must find a place in the heart; an alliance of the lips is not enough.

Even if bamboo is immersed in sugarcane juice throughout the day and repeatedly taken out, it will not become sugarcane. Lip-deep enthusiasm cannot acquire the quality of enthusiasm overflowing from the heart.

On this sacred new day of Pongal, let us undertake the effort with the conviction that those who labour for the heart to attain this nobility will themselves rise. Let us strive and see progress.

(14 January 1966, *Murasoli*, Pongal special issue)

## Translator's Notes

`வழிவழி` literally means “along the line/path” and here denotes prosperity, values and life continuing through successive generations. `தொண்டு` is public or movement service undertaken without a merely private motive. The four quoted maxims are rendered for meaning while remaining unchanged in the Tamil source. The first quoted stanza is from Pari's daughters' lament after the loss of their father and Parambu hill. One short phrase introducing the toddling child is damaged in the OCR; the translation follows the clearly recoverable action rather than inventing the missing wording.

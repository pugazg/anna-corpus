## English Translation

The pedestal is 155 feet high. Upon it stands a 151-foot figure cast in iron and copper. It is said that forty-eight people could sit near the head. The radiant torch is visible in its hand; when a person climbs and stands upon it, he reportedly looks like an ant to those viewing him from below. The Americans have placed this immense statue in New York harbour, not merely for artistic beauty but as a symbol of freedom. The Statue of Liberty: not only its name but its form and grandeur instil courage and feeling in other minds.

Yet in America, where the human longing for freedom has been portrayed in the form of a statue, Black people suffer greatly. Abraham Lincoln argued: "This is unjust! All are equal in God's creation. To treat a man worse than a dog and more contemptibly than a worm merely because he is Black is barbaric." He paved the way to emancipation.

Only a few days ago, a Black schoolgirl enrolled in a school in the state of Texas. Alas, the suffering she endured is beyond description. In another state, Tennessee, a Black sailor disembarked to spend his day of leave. It is said that even the police were insufficient to protect him and send him safely away, and an entire military force had to arrive, for racial hatred rages there to such a degree.

America has mansions that touch the sky, splendid buildings that pierce the clouds, and the Statue of Liberty. Yet this disgrace exists there.

One good man struck a blow against the perverse arguments of white racial fanatics.

One day the world suddenly heard his name. It felt astonishment and shock as well.

Twenty-eight minutes. He finished reading and sat down. The world watched with eyes opened wide.

Through that single act, he became without equal.

It is his picture that we have published on the cover of this issue. He is Warren, Chief Justice of the United States Supreme Court, who visited Madras, Calcutta, Delhi, and other places and returned home this week.

"Whites are the superior race; Blacks are the inferior race. Equal places for both? Never!" cried America's white enclave. This gave rise to a case over whether Black students could be admitted to schools attended by white students. The forces of domination all believed that because the judge was one of their own, the decision would favour them.

But Warren placed human justice above all else and removed the prohibition that barred Black students from entering those schools. He read for twenty-eight minutes the judgment explaining his decision. White people were stunned; the world awoke and took notice.

By this deed, Warren secured a place in the pages of history.

Because he removed the barrier, a path opened for Black Americans to gain some comfort and courage. The government also became responsible for enforcing the Supreme Court's order and was obliged to intervene wherever people were denied access and rights and to punish wrongdoers.

As a result, many white supremacists now seek indirect ways to establish their dominance, but there are signs that their former arrogance is fading. The world of informed opinion praises Warren for his judgment.

(*Dravida Nadu*, news item, 9 September 1956)

## English Translation

## Lakshmi's Wedding

The young woman is eighteen, an age suited to married life. Yet the poor woman is a widow. Yes, a Brahmin widow, one whom Vedic tyranny demands be cast aside as an obstacle to good omens and a social curse.

According to the true path preached by the “sacred” dharma of Hinduism, Lakshmi, as the young woman is called, should forget flowers, remove the mark from her forehead, shave her head, wear a white sari and sob endlessly over her fate. If she violates the Vedic rule and engages in any supposedly improper act, she must seek a length of rope with which to kill herself, or wander in search of a pond beside a field. Is this not the widow's life, crushing and discarding the fruit of love?

Lakshmi was married in 1926. Three years later, in 1929, she became a widow. When the marriage pendant was tied and then cut away, Lakshmi was still a girl who had not attained puberty.

Her father, Venkatachala Pattar, could not bear to see his young daughter, now widowed, drowning in an ocean of grief with eyes swollen from weeping. Wondering how he could watch this dreadful sight for the rest of his life, he courageously acted. He arranged Lakshmi's marriage to a Brahmin in Tiruchirappalli district.

To readers of *Kudi Arasu*, this incident will seem entirely ordinary. To the Brahmins of Palakkad, however, it appeared as a thunderbolt and a catastrophe of cosmic dissolution. Cruel orthodoxy sprang furiously upon Venkatachala Pattar for daring to relieve his daughter's sorrow.

This “Lakshmi wedding” took place in Pallavur village near Palakkad. The village's orthodox zealots raised a tremendous outcry: “Religion is gone! Caste is gone! Custom too is gone!”

Their excess did not end with shouting. They began socially excommunicating the champion who made widow remarriage possible and expelled him from caste. They created obstacles during rites connected with his mother's cremation and his daughter's death. Unable to cremate a body in his own village, Venkatachala Pattar reportedly had to seek firewood in the next village.

What can one call the conduct of orthodoxy, this scriptural curse and Brahminical spectre? While it grips our country, where is freedom or deliverance?

Seeking release from this historical curse, Pattar appealed to the law. He brought a defamation action against those who excommunicated and harassed him. Alas, his trust in the law too proved futile.

This is, after all, a land of extraordinary wonders. In science class a teacher reads from a scientific textbook and explains to thirty or forty children how an eclipse occurs. On the day of the eclipse, that same teacher goes to the bank of a tank to wash away its “pollution.” In such a country, would not the law itself be founded upon Hindu religious scripture?

Comrade C. Kunhirama Menon, Principal District Munsif, heard the case. Mahamahopadhyaya N. S. Ananthakrishna Sastri, Sanskrit professor at Calcutta University, and Brahmasri Venkata Patti Sastri were examined as expert witnesses and recited their scriptural interpretations.

The plaintiff alleged that nineteen Pallavur Brahmins had excommunicated and harassed Venkatachala Pattar. He argued: “I arranged my virgin-widow daughter's remarriage because I did not wish to ruin her life. Scripture permits it. A similar marriage has taken place in Trichur, and a declaration signed by twenty-five Brahmins has been submitted as evidence. I therefore committed no offence against religion. I seek damages from these nineteen Brahmins who subjected me to needless trouble.”

The defendants replied: “This marriage violates religion. Nothing like it has ever happened in Pallavur village, and we will not accept it. Unless our preceptor, Sri Sankaracharya Swamigal of the Kumbakonam monastery, issues a sacred order declaring it valid, we will not recognize this shaved widow's marriage. Where is that sacred order?”

What did the law say? What do you imagine the court of our country decided? It dismissed the case. Venkatachala Pattar, subjected to the cruelty of social excommunication, did not succeed. The orthodox perpetrators received no punishment.

Why is the law like this in our country? Although the Munsif dismissed the case, he rebuked the orthodox group: “A young widow! A maiden! Can even her remarriage not be accepted?” But that was merely the judge's counsel. His judgment had to follow the law. In substance, his legal ruling said:

> The Pallavur Brahmin community has not accepted widow remarriage, and no such marriage has occurred there before. The marriage arranged by the plaintiff therefore conflicts with the Hindu religious law and custom of the Pallavur Brahmins. Whether widow remarriage is right or wrong, valid or invalid, is another matter. Each community must decide that for itself, and a civil court cannot intervene.
>
> Excluding the plaintiff from caste does not violate the justice ordinarily prevailing. Moreover, it is entirely consistent with the defendants' communal rights. The exclusion is therefore not an offence, and the case is dismissed.

Strip away the language of law and consider its substance. Is it not clear that, whatever frenzy the orthodox may perpetrate, nobody can touch them once they cover it with the cloak of Hindu social custom? If people in the twentieth century possess the right to call widow remarriage monstrous, abandon human compassion, and torment a person until he cannot even find a place to dispose of a dead body, must Indians not cry, “Orthodoxy, when will you perish? Brahminism, when will the people dig your grave and bury you?”

Even after civilization has drawn Brahminism's poisonous tooth, it behaves like this. The body trembles to imagine the cruelties that must have occurred in the age when the great Manu held power.

We are waging war in a country where we must accomplish what nobody else has achieved and what others fear even to attempt. If courage and determination remain, victory will be ours. Lakshmis, do not fear! We shall fight and secure for you the rights that faith in Sanatana doctrine and appeals to orthodoxy cannot obtain. We shall certainly secure them. Be patient a little longer; the struggle continues. Listen to the drumbeat of Self-Respect.

## Love and Death

The poet has said, “Without love, there is death.”

At Tirunelveli Junction, a twenty-year-old youth named Subramania Pillai reportedly became heartbroken because the parents of the woman he loved refused to give her in marriage to him. He poured petrol over his body, set himself alight and died.

What cruelty! What cruelty! When will such cruelty end? Alas, in a world full of the wilfully blind, marriage remains both a blind game and a performance of servitude. Is love not the life of marriage? Is not a marriage of love the true marriage? Can denying this be called wisdom?

India, this is the freedom you have given your youth. You earned a name for denying love and gave the young man death. As long as the country clings with a monkey's grip to blind custom, horrors like the one in Tirunelveli will persist. As long as sinners ruin the country by babbling endlessly about antiquity, young men called Subramania must either kill themselves or be killed.

There is no place for the flower of love in this city of the blind. When will the restraints and abuse imposed on marriages of love disappear? When will these cruelties end?

The Tirunelveli tragedy demonstrates that the Self-Respect Movement, which has conducted countless marriages of love, must grow and receive its friends' support. Would the dead youth not have thought: “Yes, I loved that woman. Her parents' tyranny killed my love, and I too am dying. If others like me are not to meet this fate, Erode's breeze must blow everywhere and the teaching of Self-Respect must enter every place”?

## Futile Obstinacy!

“Mahatma” Gandhi feels bitterness toward machines. His doctrine is that machines have ruined human society. He says each person must weave his own clothing, remove his own excrement, lead a simple life, sit beneath a castor-oil lamp and recite Tulsidas's *Ramayana* every day. That, he says, is self-rule and Rama Rajya; Congress was created to attain it, and toward that end we fight the British in “a war without sword or blood.”

Indeed, this is what mahatmas say. To go further, they would advise people to wear bark, carry a water pot, enter the forest, perform severe penance, behold the merciful benefactor and depart for the empire of liberation. So far, India's present “Mahatma” has stopped with the spindle and spinning wheel; he has not told people to enter the forest.

Because the machine age arose, civilization and wealth have grown throughout the world. Lands that did not develop science, but placed a golden “deity” upon a silver vehicle and paraded it through the streets, are sinking and disintegrating in slavery, ignorance and poverty.

Gandhi nevertheless says that machines caused war, unemployment and other troubles in Western countries. Therefore, he argues, we need no machine. Hand-pounded rice is enough; khadi is our salvation and the sound of the spinning wheel our devotional music. Many beat time to his tune.

Yet machines continue entering India while the Mahatma remains awake and watching. Subhas Bose recently inspected and praised a marvellous printing machine. Even in Gandhi's time, khadi itself is crying out. At the Alipur Congress, Gandhi reportedly lamented, “It seems you will burn khadi along with my corpse.” Nevertheless, the Mahatma ceaselessly repeats the old refrain that machines must disappear.

Mahatmas should remain occupied with spiritual matters. When they begin entering the affairs of human society, many evils cannot fail to arise. Our country has reached that condition today.

At a time filled with eminent people who ask, “Can we contradict the Mahatma?”, Comrade D'Souza, Principal of St Joseph's College, Trichinopoly, spoke at the annual celebration of the Madras Catholic Social Guild. He condemned Gandhi's attitude toward machinery; said that capitalism's defects, not machines, caused Western troubles; argued that machine civilization must spread if vast India is to enjoy true prosperity; and alleged that Gandhi was applying the Wardha education scheme to spread his hostility to machines throughout the country.

Who could fail to praise Principal D'Souza for stating the matter as he saw it? Yet in this country the Mahatma's propaganda against machinery appears to be futile obstinacy. Machines do not customarily ask mahatmas before entering any country; they ask the people. Just as machines reduced to dust the words of mahatmas who opposed them in the West three centuries ago, the same process is occurring here.

Even so, many D'Souzas who speak the truth must address the people and alter their infatuation with the Mahatma. We ask interpreters who tell the truth to come forward.

## Quite a Splendid Feast!

Advocate Comrade K. S. Sambukesa Iyengar held a reception at his Triplicane residence for K. S. Krishnaswami Iyengar, newly appointed a judge of the Madras High Court. After the meal, Advocate-General Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Aiyar garlanded and congratulated him.

Those attending included High Court judges Justice S. Varadachariar, V. Panduranga Rao and K. P. Lakshmana Rao; Advocate-General Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Aiyar; the Sheriff of Madras; G. A. Natesa Aiyar; and the prominent Madras advocates R. Ramaswami Iyengar, P. Ramakrishna Aiyar, P. C. Sankaranarayana Aiyar, R. Jayaram Aiyar, K. V. Krishnaswami Aiyar, K. V. Sesha Iyengar, V. T. Arangaswami Iyengar, S. Srinivasa Aiyar, R. Satyamurti Aiyar, T. M. Krishnaswami Aiyar, K. Raja Aiyar, P. Sitarama Aiyar, S. Arangaswami Iyengar, K. Narasimha Aiyar and V. Govindachariar.

You ask how I know it was quite a splendid feast! I learned that the feast was “splendid” only after seeing the “list” of those present in *The Mail*. Look for yourselves: is there even one “Sudra” onion in the list for medicinal purposes? Or was any one of the Brahmin worthies present an ordinary person? Brahmin judges! A Brahmin Advocate-General! A Brahmin sheriff! Prominent Brahmin advocates! Splendid indeed, is it not?

*Kudi Arasu*, 3 April 1938

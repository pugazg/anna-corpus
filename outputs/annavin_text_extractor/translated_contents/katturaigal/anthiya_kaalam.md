## English Translation

Phlegm catches and drags in his throat.

His eyes have begun to roll.

His tongue has begun to falter.

The doctor has given up, spread his hands and driven away.

Relatives, parents and everyone in the village stand around him.

Telegrams have been sent to those who must come from out of town.

The head of the house wonders whether to send someone to fetch the drummers and musicians.

The patient lying upon the bed begins to speak, stumbling over his words.

Only a minute or two remains, yet he babbles about village affairs.

“Speak of what can be accomplished in the little life remaining. Sign the will concerning your property,” urges the family lawyer, who has only just been summoned.

But the man speaks of village affairs, becomes a corpse and fails to do what was needed in his own will.

At the final hour, instead of speaking of what could still be accomplished, he spoke of all manner of other things and died.

The man under discussion speaks in precisely this fashion: only a few hours remain, but instead of saying what can be done, he talks of the town while standing at death's door.

He is the country's leader, the lord of the republic—President Rajendra Prasad.

Without properly addressing the condition before him, he wastes time in a long speech.

It was the fourth session of Parliament.

The life remaining to it was extremely short.

Within a few months the general election would arrive and end even this brief life.

They assembled at the final hour of a short-lived Parliament.

Nor was it Parliament alone that stood at its final hour. The ruling Congress party seated upon the throne of power had reached its final hour too. They had gathered at such an ending.

It was also Rajendra Prasad's final season—not merely in his term of office. By stage of life and age, he too was an old man.

At Parliament's final hour, members of a generation in its final season assembled. Rajendra Prasad, who walked the corridor of life's evening, spoke.

What should the patient gasping with phlegm have discussed? If something had to be said about the property that remained or his own family, he should have said it.

He did not. He spoke of village stories until his throat dried and died even before the two minutes available to him had passed.

Rajendra Prasad follows the example of that corpse.

His parliamentary speech makes us think so.

When life is departing and the will is held out before him, does he intend to become village headman?

Without discussing the matters that should have been in Rajendra Prasad's address, he discoursed at length about the world.

When the tongue of the national leader moves, do the people not expect something good—that their leader will offer what their lives require?

He is the people's President. He does not speak in an ordinary hall, but from the summit of the machinery of government.

Would he choose to waste time and thought there with idle stories?

Is he an idler, an ornamental preacher or a performer of religious discourse? No. He is the country's leader. His tongue is the nation's legislature and his voice the seat of its authority. Yet that is how he spoke.

And at the final hour. His place of speech, the office he held, his party and the employment of many who had assembled—all stood at their final hour. Why such a speech at such a time?

When the monarch speaks in the British Parliament, it is a golden day for the people and a day of consequence for the government. The day Rajendra Prasad spoke was of the same kind.

Yet to us it appears a wasted day.

In convention, ceremony, banquet, display, pomp and celebration, no difference can be found between the two.

But is that what matters? Is not the speech delivered that day what is important to the people?

Rajendra Prasad spoke at the final hour and examined many matters unnecessary at that time.

They display his broad knowledge of the world, and we are proud. They demonstrate his noble ideal, and we rejoice.

Even so, this is the final hour, while the country presents a scene of tears.

A leader who sees this condition should show a way to remove those tears and offer a remedy in his address.

Instead, he grieved for the people of Korea, lamented Korea's destruction and yearned intensely for peace there.

He showed immense concern for the Japanese peace treaty.

He prayed that the Persian oil dispute be settled amicably.

He explained at length the new awakening arising across Asian countries and swelled with pride while doing so.

He described the condition of Nepal.

He expressed grief over the murder of the King of Jordan.

He thanked Turkey and Persia.

He condemned South Africa and grew angry with it.

We do not say he must not discuss foreign affairs. We know he must attend to every foreign and domestic question.

But the man who spoke with such concern about foreign affairs spoke as though unaware of the condition of his own country.

We did not expect these meaningless rhythms in a final-hour address.

When the lawyer learns the patient is about to die, he holds out the will; the patient talks of the village.

We expected the President's address to show a path out of the suffering of those shedding tears. We were disappointed.

He spoke of war with Pakistan.

He spoke of their autocratic performance in Punjab and the electoral magic created in the name of the Planning Commission.

He threshed foreign affairs thoroughly, but abandoned the living problems at home and spoke of everything else.

The dying man holds a music concert. The man about to perish builds a wedding pavilion. The man whose spirit will leave in the next instant plans a fortress reaching the sky.

Rajendra Prasad, standing at the threshold of the final hour, speaks in that fashion.

Life is about to leave. At least now he may sign the will.

Parliament possesses only a brief life. He should have said what could still be done within it.

The people do not await his speech for ornament, nor is a presidential address merely a customary ritual.

He should have spoken about the problems prevailing in the country today.

People say they are hungry. Only after answering that should he seek time to speak about Korea's horrors.

They point to deaths from starvation. Only after showing a remedy should he counsel others about Persian oil.

Workers say they have no employment; weavers say they have no yarn. Should he not have offered a conclusion to these questions?

People tremble that human freedoms are being taken away, yet his speech contains no remedy.

They cry out from hunger, suffer without clothing, complain that facilities needed for industries to prosper have not been provided, and weavers grieve that no yarn is available. The people become a volcano over their lost rights.

Yet the President showed no path.

As far as our Dravida Nadu is concerned, the food problem is becoming extraordinarily terrible.

The cruelty of the yarn shortage likewise continues to grow.

What he said about these matters not only astonishes us; it increases our grief that the country still remains beneath rulers who offer such empty words.

“The country's food position causes the government great concern. There is danger of famine in many parts of the country, especially Bihar.”

The food position supposedly causes the government concern. It is we, the people of the country, who must worry—not the rulers.

What rulers require is not concern but attention.

Only attention will reveal the causes of the food crisis. Once causes are discovered, the evils must be watched and removed. Only when they are removed will the condition improve and the crisis end.

Concern is what comes to the incapable.

The rulers sing the lament that belongs to a man who can do nothing.

They possess every facility and should not merely worry.

The President knows famine threatens Bihar, but does not understand Dravida Nadu's pitiable condition.

How eagerly that suffering continues to spread can be understood from the boon granted by Food Minister Munshi:

“It is impossible to allocate additional rice to Madras State so that its ration may be raised to eight ounces.”

That is Munshi's boon.

They asked that six ounces be made eight; he refused, saying it was impossible.

The President showed no path for the people trapped and suffering within those six ounces. Here people die of starvation for want of rice, while he mourns the death of Jordan's king.

Nor does he give a plan to people asking for yarn; he offers words of satisfaction.

Handloom weaving is an important and extensive industry of this country, employing an immense number of people.

These facts are not known only to Rajendra Prasad; everyone knows them.

What will the rulers do to make the industry prosper? That is the question before us—and before him.

His answer is:

“The development of this industry depends upon the distribution of yarn.”

Wonderful! The President has discovered it. We might now sing and dance that anxiety has ended and tears dried.

Yet even after recognising the truth, they have not corrected yarn distribution.

“Efforts are being made to improve this distribution.”

Can the head of a country speak this way about a vital problem?

President Rajendra Prasad says what idle Thimmappan sleeping upon a veranda might say.

He is not a preacher free to speak vaguely, touching and not touching the matter. He is the President, inseparable from the country's rise and fall.

He should have supplied figures explaining how, when, by what method and to what extent yarn distribution would increase, and brought smiles to wounded people.

A dying man must do what is necessary before death. What can one do when he abandons it and speaks of everything else?

Holding his wavering life in his hands, he must sign the will.

If instead he cries that he will board a train and bring back a lovely woman with the grace of a peacock and the voice of a cuckoo, people will call it delirium. No one will praise him as a great man desiring marriage even while becoming a corpse.

If, at the final hour, he avoids what can be accomplished, speaks about the vast world and evades and delays domestic affairs, he may be called a conjurer of words—but how can he be called President?

The general election approaches. He himself says, “We are going; our time to depart has come.” Does he not even know what can be accomplished before departure? Is that not what must be done?

He speaks at the final hour like a man searching for builders of a wedding stage when the moment has come to construct the funeral bier.

*Dravida Nadu*, 12 August 1951

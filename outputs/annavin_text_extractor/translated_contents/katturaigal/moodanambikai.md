## English Translation

There was an age when superstitious customs exercised dominance throughout the world. Before scientific knowledge expanded, the whole world, enclosed by ignorance, allowed many superstitions to roam and oscillated without grasping truth.

As human effort developed knowledge and scientific clarity gradually arose, superstition began gradually declining throughout the West. Yet here, in “our sacred land” alone, we see superstition stand firm without fear of any science. Why? Is science afraid of superstition? No.

Some of our country's “elders of wisdom” carry superstition in one hand and science in the other, remaining in a Trisanku heaven, as the puranic account—which we do not believe—would have it. That is the reason.

A government employee wears a scientifically manufactured watch so that he may reach the office precisely at nine. At the same time, he keeps a panchangam to see whether nine falls within Rahu Kalam or Yamagandam.

Suppose the watch shows nine, neither inauspicious period applies, and the officer sets out. If he immediately meets a lone Brahmin, two Sudras or a pot of oil, or if a cat or snake crosses left or right, that is enough: he turns home and goes to the office only after waiting.

There is no one at the office to ask why he came so late; he himself would have to question himself.

But if someone working beneath him arrives late for any reason, he rebukes him: “Why do you always arrive late? If you continue, you will no longer fit in here.”

The worker begins, “Sir, I left at the proper time, but after two steps a cat crossed my path, so—” Before he can finish, the officer asks whether anyone would accept a cat as the reason for lateness and points out that the man was late yesterday too.

“Yesterday Rahu Kalam was from nine to ten-thirty; that was why.”

The officer orders him to settle his account and leave the office for delaying work on such grounds. The officer himself nevertheless remains there, working within both the embrace of superstition made by God and that of science made by the rational.

These are only some superstitions found among educated people. Many kinds also inhabit the uneducated public. Let us explain a few.

At places selling sugarcane, one sometimes sees a contest in which a person places a hundred canes upon his hand and breaks them with a blow. People like us fear to break even one. Our fear confers divinity upon the man who breaks a hundred. We do not consider whether it is divine or remember that he trained by repeatedly doing it. Nor do we possess the confidence that if we adopted the same practice, we could break not merely a hundred but two hundred.

Little children begging for alms strike their stomachs loudly with their hands. If our children did that, they would curl up and fall. Does the begging child's ability prove divinity? If the child possessed divine power, why would it have to strike its stomach and beg? So long as many people remain too dull to understand that practice produces such ability, these feats will apparently be regarded as divine.

Even breaking a coconut against the head is called divine here. Confusion has grown to that extent.

Many English books investigate and explain such superstitions according to modern knowledge. Two important works are *Taylor's Anthropology* and *Frazer's Golden Bough*. Self-Respecters must recognise the need to translate them into Tamil.

To eliminate public superstition, its foundations must be investigated and understood, and such books are indispensable to that inquiry. What use is there in printing puranas and degrading plays when attempting to teach modern knowledge to ordinary people? Let us explain some superstitious customs here according to true knowledge.

### 13

Every day in Tamil Nadu we see a custom particularly common among ordinary people: taking the dead to the cremation ground with great noise. Without investigating its origin, this has become a major South Indian practice. It appears especially among communities described as “depressed classes.” Funeral drums are rarer among Brahmins and Vaishyas called high castes.

In countries regarded as civilised, such as Europe and America, drums are not beaten at funerals except for military commanders. But this custom appears frequently among peoples historically labelled “Savage Tribes.” Its reason emerges by examining their beliefs.

They hold two superstitions concerning the dead. First, a dead person's life or spirit—“Spirit, Soul”—leaves the body and hides in a nearby plant, tree or body of water. Second, this “soul” troubles the living.

They therefore shout, make noise and produce loud sounds with drums, large kettledrums, conches and other instruments to frighten the soul away. The louder the noise, the faster the dead person's “soul” supposedly flees.

Tamil people continue this ancient custom. The origin of our funeral music lies in the ideas of ancestors who lived as early tribal peoples. Although that is its foundation, new meanings and explanations are now supplied.

When a king or military leader dies, funeral music is used extensively. When a poor person dies here, the procession goes with one or two conches; when kings or the wealthy die, every instrument in town sounds. Why? Is the rich person's “soul” removable only by louder sound? Such ideas underlie funeral music. The claim that instrumental performance honours the dead is a later explanation. In origin, the instruments were intended to prevent the dead person's “life” from returning.

Some such customs remain among Brahmins too. One is to avoid taking a corpse through the ordinary doorway. A hole is made in a wall, the corpse removed through it and the hole sealed.

Early peoples said that if the dead left by the old route, the “soul” would return the same way. If a new opening were made and then sealed, the soul would not know the new path and could not enter through it. This was one device for deceiving the dead person's soul. Many other superstitious devices are used to confuse it.

When carrying a corpse from the house to the cremation ground, people break a pot of cow-dung water. This too follows an ancient tribal custom based on the belief that ghosts and spirits cannot cross water, especially cow-dung water. It is poured behind the corpse so its soul will not cross the sprinkled water.

The custom requiring those who lit the pyre to wet their feet in the sea, pond or river before returning home arose from the same idea. Their footprints disappear in water, so the dead person's soul supposedly cannot follow them—just as thieves enter water to hide tracks and pursuing dogs become confused.

The rule that those who lit the pyre must not look back at the corpse also came from this soul doctrine. The soul knows their faces. If they look back at the burning body, “the soul hovering near the burning corpse” might attach itself to them and return home. The practice arose from this imagined fear.

The custom of washing one's feet before entering the house likewise rests on the belief that ghosts cannot cross water.

For the same reason, present-day Brahmins sprinkle water around their leaf before eating, believing ghosts and spirits cannot cross it and eat their food.

Sprinkling cow-dung water in front of the house at dawn has the same basis: ghosts outside since midnight, unable to enter through a closed door, are prevented from entering when it opens.

In some places, if a lamp goes out during a meal, people remove the food, light the lamp, sprinkle water and sprinkle water upon their heads before eating again. This too likely entered custom through ghost belief. Ghosts supposedly eat food only in darkness. Once the lamp goes out, the food is polluted by them and cannot be touched; after the lamp is lit they flee and fresh food may be eaten. This must be regarded as contrivance.

All these customs arose from the fiction that a person's “soul” wanders outside after death. The belief says a soul the size of a thumb lives within a person. After the body dies, it remains near the dead person's house, neighbouring houses, plants and cremation grounds. These souls roam deserted places as ghosts and spirits.

If someone travels alone through an isolated place in darkness or at the “heat hour” of noon, ghosts and spirits, Annanmars, Thambimars, Kannimars, Muniswaran and other beings supposedly strike that person and cause illness.

This belief fills the world. The fiction of the soul underlies ghosts, spirits, Muniveerans and wind Karuppans. If ordinary people understood belief in a soul as invented, these invented ghosts would fly from the world.

Endless cruelties have followed belief in ghosts and spirits. People once believed false rumours of *necromancy or black magic*, and many were killed. To remove such “sorcery,” first-born children were sacrificed. Thousands of lives were lost, thousands of children killed, and thousands of elderly women tortured and killed as “witches” and practitioners of sorcery.

For a thousand years in Europe, uncounted “wizards” and “witches” were killed under the customary word *witchcraft*. Know that the invented word “soul” lay beneath this great evil.

From this one fictional word arose hells and heavens, nirvana and samadhis, ancestral rites, temples, tanks, mosques, churches, festivals, sacred waters, the *Periyapuranam*, *Kandapuranam*, Vedas and Vedantas.

Recognise this single word as fiction, and all ghosts and spirits; Irulan, Veeran and Katteri; Siva, Vishnu and Brahma; thirty-three crore gods and forty-eight thousand rishis; their temples, mosques, *viharas*, Buddhist temples and Siva temples will disappear.

For evidence, children and adults who do not know this invented word do not know the invented word “God” either; nor do they know ghosts and spirits. Scientists who recognise the first as fictional conclude that “God” too is an invented word.

As words evolved among human beings, the words “soul,” “life” and “breath” arose first. From them came “great soul” and “great life.” These words were attached to the term God: “human” was called the small life and “God” the great life. This is the evolution of the term God.

Early humans knew neither “soul” nor “God.” Only after speech began were these words invented.

19 June 1949

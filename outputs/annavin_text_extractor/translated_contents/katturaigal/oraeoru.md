## English Translation

This article continues the examination of the claim that, in the third test conducted to establish the superiority of Saivism, the palm-leaf manuscript cast into the water by Sambandar cut through the current and travelled upstream.

It is said that the leaf released by Sambandar went upstream along the Vaigai without stopping anywhere or meeting any obstacle. A minister named Kulachirai supposedly mounted a horse and rode after it in order to retrieve it. To stop the leaf travelling against the water, Sambandar is said to have sung the Tevaram hymn beginning "Vanniyum Maththamum." The leaf, after travelling twelve miles upstream, allegedly heard the Tevaram and immediately came to rest in midstream. Kulachirai, who had gone to retrieve it, is said to have entered the water, picked it up, climbed back onto the bank, taken it to Madurai and shown it to everyone. Let us examine these marvellous events to some extent.

Readers will, I trust, remember the expression "twisting sand into rope," quoted in the preceding article in connection with the claim that a piece of palm leaf placed in a river travelled against the current. Kulachirai, we are now told, mounted a horse to retrieve that upstream-travelling leaf. Why did he not walk? Did he think that only by riding swiftly could he catch a leaf that was rapidly cutting through the water and travelling upstream? Or did he think that walking rather than riding would diminish the dignity of a king's minister? Why should we investigate that?

Still, the novelty deserves mention. It resembles saying that a man wishing to twist sand into rope first turned water into sand and then made a rope from it: to retrieve the leaf travelling against the current, Kulachirai supposedly used as his vehicle a steed naturally capable of travelling at 150 miles an hour.

From Sekkizhar's account we learn that Kulachirai did not ride merely because he considered it fitting to the dignity of his ministerial office. He rode because the leaf truly travelled upstream so fast that he believed it could not otherwise be found. Sekkizhar himself says that, to find the leaf, Kulachirai went

"mounted on a fierce steed
that sped faster than the wind."

When we contemplate the speed at which Sambandar's leaf must therefore have travelled, we can only wonder what further proof anyone could require of the greatness of Saivism!

What method did Sambandar use to stop the upstream-travelling leaf? Just one hymn! The leaf that had been cutting rapidly through the water stopped the moment it heard that hymn. Where did it stop? In midstream near the village called Thiruvedagam. How did it stop? It remained there without travelling any farther upstream. What more could one undertake to measure the superiority of Saivism? For one hymn, the leaf travelled upstream; for another, it stood still exactly where it was!

Yet Kulachirai had to ride out on horseback merely to bring back that leaf. Sambandar, who had sung to make it travel upstream and then remain in midstream, was apparently unable to sing, "You have gone far enough; turn back and come here." Would doing so perhaps have lowered the greatness of Saivism? Or might the leaf have refused to return? Would the Jains not have accepted it if a hymn had made it return? Why, instead, was Kulachirai sent on horseback to fetch it? An explanation is required. I request the devotees of Sekkizhar to offer one kindly, without becoming angry and imagining that we are needlessly disturbing their Saivite reflections.

It is next said that Kulachirai's eyes saw Sambandar's leaf resting in the middle of the river. How can we believe that Kulachirai, standing on the bank, saw a leaf lying in the water at midstream? A tiny piece of palm leaf in the middle of a river becoming visible to a man on its bank would indeed be astonishing.

Nor can we suppose that the Vaigai was so narrow there that people on its bank could see every object in it and Kulachirai could therefore see the leaf. Anyone who visits the place can observe that beside the temple in Thiruvedagam mentioned by Sekkizhar, the Vaigai is wider than at many other points. It is broad enough to prevent someone on the bank from discerning a small object such as a palm leaf lying in the water at its centre. At Thiruvedagam, the river is approximately a quarter of a mile wide. The claim that Kulachirai saw with his own eyes a little palm leaf lying in the water halfway out from the bank is a complete falsehood that none but the intellectually blind could believe.

Nor can we argue that the Vaigai's water was as clear as glass, making every object in it easily visible. Like other rivers, the Vaigai does not carry flowing water in every season. To find water there one must either dig or wait for rain. Without either, there is no water in the Vaigai. In a rainless season, one must "place the hand" in the river, that is, dig, to find water. It is said that this is how the river received the name Vaigai: *vai-kai*, place the hand, or dig, and water will be found. In other rivers, water is not easily obtained by digging during dry weather. In the Vaigai, however, even after several rainless years, digging a pit one or two feet deep is enough to reveal water. The underground flow beneath the riverbed lies very near the surface, so water appears upon digging two or three feet.

The *Thiruvilaiyadal Puranam* contains a story explaining why water is found as soon as one digs in the Vaigai. That extraordinary explanation appears in the episode titled "Feeding Gundotharan." If I began explaining it here, Sekkizhar's devotees might cry out that I was comparing the *Thiruvilaiyadal Puranam* with the *Periya Puranam*, so I refrain.

If I were to say that Sambandar placed his leaf in a hand-dug pool and that it cut through that water and travelled upstream, I know very well that Sekkizhar's devotees would fume at me: "Why are you dragging in things that did not happen and insulting our Purana?" I shall therefore never say that this was what occurred. If they wish, they may marvel at the thought that the consort of the bamboo-shouldered goddess, capable of doing anything in any manner desired, could launch even a vessel or ship in a handful of water. I, however, wish to explain events rationally, in a manner that can withstand investigation. That is my purpose.

Let us therefore grant as "true" the following account: rain fell and water overflowed both banks of the Vaigai; Sambandar cast his leaf into it; the leaf climbed against the current and stopped in midstream near Thiruvedagam; and Kulachirai saw it. I hope Saivite devotees will at least permit us to investigate how far this could be true.

Even if we assume that rain had filled the river when Sambandar cast the leaf into the Vaigai, floodwater would have been muddy, not clear enough for a particular object to be pointed out. Moreover, the leaf supposedly released by Sambandar was a small strip bearing the short hymn "May the Brahmins prosper." Could it have been seen without confusion? Countless objects would float in a rain-fed flood overflowing its banks. Many fragments of palm leaf like the one Sambandar cast would travel in that wild water. His leaf could only have been one among them. How could anyone identify it as *the* leaf released by Sambandar?

Perhaps it will be said that every other object was swept away while this leaf alone resisted the water and remained stationary, and that Kulachirai saw it in that state. But was the thing standing there a great teak tree or some other large tree that it should be visible? It was a tiny strip of palm leaf. Amid a river flood carrying heaps of rubbish away, would its remaining still have made it stand out as though proclaiming, "Look at me! I am the leaf released by Sambandar"? What are we to call the "clear" intelligence of Sekkizhar, who was so intent on glorifying Saivism that he had no time even to consider whether anyone with the slightest clarity of mind would believe this?

After narrating so many events contrary to nature for the glory of Saivism, Sekkizhar does state one fact as it was: Sambandar's leaf stopped near the village of Thiruvedagam. He says that a village already bearing the name Thiruvedagam existed before Sambandar placed the leaf in the river. In other words, according to Sekkizhar, the leaf came to rest in midstream near the village of Thiruvedagam, twelve miles from Madurai.

Some Saivite devotees, however, explain the origin of the name Thiruvedagam by citing another Puranic account: because Sambandar's *edu*, or leaf, stayed at that place, it came to be called "Edagam." According to them, the settlement now called Thiruvedagam must have borne another name before Sambandar's test. Sekkizhar contradicts this claim by saying that the village had been named Thiruvedagam even before Sambandar's time, and that Sambandar's leaf later went and stopped there.

No great investigation is required to decide whose statement is true and whose is false. If the place truly acquired the name Edagam because Sambandar's leaf stopped there, Sekkizhar would have treated that too as an event glorifying Saivism. He would have added ten or fifteen more verses to the *Periya Puranam*, saying: "The village now called Thiruvedagam bore such-and-such a name before Sambandar released his leaf; after his leaf went and rested there, its name changed to Thiru Edagam." In doing so, he would have obtained still more glory for Saivism. But he did not.

Thus, when some Saivite devotees claim that the village is called Thiruvedagam because Sambandar's leaf rested there, they merely wish to show that they too are Sekkizhar's heirs and to rejoice that, just as he sought glory for Saivism, they have done likewise. They have not even examined the fact that Sekkizhar's account and theirs are constructed so that each makes the other false.

Why would they investigate? Is it not their doctrine that examining religious principles is wrong and sinful? Yet the very people who regard such inquiry as sinful consider it a meritorious and honourable deed to teach religious doctrines through falsehood, tall tales and fabrication. What conduct is this!

Finally, factional divisions arise among Saivites themselves concerning Saivism, and the assertion of one faction renders that of another false. This makes clear that religions such as Saivism and the doctrines described as divine are themselves false, and that they are treasuries of imagination created to provide a livelihood for a few people.

22 December 1946

சைவரோ - வைணவரோ மற்றெந்தச் சமயத்தவரோ, தங்கள் சமய
உண்மைகளை விளக்க எழுந்த நூலென்றோ, அல்லது

இடமில்லாது செய்து விட்டனர். என்றாலும், பொன்னின்

மன்பதையின் பொதுநூலாக்குவது, முயன்றால் முடியாத
காரியமன்று, மணிகளிடையே பவளத்தைக் கோர்ப்பதுபோல்,

## English Translation

If the god or gods worshipped today by some Dravidian people were truly gods of the Dravidians and knew even one Dravidian language, they would have arranged for all their affairs to be conducted by Dravidian people in Dravidian languages. Yet it is plain to see that, even today, all worship and ritual in the temples of the Dravidian land are conducted by Aryan Brahmins in the northern language.

Would any Dravidian accept as his own god a being who drinks in the northern language like honey, savours Aryan doctrine like sugar candy, reduces Tamil people to Sudras, regards Tamil as unsuitable for itself, and leaves no room for Tamil principles, whatever form or power that being might possess?

Some say that the Dravidian people once had a god and scriptures concerning him in a Dravidian language, Tamil, but that these disappeared or were destroyed through Aryan intrigue and the floods of the month of Adi. I say that intelligent people cannot accept even an atom of this claim. If that god and the scriptures written about him could be destroyed by Aryan intrigue and seasonal floods, how can we accept the god as the omnipotent First Cause and those manuscripts describing him as Vedas that pronounce the ultimate conclusion? Even little children happily cooking pretend rice from sand would laugh at the claim that a god unable to protect himself and the books explaining him possesses the power to protect us and everything visible in this world.

Those who assert that the Dravidians had a god do not stop with the foregoing explanation. To establish their counterfeit doctrine, they attempt to offer various proofs. They say that long before Aryans, Jains and Buddhists entered the Dravidian land and established their respective religions, the Dravidians possessed divine worship and its scriptures. After these disappeared through Aryan intrigue and the Adi floods, they claim, Tiruvalluvar created the *Tirukkural*, the Saiva teachers created the *Tevaram* and *Tiruvachakam*, and Vaishnava teachers created the *Prabandhams*, thereby re-establishing the ancient Dravidian conception of God.

This is an incongruous falsehood like trying to hide a whole pumpkin in a serving of rice. Scholars have concluded that Valluvar's *Tirukkural* is not a sectarian religious text but a universal work composed to enlighten all humanity. Therefore Saivas, Vaishnavas and followers of no other religion can claim that the *Tirukkural* was written to explain their religious truths or assert an exclusive right to it by saying that it belongs to them alone. It is a universal work created to explain human character.

Religious zealots have nevertheless stained it and prevented it from becoming humanity's common book. Just as dross can be removed from gold by refining it, it is possible, with effort, to wipe away the religious stain introduced into the *Tirukkural* and make it a common book for humanity. Religious doctrines have been strung through that jewel of wisdom like pieces of coral among gems; this stands plainly visible like a lighthouse. Learned people free from aversion and attachment can remove them very easily.

There is abundant evidence that the *Tevaram*, *Tiruvachakam* and *Nalayira Divya Prabandham* were created on the basis of the Aryan Vedas called Rig, Yajur, Sama and Atharvana. Fearing excessive length, I give only one example here:

> The Veda is the cow of the Lord;
> the true Agamas are its milk;
> the Tamil hymns recited by the four are its ghee;
> the flavour within that ghee is the truth
> of Meykandar's Tamil work.

This verse shines as a poem proclaiming the distinction of the Saiva Siddhanta work *Sivajnana Bodham*. Its meaning is that the Aryan Vedas, beginning with the Rig and Atharvana, are the cow; the Agamas are the milk produced by that cow; the *Tevaram* and *Tiruvachakam* are the ghee produced from that milk; and Sivajnana is the flavour within that ghee.

Moreover, the hymns themselves contain proof that the *Tevaram* and *Tiruvachakam* were sung with the Vedas beginning with the Rig as their foundation and with the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata* epics as scripture. It is as obvious as a gooseberry in one's palm that the *Tevaram*, *Tiruvachakam* and *Prabandhams* could not have been sung without those two epics. The claim that these Prabandhams are the essence of ancient scriptures is therefore itself without substance.

It follows that the ancient Tamil people possessed no Vedas. It also follows that none of the gods described in the present *Tevaram*, *Tiruvachakam*, Prabandhams and other Saiva and Vaishnava works was worshipped by the ancient Dravidians. The truth becomes clear: among the Dravidian people there could not have existed the unwanted and unnecessary superstition of divine worship as something suited to humanity. Thus the claim by some that the *Tirukkural*, *Tevaram*, *Tiruvachakam* and Prabandhams embody the essence of ancient Tamil texts must be regarded as sophistry.

Concerning the three great academies called the First, Middle and Last Sangams, the first volume of *Research on the Periya Puranam* reportedly explains with suitable evidence that “Sivan” was the name of the Tamil poet who presided over those academies; that because he excelled in learning, memorial stones were raised in his name after his death and commemorative days were observed annually by Tamil people; that after Aryans entered this country those stones were converted into Siva temples; and how and why that poet came to be called Sivan.

Likewise, we should consider the claim that Vaishnavism and its worship of Tirumal were established in the north by Sadagopa, Munipanar, Pavanachari and others; that Rama, the king of Ayodhya, was a mortal of the human race like us who died; and that Ramanuja brought the religion into Tamil Nadu with Rama treated as a god.

Similarly, everyone knows that Buddhism and Jainism were brought from the north, Christianity from Asia, and the Muhammadan religion, Islam, from Arabia and imported into the Dravidian land. It is therefore an undeniable truth, the argument says, that the Dravidian people had no religion, god or scriptures concerning such a god in ancient times, and that all these were introduced by various people at different points in history.

Should we not at least ask what good these gods have done for the people all this time? Has the torment of hunger ended? Has anxiety disappeared? Has poverty changed? What benefit have people obtained by creating and worshipping a god who lives under guard on Kailasa and another who sports with women in Vaikuntha? What benefit have they received from the Nayanmars, Alvars and other religious leaders described as incarnations of these gods? Have hunger, disease, poverty and worry ceased among the people?

From then until today, we see pleasure and pain continuing like heights and hollows. People of understanding say that this is nature's character. The wise say that nothing is impossible through effort. Those who neither understand nor wish to understand these truths continue vainly deceiving the world by declaring that something which does not exist, cannot exist and is not needed does exist, can exist and is necessary.

A man who says that he has seen God, or that God can be seen, does not obtain what he needs from that God. Instead, he goes begging to a person who lives without concern for God. “How excellent, excellent, is the condition of God!” the rationalist laughs, and then teaches the beggar both a way to relieve his hunger and knowledge joined with inquiry to remove his ignorance. It is the beggar's duty to listen and act accordingly.

I shall now offer another strong proof that, according to the characteristics assigned to God by religious books, a god cannot exist at any time, in any manner or in any place.

All religious texts generally arrive at this final doctrine: all moving and unmoving things in the world arise through God's command, grace, love, thought and gaze; every living body is a temple of the Lord; and that one God directs everything. Although the religions differ in describing how the world arose, they describe the relationship between God and living beings, and the distinction between their actions, in essentially the same fashion. All religions that uphold God accept the idea expressed by the line, “If He makes them dance, who can refuse to dance?”

When we take this shared foundational doctrine about God and living beings as our evidence and employ rational inquiry, we must arrive at the sound conclusion that no entity called God can exist in any form.

If God directs everything in the world, why can the one being directed not know who directs him? Or why does the directing God not reveal himself to the being he directs? For example, why is a child born without any thought or sign that a god exists? The almighty God either cannot or does not give a child he creates the capacity to know him from birth. If so, how can omnipotence belong to that god?

A tiny child can communicate its hunger to others by crying. Why can it not realize that God created both it and its hunger? If awareness of God were truly present in the child, it would know without being taught by others that a god exists. If the child learns of God only after another person tells it, then until that message is conveyed there can have been no relationship between the child and the god described to it by somebody else.

We identify objects in the world to one who does not know them by saying, “This is such-and-such; this has these characteristics.” If God too must be introduced by saying, “This person is God; these are his characteristics,” can there be any difference of status between worldly objects that arise and perish and a God said neither to arise nor perish?

Whether it is the Hindu god or the god of any other religion, if it remains unrelated to other beings until some intermediary establishes that relationship, such a god cannot be accepted according to the very scriptures that define divinity.

If “humanity is one community and God is one,” why do communities and gods exist in swarms like termites? If one God is worshipped under many names, why does one god quarrel with another, saying, “I am great and you are small”? Why does one community wage communal war and inflict cruelty by telling another, “I am superior and you are inferior”?

Does one supreme First God govern the world by keeping many lesser gods beneath him, just as a king rules his country through many petty kings? If so, what does it mean to attribute to that God absolute primacy and omnipotence? If the god discussed in Hinduism truly possessed these characteristics, there would be no reason for many different religions and their many different gods to exist on this Jambudvipa.

Would a god without preference or hatred desire others to worship him? Would he grant rebirth to those who worshipped him and freedom from birth to those who did not? If that is how it works, what difference is there between that god and a man who accepts a bribe to complete a task?

Anyone who investigates these brief truths honestly and intelligently will easily understand what the nature of God is said to be; whether one can live in the world without God; how, by whom and at what time belief in God arose among the Dravidian people; and whether any relationship can exist between God and living beings. Such a person will also understand the truth that the moving world is constituted through nature. For that reason, I end here without expanding the discussion further.

*10 October 1944*

## Translator's Notes

The article presents an atheistic and Dravidian rationalist argument. Its claims about the origins and chronology of Saivism, Vaishnavism, Jainism, Buddhism, Christianity and Islam, and about ancient Tamil society, are translated as claims made in the source; they have not been independently verified or silently revised.

`ஆடிப்பெருக்கு` is both the seasonal rise of rivers during the Tamil month of Adi and a Tamil observance associated with it. The argument invokes flood destruction literally. `மன்பதை` is an older word for humanity or humankind. `அங்கை நெல்லிக்கனி` is an idiom for something plainly evident. `நாவலந் தீவு` is Jambudvipa, a traditional name for the Indian subcontinent or inhabited world.

The quoted verse on Image 3 is damaged by OCR, including its line division. Its prose explanation immediately following supplies the translated sense. Several names on Image 4 (`சடகோபர் - முனிபாணர் - பாவணாச்சாரி`) may themselves be corrupt or historically idiosyncratic; they are transliterated conservatively. The sentence around `அறியாமைப் பக்கு` on Image 5 is also damaged. No conjectural correction has been inserted into the preserved Tamil source.

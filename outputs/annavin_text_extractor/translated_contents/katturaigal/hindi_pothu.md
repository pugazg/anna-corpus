அறிவுக் கண்கொண்டு படிப்பவர் எவரும் இவ்வேண்டா இந்தியை வலிந்து

என்பது எமது துணிபு.

வழங்கிவரல் வேண்டுமென்று, இந்நாட்டின் நன்மைக்காக உழைக்கும்
வடநாட்டு அறிஞர் பலருந் தென்னாட்டறிஞர் சிலரும் பேசியும் எழுதியும்

நடத்திவருகின்றார்கள். ஆகையால் இந்திமொழிதம் நாட்டவர்க்கு நன்மை

இப்போது, ஆங்கிலத் கல்விக் கழகங்களிற் கல்வி பயின்று
வெளிவரும் நம் இந்திய மக்களிற் பெரும்பாலார் பொருள் வருவாய்க்கு
வேண்டுமளவு சிறிது ஆங்கிலம் பயின்றவராயும், அதனோடு சேர்த்துச்
சிறிதே கற்பிக்கப்பட்ட தமிழ் முதலான மொழிகளைத் தப்புந் தவறுமாய்ப்
பேச எழுதத் தெரிந்தவராயும் வெறும் போலி வாழ்க்கையிற்
சில்லாண்டுகளே உயிர் வாழ்ந்தொழிதலால், இந்நாட்டின்கட் பெருத்
தொகையினராய் வெற்றுயிர் வாழ்க்கை செலுத்துங் கல்லா மாந்தர்க்குந்

## English Translation

This article is a summary of the book *Can Hindi Be the Common Language?*, written by Professor Maraimalai Adigal. We are convinced that no one who reads it with an eye to reason will undertake the destructive enterprise of forcibly making this unwanted Hindi a compulsory subject.

“In many places in the north of this Indian country, the language called Hindi is spoken with many variations. Many northern scholars and some southern scholars who labour for the welfare of this country speak and write that all the people throughout India should learn it and use it as their common language. They have also opened and are operating Hindi schools in various places. We must therefore first investigate whether Hindi possesses the qualities and capacity to benefit the people of our country.

“At present, most Indians who emerge from institutions of English education have learned only enough English to earn an income. Along with it, they have been taught only a little Tamil or another language, which they can speak and write with mistakes. They live only a few years in an empty, artificial way of life and pass away. Consequently, those educated in English bring very little benefit either to the great mass of uneducated people who merely remain alive in this country, or to those few poor people who have struggled to master their mother tongue and are scattered here and there like the crescent moon of Karthigai. This being so, scholars must deeply consider whether any benefit could result from introducing the study of the alien Hindi language into Tamil Nadu and into other regions that speak other languages.

“Because the people living in the many regions of India speak many languages, some national workers say that, whenever all the country’s people must assemble and discuss a common good, it would be best for them all to use Hindi as a common language. We shall show that their claim is unsound.

“Hindi is spoken in many northern regions with numerous variations; it is not spoken everywhere in one uniform manner. Scholars who studied this varied language divided it into three large groups called Western Hindi, Eastern Hindi and Bihari. Within those groups they further identified numerous smaller divisions, including Bangaru, Braj Bhasha, Kanoj, Batheli, Urdu, Awadhi, Bagheli, Chhattisgarhi, Maithili, Bhojpuri and Magahi. There are still many more small languages spoken as divisions of Hindi. The inhabitants of the many regions where these varieties are used live without understanding one another’s languages. How, then, can the assertion that Hindi is a common language known to all of them be valid?

“Which one of these different Hindi languages, spoken with so many variations in so many regions, is the southerner to master? Whichever one he learns, will he be able to use it to speak with all northern people? He cannot. Besides the Hindi varieties mentioned above, countless languages such as Sindhi, Lahndi, Punjabi, Gujarati, Rajasthani, Kumaoni, Garhwali, Nepali, Oriya, Bengali, Marathi, Shina, Kashmiri, Kohistani, Chitrali, Tirahi, Pashai, Kalasha and Gawar-Bati are spoken by numerous people in many northern regions. Can a southerner who knows just one Hindi language converse freely with all these large bodies of people? Not in the least.

“Even among northerners there are many groups who do not know Hindi and use distinct languages of many kinds. What benefit, then, can southerners obtain merely by learning to speak Hindi? Any scholar capable of considering these facts impartially will clearly understand that forcing southerners to learn as a common language one of the Hindi languages, which is both unknown and useless to them, can produce nothing but wasted time, wasted labour and wasted expense.

“For five thousand years, teachers who had mastered Tamil composed *Muthukurugu*, *Muthunarai*, *Kalariyavirai*, *Perumparipadal*, *Tolkappiyam*, *Perunkalithogai*, *Kurugu*, *Venthali*, *Viyazhamalai*, *Muthollayiram*, *Natrinai*, *Nedunthogai*, *Akananuru*, *Purananuru*, *Ainkurunuru*, *Kurunthogai*, *Sitr Isai*, *Per Isai*, *Pathitrupathu*, *Paripadal*, *Kurungkali*, *Tirukkural*, *Silappathikaram*, *Manimekalai*, *Tirukkovaiyar*, *Civaka Cintamani* and other rare and great works of Tamil grammar and literature. They also composed short and extensive commentaries rich in subtle meaning and taste. By keeping Tamil in the unchanging youth and vigour of a civilized language until today, they enabled Tamil-speaking people, though living far from one another, to converse by speech and writing and live as one people gathered in a single place.

“Hindi, Urdu and the other northern languages are not ancient like Tamil. They arose only after the Mughal rulers, adherents of Islam, invaded the north, seized Delhi as their capital, and began ruling from there. At that time a language descended from Prakrit was spoken in Delhi and its surroundings. After Muslim rule was established there, numerous Arabic and Persian words brought by the rulers mixed into that language, which consequently came to be called Urdu.

“The literary Hindi now in use was later separated from Urdu and reformed by Lallujilal. He entirely removed the Persian and Arabic words that had entered the earlier Prakrit-derived language, inserted a great abundance of Sanskrit words, and thereby created the new Hindi language. Some people obstinately claim that, by learning this recently constructed language of northern words, now only lately brought into literary use, southerners will be able to converse with all northerners. That is a falsehood intended to deceive our people.

“Between 1400 and 1470 CE, that is, 467 years ago, an ascetic named Ramananda spread through many parts of the north the doctrine that Rama should be worshipped as the supreme deity. The songs he composed upon Rama are regarded as the first Hindi work and are called the *Adi Granth*.

“After Ramananda, one of his disciples, Kabir Das, sang in Awadhi, a branch of Hindi. He declared that it was improper to say God had taken many births, that worshipping God in forms of stone, copper or wood was a great wrong, and that Hindu rites and ceremonies were meaningless, base acts.

“After Kabir Das, his disciple Nanak composed songs in a mixed language combining Punjabi and Hindi and propagated the Sikh religion.

“About 480 years ago, a Vaishnavite named Vidyapati Thakur lived in the village of Bisapi in the Darbhanga province. In Maithili, another branch of Hindi, he composed many songs elaborating the love between Krishna and his beloved Radha. Chaitanya later translated these songs into Bengali and spread them throughout Bengal. Does this not show that Hindi was not current among the people of Bengal? Even if southerners learn a Hindi unknown to the people of so large a northern region as Bengal, they cannot use it to converse and maintain relations with Bengalis.

“What has been stated so far therefore makes several things clear. Five hundred years ago Hindi had no literary usage. It was variously altered and spoken in different northern regions by people without formal education, so that the speech of one group was unknown to another. It consequently remains divided into many languages even today. The claim that Hindi is the common language of all northerners is therefore untrue, and southerners who learn it cannot use it to converse with all northern people.

“To those who emphasize that the Hindi languages are spoken by the large number of forty million people, we may point out that Bengali is spoken by fifty million and that Tamil and the languages related to Tamil are spoken by sixty million. If one group says Hindi should be made the common language, will not Bengalis insist that Bengali, spoken by a still larger population, should become the common language? Will not our Tamil people similarly insist that Tamil should be learned as the common language? It is the primary language of the Dravidian peoples spread to all four boundaries of India, and it is spoken not only in India but by Tamil people who have settled and prospered in commerce in Ceylon, Burma, Malaya, South Africa and other countries.

“Furthermore, most songs in Hindi and the other northern languages exalt as gods Rama, Krishna, Balarama, Vasudeva and other petty kings who, like us, took many births, struggled and died. These northern languages are not as ancient as Tamil. The source also argues that their speakers did not possess from antiquity the same civilized life it attributes to the Tamil people; that the northern poets who appeared during the past four or five centuries did not possess the independent great learning of the ancient Tamil masters, but followed Sanskrit Puranic tales in delusion; and that northern people embraced Aryan textual codes containing practices such as killing living beings, eating flesh, drinking liquor, worshipping many minor gods and maintaining caste divisions, instead of the Tamil textual path that, in the author’s view, rejects them and stresses compassionate conduct and worship of the one supreme God. It therefore concludes that Tamil people will obtain no good or benefit by studying those languages and their recently produced books.

“Tamil thus possesses, in every respect, the qualities required to become the common language of all India. Yet instead of attempting to make Tamil the common language, people clamour to impose upon this country Hindi and other derivative mixed languages: tongues only four or five hundred years old, poor in ancient and distinguished literary wealth, full of many deficiencies, and spoken largely by northerners whom the source describes as uncivilized. Intelligent people must think deeply about whether those who make this attempt are genuine servants of the country.

“From the facts demonstrated thus far, let people recognize that the study of Hindi brings not the slightest benefit to our Tamil country. Let them prevent its instruction, spread everywhere the study of our independent Tamil alone, and enable our Tamil people to shine in knowledge of their mother tongue!”

*Dravida Nadu*, 4 July 1948

## Translator's Notes

The article explicitly identifies itself as a summary of Maraimalai Adigal’s *Hindi Pothumozhiya?* and reproduces its historical anti-Hindi argument. Language-family labels, dialect names, lost Tamil titles and personal names are transliterated conservatively from the scan; several may differ from modern scholarly romanization. The source contains strong period judgements about northern peoples, religion and civilization. The translation marks one especially broad passage as the source’s argument rather than presenting those judgements as established fact. The printed chronology and attribution of *Adi Granth* to Ramananda are translated faithfully but are historically questionable and were not silently corrected.

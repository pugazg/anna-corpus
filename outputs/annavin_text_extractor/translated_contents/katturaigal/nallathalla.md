## English Translation

Among human beings are some with a peculiar disposition. With affection and admiration they praise a person, saying, "Ah, is there anyone like him?" They celebrate his worth in an *antati*, sing a *sindhu* about his distinction, and extol his fame.

But if the person they celebrated with such admiration and grandeur becomes involved in anything they dislike, praise immediately turns into abuse; affection turns into fury. They become angry and say irritably, "What does he know?"

This behaviour is caused by a disposition possessing two traits: lack of clarity in the heart and selfish desire.

Through its editorial of the 31st, the "national" newspaper *The Hindu* demonstrates that it occupies first place in this category.

It has written an impassioned editorial about the very Nehru whom it praised as "a jewel among men," "a selfless man of sacrifice," and "a treasury of knowledge"—Pandit Nehru, the Prime Minister of India—as though beginning in the raga Atana and ending in Mukhari.

*The Hindu* begins its editorial by saying, "No one in this country can doubt Pandit Nehru's magnanimity or his zeal to complete what he undertakes," and then gradually reveals its angry assault upon him. This is not about just anything. It is about his speech supporting an amendment to the Indian Constitution concerning the right to social equality.

The paper indirectly levels the accusation that he has not behaved in a manner worthy of the trust the country placed in him. At the same time, it does not fail to note what he declared in the Indian Parliament while speaking about social equality: "The amendment being made is intended to remove an error that has occurred. I accept full responsibility for this error."

Until now, we have seen *The Hindu*'s pen write only in praise of Nehru.

But the moment he spoke in some measure of support for social equality, its anger turned into prose.

Another English newspaper, the *Mail*, writes that the demand for "Dravidastan, which seeks separation in opposition to northern domination," may have created such circumstances in Nehru's mind.

Whatever reason led Nehru to speak in favour of the amendment, no one can deny the goodwill inspired by his views on social equality.

Though doubt continues to arise in our hearts as to whether this amendment will fulfil the demand for social justice to the extent we expect, Nehru's speech is nevertheless capable of giving fresh courage.

But *The Hindu* lunges. The moment it sees the route by which the dominance of "its own" people might continue being closed, it spits flames of anger. It heaps blame and ridicule upon Pandit Nehru, whom it had praised as its unrivalled leader.

The path of *The Hindu* is not unknown to us. A downtrodden society has understood that, true to its name, it has long practised deception. Nevertheless, to shout about rights while creating a storm in the lives of the majority is the conduct of fanatics. At a moment requiring wisdom, it reviles the very person it once praised as soon as its channel of self-interest appears threatened.

The spectacle presented by *The Hindu* shows domination-madness at its height. We are not among those who believe Nehru will do us good; we cannot believe it. Yet some circumstance has given him the state of mind that no path other than this is available.

*The Hindu*'s circle consists not only of people who have received Nehru's embrace. The fathers of two of *The Hindu*'s assistant editors sit in the central government as Nehru's colleagues.

Such is *The Hindu*'s connection with him.

And still *The Hindu* lunges. Its assault is the product of domination-madness. Such madness is not good; it brings danger. It would be good for *The Hindu*'s circle to realise this and save itself from becoming dust before the rising voice of rights.

(*Dravida Nadu*, 3 June 1951)

## Translator's Notes

The source uses the Carnatic ragas Atana and Mukhari as a metaphor for an editorial moving from praise to lament or reproach. The exact date of *The Hindu*'s referenced editorial is given only as “the 31st.”

## English Translation

(On seeing the title, do not imagine that this is an article written about Periyar's marriage. This is an editorial written by C. N. A. in *Viduthalai* in 1940. Read it and see!)

A seventy-two-year-old Brahmin elder, endowed with a body in which "the belly sagged, the hair greyed, and the row of teeth shook," was struck by "the arrow of leaping lust" and wished to marry.

He was seventy-two! This Brahmin elder had already married and seen his wife become a corpse; he had children and grandchildren. But what of it? In benighted India, a man may marry as many times as he wishes.

It is only the woman who, whether a maiden in the bloom of youth or a tiny girl dressed in silk and playing *pallankuzhi*, must become a widow if the husband dies after marriage. She may possess youth, but that community will not permit her to obtain admission to a life of happiness. Her eyes may turn toward the beautiful pictures of life in the world, but what use is that? She may live sobbing in the guise of widowhood; otherwise, she may become a prostitute. The path to remarriage is not strewn with flowers. Is it not instead filled with stone, earth, thorns, the brutal conduct of people with blind doctrines, and the mire called shastra?

She may be an eighteen-year-old maiden, a flower in bloom whose fragrance spreads all around. Her eyes may fall upon the sight of her father fondling and delighting in the company of his third wife. Yet she, the flower that has lost its blossom, must be tormented by orthodoxy, the depraved trap fashioned by witless people. She must look upon the moon moving through the blue sky, think many thoughts at midnight, and live a ruined life. Otherwise, she must strangle the child she ought to place in a cradle, cherish, nurse, and raise, and throw it into a desolate well.

"Why is the foolish woman so rash? Why such agitation?" all the "great" men will ask angrily. Thus the starving maiden will be battered and crushed by famine.

What constraint is there upon a man? He may marry as many times as he wishes. He may have tuberculosis, but will he refrain from marrying on that account? Will the townspeople ask, "You have consumption; why marry?" No. They will say that this man suffering from tuberculosis needs a good woman to "administer medicine" tenderly and at the proper time. The law will not stand in his way. Society will not ask why. The marriage will take place amid drums and auspicious music. It may be his third marriage, or his fifth or sixth. A man possesses that right.

"Injustice!" intellectuals will say. Yes, it is indeed injustice. But who will challenge it?

People in one town did challenge it. They did not merely question it; they stood in the way and prevented such an improper marriage. Nor did they stop there. At that very time, they married the bride to a suitable groom. We praise the people of character who performed such an admirable deed.

In Mymensingh, near Calcutta, a seventy-two-year-old Brahmin elder arranged to marry a young woman.

How could the old man persuade a woman with a golden, radiant body to marry him by displaying his corpse-like frame? He possessed no youth, but he had money. The woman's parents looked at the money. Even if she became an old man's wife, would she not have her hands full of wealth? They consented to the marriage. Every arrangement was made.

Many people in the town went to the elder and argued in various ways that this improper marriage must be prevented. He would not listen. He fixed the wedding day, erected the wedding pavilion, took the auspicious bath, dressed in silk, adorned himself with jewellery, applied fragrance, and made himself ready to marry the pitiable maiden.

The people of Mymensingh saw what was happening. They resolved that they must somehow stop this injustice. If one merely says, "Peacock, peacock, give me a feather," will the bird yield one? The determined people of the town therefore entered and carried away the woman dressed as a bride.

Muslims and Hindus worked together in performing this splendid and startling act.

The elder arrived at the wedding pavilion and found only an empty space. He scowled, grimaced, and shouted wildly.

Meanwhile, the townspeople themselves married the young woman to a suitable man.

The elder saw that events had passed beyond his control. Thinking, "Let the maiden go, but let something at least come into my hand," he demanded monetary compensation for the humiliation he had suffered.

The virtuous people who prevented this improper marriage collected money among themselves and held the wedding feast as well.

We praise them.

Once his greed for money had been satisfied, the Brahmin elder who wandered in a frenzy of desire for women decided that this was enough. The maiden married a suitable man. We wholeheartedly praise the people of Mymensingh who prevented the thali that "grandfather" had been about to tie.

Yet how many marriages like the one prevented in Mymensingh continue to take place without being stopped? There is no one to prevent them. Reason has not grown.

We ask why the government should not come forward to monitor and prevent such improper marriages. How often can determined townspeople stop them? A civilized government must devise some means of preventing this folly that ruins women's lives. The government could establish in every town committees of prominent people and rationalists and empower those committees to prevent such improper marriages.

Just as the government enacts laws to prevent people from destroying themselves through drink, gambling, and prostitution and thereby removes social disorders, it must enact a law to stop women's lives from being trampled underfoot through such marriages.

In Turkey, before a marriage can take place, the bride and groom must first obtain a doctor's certificate stating that they are free of disease and send it to the government.

In the princely state of Indore, a law was introduced prohibiting a man from marrying a woman when there was a great difference in their ages. Some other princely states have similar laws.

In the Bombay Provincial Legislative Assembly, a woman MLA sought a law preventing a man over forty-five from marrying a maiden under eighteen. In the Sind Provincial Legislative Assembly, Professor Ghanshyam argued that a man more than twenty years older than a woman should not be permitted to marry her. They attempted to enact such laws.

The Mymensingh marriage incident demonstrates precisely the necessity for such legislation.

Will the government pay attention? Will society demand that the government do so?

(Note: When Periyar now boldly announces in *Viduthalai*—a paper published with these very ideas as its guiding purpose—the marriage he intends to contract at ages seventy-two and twenty-six, how can we accept it? Comrades of the movement, think!)

*Dravida Nadu*, 10 July 1949.

## Translator's Notes

The central text is identified by the source as a 1940 *Viduthalai* editorial; the opening and closing parentheses frame its 1949 republication during the controversy over Periyar's proposed marriage. `தாலி` is retained as *thali*, the marriage pendant tied by the groom. `கூடாமணம்` is rendered as “improper marriage” in the article's age-disparity and coercion context. The source's location description and comparative legal claims are translated without silent historical correction.

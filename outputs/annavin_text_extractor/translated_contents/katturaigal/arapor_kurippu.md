அக்டோபர் கடைசி நாள், அறப்போரை மேற்கொண்டுள்ள,
திராவிடர் கழகத்தார், சென்னை ஜிம்கானா மைதானத்திலே, நடத்திய
பொதுக் கூட்டம், முக்கியமான சம்பவம். இடையே நிறுத்தப்பட்ட
அறப்போர், மீண்டும் துவக்கப்படுகிறது - இந்த வரலாற்றின், முன்னுரை
போன்றது இக்கூட்டம். திரு.வி.கலியாணசுந்தரனாரின் சீரிய
தலைமையிலே, கூட்டம் நடைபெற்றது - அதாவது அறப் போரின்
துவக்கத்துக்கு, திரு.வி.க. தமது பேராதரவைத் தந்தார், என்று பொருள்.

உழைத்து, உள்ளம் நொந்து, உருமாறிப் போயுள்ள அந்த உத்தமர்,
அன்று, 'உபதேசம்' செய்தார், ஊராள வந்துள்ள ஊதாரிகளுக்கு.
நல்லவர்களின் குணம், யாரையும் நல்லுரையால் நல்வழிப்படுத்தி
விடலாம், என்பதுதானே. அம்முறையிலே, அறப்போர் முரசறையக்
கூட்டப்பட்ட இடத்திலே, பேரறிவாளர், ஆட்சியாளருக்கு அறிவுரை
புகன்றார், '“ஆட்சியாளரே! ஆணவத்தால் அழியாதீர்! நாட்டு மக்களின்
மனத்தை நன்கு அறிந்து நான் கூறுகிறேன். இந்தி விஷயத்திலே, வீம்பு
வேண்டாம். வீண் பிடிவாதம் வேண்டாம், கட்டாயத் திட்டத்தை விட்டு
விடுங்கள் : கபடம் வேண்டாம் - கர்வம் கொள்ள வேண்டாம் -
பெரியாரைப் பகைத்துக் கொள்ள வேண்டாம் - இன்றிரவே யோசித்து ஒரு
நன்முடிவுக்கு வாருங்கள், - அறப்போர் துவக்குவதற்குள், நாளையதினமே,
ஒரு உத்தரவை வெளியிடுங்கள், கட்டாய இந்தித் திட்டத்தை ரத்து
செய்துவிட்டோம் என்று; அறவழியை நாடுங்கள் - அன்பு மார்க்கத்தைத்
தேடுங்கள்'' என்று கூறினார். உருக்கமான உரை! உள்ளத்திலே
ஏற்கெனவே ஊராள வந்தோருக்கு ஏறிப்போயுள்ள ஆணவம், இந்த
அறிவுரையைக் கேட்டு நடக்கும் பண்பினை - ஆளவந்தார்களுக்குத் தராது
எனினும் அவர் கூறினதிலே ஒருவகைப் பொருள் இருக்கிறது. :

அறப்போர் தொடுக்கு முன்னம், அதனை அறிவிப்பது மட்டுமல்ல,
என்ன செய்தால், அறப்போரைத் தவிர்க்கலாம், என்ற சீரிய யோசனை
யையும் கூறுவது, களத்திலே கூடினோருக்குற்ற கடமை, என்ற முறையைக்
கலியாணசுந்தரனார் கையாண்டார் என்றே கருதுகிறோம். அவருடைய
நல்லுரைக்குப் பிறகு, ஆட்சியாளர்களின் மனம் மாறவில்லையானால்,
ஒரே ஒரு பொருள் தான் கொள்ள முடியும் - ஆட்சியாளர்கள் வலுவில்
நம்மை அறைகூவி அழைக்கிறார்கள் போருக்கு என்பது தான் பொருள்.
அந்த அறைகூவலை ஏற்றுக் கொள்ள நாம் தயார் என்பதை, நவம்பர்
1ந்தேதிய கூட்டம் - ஜிம்கானா மைதானத்தில் நடைபெற்ற கூட்டம் - நன்கு
விளக்கிக் காட்டிவிட்டது. அறப்போர்க் காதையிலே, இரண்டாம் கட்டத்தில்,
முதல் பகுதியின், (முதல் பக்கம்) என்று கூறலாம், அன்றைய கூட்டத்தை.

அறப்போர் மீண்டும் தொடங்கப்படுவது உறுதி என்பதை மட்டுமல்ல,
அதற்கு ஆதரவு மிக மிக அதிகமான அளவு என்பது, அன்று ஜிம்கானா
மைதானக் கூட்டத்தினால், எதையும் கவனிக்க மறுக்கும்
ஆட்சியாளர்களுக்கும் விளங்கி இருக்கும்.

மூன்று கலர், நாலு கலர், நாலு ராயல், ஆறு ராயல், லித்தோ
போஸ்டர் என்ற இவ்வகையான விளம்பரங்கள், ஓட்டப்பட்டிருந்த
சென்னைச் சுவர்களில், 'இப்படி இருநீத அச்சுத்தொழில் இப்போது
இவ்வளவு அழகாக வளர்ந்திருக்கிறது' என்பதை எடுத்துக்காட்டும்
விநோதமான, போஸ்டர், ஜிம்கானா மைதானத்தில் திராவிடர் கழகக்
கூட்டம் என்று காவி ஏறிய வெள்ளைத்தாளில் வெண்மை கலந்த கறுப்பு
எழுத்துக்கள் கொண்ட போஸ்டர்கள் அங்கொன்றும் இங்கொன்றுமாகக்
காணப்பட்டன. விடுதலையில் தொடர்ந்து மூன்று நாட்கள், அதே 'சேதி'
விளம்பரமாக வெளிவந்திருந்தது. இவ்வளவுதான், விளம்பர ஏற்பாடு -
இதற்கு, அன்று, ஜிம்கானா மைதானத்திலே வந்திருந்த கூட்டம் எவ்வளவு!
எதிர்க்கட்சிக்காரரும், உண்மையிலேயே, மலைத்துத்தான் போயிருக்க
வேண்டும்.

## English Translation

On the last day of October, the public meeting held by the Dravidar Kazhagam at the Gymkhana Grounds in Madras was an important event in the moral struggle it had undertaken. The meeting was like a preface to the history of the struggle, which had been suspended in the interval and was now to resume. It took place under the distinguished chairmanship of Thiru. V. Kalyanasundaranar. In other words, Thiru. Vi. Ka. gave his wholehearted support to the commencement of the moral struggle.

That noble man, worn down and transformed by labour and anguish, offered counsel that day to the prodigals who had come to rule the country. Is it not the nature of good people to believe that anyone can be guided onto the right path through good advice? In that spirit, at the place assembled to sound the drum of moral struggle, the great scholar advised the rulers:

“Rulers! Do not perish through arrogance! I speak after understanding the minds of the people of this land. Do not be vain over the Hindi question. Do not persist in futile obstinacy. Abandon the compulsory scheme. Do not resort to deceit; do not become conceited. Do not make Periyar your enemy. Think it over tonight and arrive at a good decision. Before the moral struggle begins, issue an order tomorrow itself declaring that the compulsory Hindi scheme has been cancelled. Seek the path of righteousness; seek the way of love.”

It was a moving speech. The arrogance already lodged in the hearts of those who had come to govern may prevent them from acquiring the character required to heed this counsel. Even so, there is a particular significance in what he said.

Before commencing a moral struggle, it is the duty of those gathering for it not merely to announce it but also to offer sound advice on what could be done to avert it. We believe that Kalyanasundaranar followed precisely this method. If the rulers' minds do not change after his good counsel, only one conclusion is possible: trusting in their strength, the rulers are challenging us to battle. The meeting held at the Gymkhana Grounds on November 1 made it abundantly clear that we are ready to accept that challenge. In the story of the moral struggle, that day's meeting may be called the first page of its second stage.

The Gymkhana meeting made clear even to rulers who refuse to notice anything not only that the moral struggle would certainly resume, but also that support for it was extraordinarily great.

The walls of Madras had been covered with advertisements described as three-colour, four-colour, four-royal, six-royal and lithographic posters. Amid them, as if to demonstrate how beautifully the printing trade had advanced, a curious poster appeared here and there: on white paper browned with age, in black letters mixed with white, it announced a Dravidar Kazhagam meeting at the Gymkhana Grounds. The same “news” had appeared as an advertisement in *Viduthalai* for three consecutive days. That was the full extent of the publicity. Yet what a crowd gathered at the Gymkhana Grounds that day! Even the opposition must truly have been astounded.

The next morning we happened to pass that way by car. “Look there, look there...” said younger brother Sampath, pointing towards the Gymkhana Grounds. It was one vast gathering of crows, busily at work. “So many crows have gathered to pick up the groundnuts dropped by those who attended our meeting yesterday,” Sampath said. He was not merely joking; he meant to illustrate how immense the previous evening's meeting had been.

The general opinion was that no gathering of such size had taken place in recent times. It was the first meeting in Madras after the Erode conference, and a vast crowd had assembled in eager expectation that the resumption of the moral struggle within the next two days would be announced. The publicity effort made for such an enormous meeting consisted only of the old-fashioned posters I mentioned first.

Young people naturally wonder how splendid the event might have been had this same meeting been advertised a little more generously and adorned appropriately. For some reason, we still leave that field unattended.

We travelled along Mount Road in our “motor van,” not expecting so great a gathering. Streams of young men, with mothers among them here and there, moved along both side paths towards the Gymkhana Grounds. When we drew near, we saw the immense crowd. The location of the stage could not be seen. This was not only because the crowd was so large; the stage had not been built high enough for such an assembly. The lights appeared as dim as the ministers' intelligence. Some flared fitfully and then went out, rather like the ministerial career of men such as Dr Subbarayan. The people, however, were present in multitudes.

In these circumstances the loudspeaker suddenly decided to take a rest. Those far from the platform suffered and grew anxious because they could not hear the speeches. Yet I praise their sense of responsibility: no shouting arose and there was no commotion. Such moments usually produce disorder, but the conduct of our comrades that day deserves the highest praise. Somehow the meeting ended; there was no facility for lengthy speeches. We also watched the people depart. They may have gone with disappointment that the arrangements were inadequate. But in the short time the meeting lasted, they clearly learned one fact: every preparation for the moral struggle had been completed.

A new arrangement had been introduced at the Thondaimandalam school: pupils attended and received lessons in two sessions, under a shift system [the parenthetical wording in the scan is damaged]. Consequently, the customary anti-Hindi picket could not be conducted there. “This has turned out very well indeed,” said the Muthialpet Dravidar Kazhagam comrades with delight, asking that the picket be held at the school in their quarter.

The field of the moral struggle was therefore changed. On November 1 we went to inspect the new location and its surroundings. Poet Abdul Khader, president of the Muthialpet Dravidar Kazhagam; its secretary Manimozhiyar (Manickavasagam); his son Ilanchezhiyan; and comrade Zakaria conferred enthusiastically with the leader. Seeing us in that area, members of the public began speaking of the next day's picket. Interested comrades gathered here and there in small groups.

It was decided that volunteers for the anti-Hindi picket would set out from the Muthialpet Dravidar Kazhagam office and proceed to the Muthialpet school on Linghi Chetty Street. The school had two entrances, one of which, we were told, was not ordinarily used. The volunteers would stand and picket at the entrance normally used by the pupils. If the school authorities decided to use both entrances, our volunteers would have to picket at both.

It is noteworthy not only that we enjoy tremendous support in Muthialpet, but also that the pupils of the Muthialpet school strongly support us. Wherever enthusiasm and support are greatest, our comrades must be especially careful to observe the point Periyar stressed: not even the slightest feeling of disorder or violence must be allowed to appear.

If we truly possess an unshakable faith that our struggle is just and that final victory will be ours, we must demonstrate our ability to preserve peace. There must be enthusiasm, but we must not forget that peace is its boundary. We must behave so that even our opponents are compelled to admit that our picketing is orderly, peaceful and dignified. The policy and programme we have undertaken give others opportunities to cast many hardships, humiliations and cruelties upon us. Our power of endurance must therefore be exceptionally great. Victory depends upon it.

At the meeting, the leader explained that members of other parties might dress like our party members, mingle with us and cause harm. Kazhagam administrators and those responsible for the moral struggle must keep this firmly in mind. They should entrust party work and the duties of the struggle only to seasoned comrades whom they know well, and avoid inviting trouble by assigning tasks indiscriminately.

Throughout Madras the conversation was the same: only one day remained; the anti-Hindi picket would begin tomorrow. Many conjectures arose about the government's likely response. Some said that this time too the volunteers would not be arrested for picketing, since picketing was not unlawful and the Chief Minister himself had issued a statement to that effect. Yet, they said, the authorities might arrest them on some other pretext, alleging that they had marched in procession or behaved in a disorderly manner, and imprison them without openly citing the picket.

Others said the leading figures of the Kazhagam would all be rounded up together. Still others said the ministerial party had planned this time to finish off the Kazhagam through a regime of baton charges. The warriors of the moral struggle were ready to face whatever method the rulers might adopt.

After the Gymkhana meeting, many people came forward to give their names for participation in the picket. Many mothers eagerly joined the front ranks. The government could not be unaware of all this news. Though the rising waves of condemnation caused it some alarm, the growling speech of Sardar Vedarathnam, who insisted that they must “try their hand” against us, seems to have produced in them a kind of intoxication, temptation and reckless courage.

Some are reported to say that their anxiety will not end unless the Kazhagam is dissolved; many ask why it should not simply be declared unlawful. The cabinet's course remains unclear: all is confusion there. Meanwhile, an enthusiastic section within the Dravidar Kazhagam continues to press the leader that this is not enough and that picketing should begin at Marwari shops as well.

Proceedings have been initiated under Section 107 against comrade Vallimuthu, the Kazhagam leader at Kovilpatti, and several other friends, requiring them to furnish bonds for good behaviour for one year. It is said that the government's plan may be to follow the same method and act against the Kazhagam's principal workers in every town. If the Kazhagam had only five or six people in each place, the government might try such a scheme. But if six are sent inside, sixty will come and stand in their place. That is the position of the Kazhagam. It cannot be overcome with the aid of Section 107.

Whatever dangerous experiments the government wishes to conduct are agreeable to us. Nothing will stop the wave of condemnation racing towards it. It is because the warriors of the moral struggle understand this well that their enthusiasm continues to rise. It will only keep growing.

The Kazhagam comrades had worried that the atmosphere necessary for the moral struggle might have dissipated during the days of respite. But as soon as the struggle resumed, that concern disappeared. The customary enthusiasm returned.

The physical setting of the Muthialpet school greatly assists the government's purpose. Its whole object is to prevent the public from learning how much support exists for opposition to Hindi. There are plenty of newspapers that efficiently perform the sacred service of preventing people outside Madras from knowing the true situation, however great an agitation may take place in the city. The government takes enormous pains to conceal the situation even from the people of Madras itself.

The government's aim is that if the picket can be hidden from people's eyes, their understanding too can be blinded. You may ask whether Congress ministers hope to survive with the aid of this blindness. What can they do? They must survive; for that purpose, they have acquired the courage to use any path whatsoever. They work according to a plan to prevent the force of the anti-Hindi agitation from spreading at any cost.

The newspapers impose a blackout, and the very sight of the picket is kept from the public. The government imagines that under these conditions the anti-Hindi cause will be neglected; that its activists will become anxious and ask what benefit there is in continuing this way; and that they will themselves abandon the picket. It also imagines that, even if they do not stop, the movement will grow weary of the lack of excitement and begin acting wildly for the sake of sensation. It could then be blamed and easily destroyed. The government's actions accord with this objective.

At the same time, the government has never considered how the Dravidar Kazhagam plans to use this calm atmosphere. Like a one-eyed man, it sees only its own scheme clearly and cannot perceive the other's. Taking this absence of excitement as the foundation for peace, the Dravidar Kazhagam is planning anti-Hindi pickets at more schools and in more towns. If the government continues on its present course, a way will soon open to conduct a picket at every school. The government knows how successfully and honourably token pickets were held in every town. Those who conducted them in the important towns are eager to continue in their own places.

As usual, the volunteers went to the Muthialpet school and began raising the slogans, “Down with Hindi! Long live Tamil!” The police stopped the public a considerable distance away. Police were present in great numbers, including the Malabar Police, along with several inspectors. They made sure that members of the public did not even proceed in the direction of the school. Assistant Commissioner Sukumaran supervised the operation.

Although the public's gaze was not upon them, the picketing volunteers performed their duty with joy and responsibility. Their methods consisted only of a gentle appeal and a respectful greeting.

On the opening day we learned of one small difficulty at the Muthialpet school picket. There was no shade near the school entrance, and so the volunteers had to stand and work in the fierce sun. Seeing them stand there streaming with perspiration, some friends who could not bear it went to hold umbrellas over them. With untroubled faces, the volunteers declined. They continued to stand in the sun, including the women.

The pupils were moved by the sight. They were distressed that these people were suffering to enrich their future lives, and that the intrusion of ruinous Hindi had caused them such hardship. They expressed their purpose through the slogans “Long live Tamil! Down with Hindi!” Most of the young pupils entered the school only after declaring, “We will not study Hindi!”

Teachers stood in different parts of the school watching the picket outside. Headmaster Perumal saw both the picket and the pupils. Who knows what he thought within himself? After one hour of picketing, the comrades began to return. Until then, the great crowd had been confined behind the police cordon. It now rushed towards the volunteers with the mighty cry “Down with Hindi!” The police again blocked its way. The Kazhagam volunteers themselves called for peace and directed the enthusiastic people to move elsewhere. The picket has continued every day in the same orderly manner.

“I have an idea!” a friend began.

“What is it?” I asked.

“To go and picket at the Hindi teacher's house...” he said.

“That is not our present programme,” I replied.

“Our Kazhagam has no such plan, but I hear that some lovers of Tamil who oppose Hindi intend to picket at the Hindi teacher's house. Why should we not do it ourselves?” he asked.

“Younger brother, your enthusiasm is welcome. But why is there such urgency now?” I asked.

“There is no urgency. But what are we to do if somebody else starts picketing the Hindi teacher's house before we begin?”

“What can we do about that, my friend? Have we surveyed everyone who possesses such enthusiasm? Some may set out, as you suggest, to picket at the Hindi teacher's house. Others may picket at the house of Education Minister Avinashi, who is responsible for all this. What limit can there be to such things?” I said.

“That is exactly what I say. Why should we not do it ourselves?” he asked again.

“For the present, we have no such plan. If someone else adopts one, we are not going to prevent it,” I said.

I record this only to show a government that thinks it can kill the picket through silence or ridicule what kinds of plans are taking shape in the people's minds. Support for the moral struggle continues to grow day by day.

That evening we boarded a boat on the coastal canal and sought a little rest. The boat moved with a gentle rocking. Above us, the sky was embracing darkness. As our boat travelled slowly, three or four armed policemen stood guard at the gate of a small mansion beside the neighbouring road. It was the Cooum mansion, the Chief Minister's residence.

From the boat I looked at the mansion and grieved as I thought how much distress its resident's scheme had caused throughout Tamil Nadu. Bulls in the prime of youth, who should spend their evenings strolling on the beach, taking boat rides, watching pictures and speaking joyfully with their families, instead stood in the harsh sun each day, performing penance to protect Tamil.

Why, I wondered, should the gifts brought by a Tamil becoming Chief Minister be danger to Tamil and suffering to Tamil youth? Why do ministers who attend to the most trivial matters refuse to understand that the language question has become so entangled that a moral struggle is necessary? Perhaps they think our struggle presents no danger to their offices and that, so long as they retain those offices, they need care about nothing else.

I reflected that they were ruining the very political principle that people can secure greater rights under a democratic government. We left the boat and sat by the shore. Waves rolled in, kissed our hands and ran back again. Here and there, men and women, children and elders conversed cheerfully in Tamil, in sweet Tamil.

If we do not wage a moral struggle and bar Hindi's entry, will we still be able to hear this sweet Tamil on this same shore ten years from now? Professor Sundaranar praised Tamil in wonder at its eternally youthful power. Tamil has crossed many obstacles; whenever dangers surrounded it, Tamil somehow protected itself. Now Tamil must overcome the danger brought by Hindi. Only if it triumphs will the sweet Tamil heard there continue to live in that place and throughout Tamil Nadu.

If Hindi is admitted and receives political status, what place will remain for Tamil? Who will cherish Tamil? The language of the government will naturally become the language of administration. Once it becomes the administrative language, will not everyone conclude that they can live only with its aid? If they think so, will they not abandon Tamil? I grieved over what would become of Tamil's antiquity and sweetness.

We returned home on a government bus. Two young Brahmins seated beside me were talking. One said that he would arrive at the other's house by eight that night. The other man's reply plunged me into sorrow once more. His exact mixed-language utterance is illegible in the scan, though the Hindi word *khana* is visible. He was saying, “I shall eat and wait for you.” Look at the path language has taken: English, with a Hindi word, *khana*, inserted in the middle.

This is the manner of speech heard even while we wage a moral struggle to prevent Hindi's entry. Had we not begun the struggle, would that friend have been satisfied with only the single Hindi word *khana*? Language is being ruined in this way. What doubt can there be that Tamil will disappear very swiftly if Tamils do not struggle to block Hindi's path of entry?

We reached home and found an incident that drove away the sorrow once more. Twenty young men stood there while Periyar spoke to them.

“Younger brothers! Do not be hasty. Go and remain in your towns. You may come when you are needed,” Periyar told them.

But they had no heart to return and remained standing there, saying they had come to take part in the moral struggle. Ministers claim that all this is done to obtain office. What office could those twenty young men have had in mind when they showed such enthusiasm? Their ardour clearly demonstrated that the ministers' allegation was a barefaced lie. My sorrow was dispelled.

The moral struggle continues, and various items of “news” keep arriving to say that victory too will soon and surely come towards us!

*Dravida Nadu*, 7 November 1948.

## Translator's Notes

The Tamil source contains a damaged parenthetical expression after “shift” and an unreadable mixed-language bus quotation. They have not been reconstructed. The surrounding prose establishes the first as a two-session school shift system and explains the intended meaning of the second; the translation reports only that recoverable meaning.

“அறப்போர்” is rendered as “moral struggle,” denoting a disciplined and explicitly nonviolent campaign. “மறியல்” is rendered as “picketing,” “அடையாள மறியல்” as “token picketing,” and “இருட்டடிப்பு” as “blackout” or deliberate suppression of news.

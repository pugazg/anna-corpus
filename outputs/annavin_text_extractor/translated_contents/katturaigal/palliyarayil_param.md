## English Translation

“Meenakshi, why are you so angry? Kamakshi, do my tender words not reach your ears? Neelayadakshi, can my heart be at peace while you remain like this? Akilandeswari, what wrong have I done you? Ambikai, look this way. Thyagavalli, turn around. Tripurasundari...”

Veeran said, “I heard the sound of such amorous persuasion. I wondered who was calling out the names of so many women at midnight. Then I saw Aalavayappan, Sokkan, Lord Siva himself, speaking with his lawful wife. Do he and the Goddess not possess thousands of names, a different one at every temple? That is why he called her Kamakshi, Meenakshi, Neelayadakshi, Ambikai, and Tripurasundari. But the Goddess did not move her lips.”

“Enough of your latest fabrication,” I said. “I have other work. I have no time for your tale.”

Would Veeran let it go? “Would you ever have the mind to hear my story? Am I a knight bearing the title ‘Sir,’ a landed magnate, a decorated poet, a bard, or a puranic storyteller? Of course you treat me with contempt,” he protested.

“Do not be angry, Veera. What you wish to tell is some new purana. So many different events are taking place in the country and so many opinions are being expressed. There is the government’s course and that of its critics, the positions of Congress and the League, the Dravidian agitation, the students’ awakening, the growth of art, and the stirring of connoisseurs’ hearts. With all these subjects before us, you drag in something else,” I said to pacify him.

Thinking that a true incident might satisfy the still-unappeased Veeran, I continued: “Listen, Veera. You complain that I will not attend to real events. There is a war in Italy, is there not? Hear of a marvellous act of courage on the battlefield of Cassino, and see Tamil fame blaze forth.

“It was a field where cannon roared, bullets split chests, and Germans attacked ferociously. The enemy had laid mines in many places. A detachment of Allied troops undertook the dangerous task of removing them. At that moment a naik stumbled upon a mine and its fuse caught fire. It was over: the next instant it would explode and tear the officer’s body to pieces. He did not know it. He did not know death had reached out to seize him.

“Do you know what happened, Veera? A Tamil son of an ancient and glorious people was there. He saw it. He did not panic or stand aside saying it was none of his concern. He did not merely shout, ‘Naik, move! Run! Save yourself! A mine!’ Without delaying even a second, he sprang upon the bomb and embraced it. It exploded; his body was shattered. The officer who was about to die escaped. The warrior perished, but his fame rose like a mountain. The deed performed at Cassino a few days ago proclaimed that Tamils possess the courage to save another’s life even at the cost of their own and the manliness of a heart unafraid in danger.

“The man was Subedar Subramaniam, a villager from near Walajabad. Crossing the sea, he revealed Tamil tradition to the world on the battlefield of Cassino. General Wingate, who demonstrated in Burma a new method of landing a small force by air behind enemy lines, died in an aircraft accident. Just as the world speaks of Wingate’s ability, courage, and tragic end, it marvels at Subedar Subramaniam’s heroic sacrifice. The lamp in the fortress of valour burns brightly. Seeing such radiant light, the Goddess of Freedom will recognize this as her own temple.”

Would Veeran stop? Was he one to submit to my diversion? He adjusted his voice and began again. I had no choice but to listen. Still hoping to silence him, I tried once more.

“Veera, have you heard? The Japanese have reached Imphal itself. Kohima too is in danger. The war has come near us,” I began, hoping to draw his attention from Siva and Parvati’s bedchamber to the battlefield.

“That was the very subject they discussed,” he told me.

“Strange indeed. Did Siva and Parvati speak of this too? I had been grieving that they did not turn their sacred gaze toward our direction,” I replied, knowing Veeran enjoyed mockery.

“Bharata, listen. Paramasiva took the angry Parvati by the chin and did something that made the Goddess laugh aloud. ‘Ah! Did it take you this long to smile? Has your anger gone?’ asked the tiger-skin-clad Lord tenderly.

“But the lady restored her moonlike face to its former expression. ‘When women become angry, men customarily say or do something to make them laugh and then achieve their purpose. Are you not the master of that art?’ she said pointedly. ‘You ask why I am angry and displeased. How could I not be? If many people revile a husband within his wife’s hearing, would her heart not be wounded? You can remain unconcerned, letting the world say whatever it pleases. I cannot.’ She wiped her eyes.

“Kapali opened his eyes wide. ‘Parvati! Who spoke degradingly and falsely about me? Tell me at once. I shall reduce them to dust!’ he cried in rage.

“‘See directly with your own eyes and hear with your own ears who insults you and how,’ said Kamakshi, leading Paramasiva to the upper storey. Quietly, without making a sound or being seen by them, I too went upstairs.

“Paramasiva saw the radiant full moon surrounded by countless stars in a spotless sky. ‘Beloved, look! The moon appears like a young princess strolling amid many beauties. At such an hour, in this moonlight, instead of speaking about someone reviling us for something, you and I might...’ Before he could finish, Parvati said, ‘Look there,’ and pointed to a scene on earth.

“I saw it, and so did Paramasiva. It was somewhere on earth. People carrying children and pulling one another along crossed forests and hills with tear-filled eyes, trembling bodies, and terror-stricken hearts.

Goddess, hear this injustice! They say that for a devotee named Sundarar, I carried a message to a courtesan’s house. Can such stories be written? What am I to do with the villains who revile me so and the wretches who deceive people using my name?’

“I felt one ought simply to say, ‘What should you do? Bring them here and make each study for six months in a Self-Respect school.’

“Meanwhile Parvati said, ‘Lord, hearing you has cleared my delusion. What can you do? People ruin themselves and have degraded us too. They have no path to recovery except the growth of reason. It is late, and I am sleepy.’

“At that same moment my younger brother struck my back and said, ‘It is late, get up!’ I awoke, and the dream of the conversation on Kailasa dissolved,” said Veeran.

“A fine dream indeed! And you came to tell it as though it were something extraordinary,” I mocked.

“Bharata, I have not merely told you. It must appear in this week’s newspaper,” Veeran insisted.

“Have you no other work? Go away! You dreamed that you went to Kailasa and heard Parvati and Paramasiva converse. Are we to print all that? A fine business!” I refused.

Veeran then dropped a bombshell.

“Very well, Bharata. If I dream, you consider it useless. If an Iyer had the same dream, it would become a divine utterance and fill page after page of the newspapers,” he said.

“No newspaper publishes whatever dreams people happen to have,” I explained, lecturing him on journalistic practice.

“Your memory is very poor,” said Veeran.

“Mine?” I asked.

“Yes. Many people’s dreams still roam the country under the name of puranas, believed and discussed by the public. Bharata, I do not even offer that as evidence. Put aside your one-and-a-quarter-anna paper. Do you not know that such dreams are published and planted in readers’ minds by newspapers run by Brahmasris, with great capital and abundant revenue?

“I shall give only two examples. Gandhi reportedly appeared in Birla’s dream and asked for money. That appeared in a newspaper! Its editor was no ordinary man: Devadas, the Mahatma’s son and our Acharya’s son-in-law. The dream appeared in his newspaper. Nor did it remain merely a dream; it brought money. Upon having that dream, the millionaire actually gave Gandhi money. If you doubt it, look at the Delhi newspaper *Hindustan Times*, an English newspaper, not your Tamil one!

“There is also *Swadesamitran*, a weighty paper born before the Congress. Is it ordinary? Do you know how much advertising a four-page issue carries? Such a newspaper published someone’s dream. No ordinary dream! You laugh when I say I saw Kailasa and heard Siva and Parvati speak. Similar news was sent to the editor of *Swadesamitran*. He did not laugh, Bharata. Unlike you, he did not preach journalistic ethics and ask whether such things could be published. He printed it beautifully and with ornament.

“Do you know the dream? Mahamahopadhyaya Srinivasa Sastri reportedly saw Maha Vishnu. At first there was a little light; then the light grew larger and larger. Finally Maha Vishnu granted a vision to the Saivite dignitary. To publish this, the editor of *Swadesamitran*, whose mind is otherwise absorbed in such mighty subjects as world economics, the course of the war, exchange rates, public finance, Churchill’s errors, Stalin’s inattention, China’s diligence, and America’s concern, found space and care.”

Devotees may have seen people with great vertical religious marks rolling upon the Tirupati hill. Whether Lord Venkateswara resolves their endless karma may be left aside. Rationalists’ mockery of this uncivilized display of devotion may be left aside. The fact that Brahmins are clever enough to display devotion without rolling about in this fashion may be left aside. So too may the dreadful sight of women descending the hill in an unbearable appearance after losing their dark hair. Whatever karma the Lord of the Seven Hills may cure, one thing can be said with certainty: he cannot cure even a speck of the antiquarian madness of Hindu Mahasabha leaders. Anyone in doubt should observe Dr. Moonje.

What a strange man! His speech is heroic, but his practice remains that filthy Sanatana orthodoxy.

An effort is underway to establish a college from Tirupati Devasthanam funds, and there is a proposal to amend the law accordingly. Dr. Moonje opposes it. He clamours, “Can Hindu money benefit others?” and asks whether money belonging to a religious institution may be spent on another purpose.

If it is true that love is God, is it proper to say the money must not be spent on anyone outside the Hindu path? They philosophize that knowledge is God. If those who say it believe it, can they oppose the use of temple funds for the growth of knowledge? If love and knowledge are unwanted, what else do they expect to find in the temple? Are dark corridors, bats circling within them, pot-bellied crowds of priests, and the images they touch and worship enough?

What is the Hindu Mahasabha’s intention? Dr. Moonje’s speech reveals what policy would prevail if that party held power. The new world claps its hands and laughs at such lovers of antiquity!

16-4-1944

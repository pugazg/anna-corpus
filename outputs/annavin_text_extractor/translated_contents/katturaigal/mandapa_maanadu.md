## English Translation

“The Vedas arose for the welfare of the world.

“Merely hearing the sound of the Vedas is sweet and auspicious. Learning them with an understanding of their meaning is best of all.”

Agnihotram Narayana Thathachariar, a “most eminent Brahmin,” reportedly spoke thus this month while inaugurating a Vedic recitation at the Perumal temple in the little village of Navalpakkam. The 20 August issue of *Mithran* lovingly published this “news,” giving it space equal to that accorded reports of the strenuous efforts of national leaders to prevent another world war.

At a time when nations are anxious about how to remove the want of peace in the world and the misery in people's lives and usher in a golden age, let some Thathachariar find sweetness in thinking about the glory of Vedic sound if he wishes. But why should a daily newspaper, responsible for informing the town about world events and preparing people for a new world, show such concern for it?

Beyond the fact that a Thathachariar speaks in a temple and an Iyengar publishes it under alluring headlines in his newspaper, what else does it reveal?

It would not be right to say that there is no truth whatever in the claim that merely hearing Vedic recitation brings a certain “sweetness.” After the recitation, are not the sweet pongal—made richly delicious from cashews, ghee, raisins and sugar—the carefully prepared ven pongal, with pepper in measure and ghee in abundance, and the other “prasadam” all tasty? Those who have eaten them can testify to their excellence!

Thathachariar's claim that merely hearing Vedic sound produces “sweetness” makes sense only in this fashion.

He says that the purpose of the Vedas is “the welfare of the world.”

Not only did “welfare” mean something different in Vedic times; the very world known to the Vedic age was different.

The Vedic meaning of “welfare” was that every person should conduct life along the path Brahma allotted specifically to him. The “Brahmin” must not labour, hold a plough or engage in trade. He must study and teach, perform sacrifices and have them performed, and occupy himself with such “divine work” for happiness in this world and the next. To enable him to complete that work without hindrance, everyone else must toil until the chest aches, surrender the fruits rather than keep them, giving them as gifts, fees, offerings and taxes, and continue contemplating the heaven “yet to come,” however poverty pours down and suffering assails them. This was the practical meaning of “welfare” in Vedic times.

When one next considers the “world,” one feels like clapping and laughing.

The Vedic “world” did not comprise America, Australia, Africa, China, Europe and Asia. It did not contain oceans such as the Atlantic, Pacific and Arctic, rivers such as the Nile, Thames and Amazon, or mountains such as the Alps.

Seven worlds below and seven above, an ocean of milk, an ocean of ghee and an ocean of curd—that was all.

This one difference between the Vedic world and today's world is enough to show how useless and tasteless a husk Vedic knowledge is for modern life.

Yet a Thathachariar speaks about it, and an Iyengar carefully publishes it in a daily that carries news from across the globe. Such people imagine that suitable propaganda about the excellence and greatness of the Vedas can make that age sprout again.

There remain great numbers of ordinary people who have not yet understood the direction in which the world is moving or the path shown by true knowledge, and who have had no opportunity to understand. Thathachariar says that great wisdom will arise if they learn the meaning of the Vedas.

Members of Thathachariar's own community say, “Grammar or onions—forget them and find a way to earn a living, Pichu!” They find some well-paying occupation as doctors, engineers, lawyers or commission agents, live their lives, and in their leisure attend the temple-hall conferences conducted by Thathachariars and “listen to scripture.”

Even Nehru occasionally speaks about the excellence and glory of Sanskrit, as though saying, “Remember, I am not merely Jawaharlal Nehru; I am Pandit Nehru.” But he delights his grandchildren by showing them the banks of the Rhine, the slopes of the Alps, Paris and London.

The man who spoke of Vedic glory at the “conference in the temple hall” later raised a lament:

“The number of people learning the Vedas is steadily declining. If this condition continues, it will soon become difficult to find anyone versed in the Vedas among the country's future generations.”

If the Vedas seemed as necessary as Thathachariar claims, would not Rajaji, C. P. Ramaswami Iyer, the late Right Honourable Srinivasa Sastri and Sivaswami Iyer have been running Vedic schools?

That is not what we see.

In the very newspapers that publish Thathachariars' sermons on Vedic glory, we find a Ganapathi Iyer writing about fish farming, a Sabesa Iyer about profit in the footwear trade, and a Natesa Sastrigal about the efficiency of a match factory.

For the welfare of the world, let the distressed Thathachariar who says the numbers are “steadily declining” try telling the people of his own community, “From now on, all of you must recite the Vedas; other occupations are evil and sinful,” and restrain them!

The *mamis* will run at him in fury, brandishing their papad rolling pins and asking, “Has this madman no other work?”

What reaches their ears today is news such as: Komalam's husband has a job in Canada; Amsal's husband owns a company in America; Raja's brother-in-law is an engineer in Russia. If Thathachariar went to them and spoke of the glory of Vedic sound, would it enter their ears? It does not, and that is why the number studying the Vedas steadily declines.

“Though born and raised in wealth, these young men do not immerse themselves in pleasure like members of other wealthy families. They lead good lives as workers. Their journey to the West and return will therefore undoubtedly benefit the country.”

Karumuttu Thiagarajan offered this blessing at a farewell he held when four young men were leaving for America.

This happy function took place in Madurai. Four young men planned to travel to America for industrial training, and friends held a celebratory farewell banquet in their honour.

The sons of great wealthy houses could spend their days in entertainment and revelry. They could have a golden plate studded with diamonds brought beneath a golden standard, pick and taste the fruits upon it, and coo to a slender, peacock-like maiden: “My dear, when fruit was here, I plucked the unripe one! Come closer. When there was a way to enjoy pomegranates, mangoes, sweetness and the fragrance of jasmine all together, fool that I am, I remained deceived by tasting sliced mango and grapes picked out for me! Only now do I understand the meaning of your smile.” Wealth provides the opportunity for such pleasures.

Yet these four do not sink into such enjoyment. They travel to America to acquire technical skill. Seeing this, Karumuttu warmly praised them.

There is a great deal to be done in industry. Karumuttu was delighted by the hope that their return after training in America would help Tamil Nadu's industrial development.

Though born into wealthy families, the four who leave for America to gain industrial expertise rather than drown in luxury are R. Ramachandran, S. Ramachandran, K. Ramesh and K. Suresh.

Some may wonder whether Suresh and Ramesh are Bengalis. All four belong to the ancient city of Madurai!

The young shoots of the family of T. V. Sundaram Iyengar, now a leading industrialist in the South, are going to America—not to the Ahobila Math!

Thathachariar stands wailing in the temple hall that fewer and fewer people are learning the Vedas. Members of the T. V. S. family set out for America to learn the intricacies of the motor industry!

Why would the number not decline? Where are these four going? Not to Bharadvaja's ashram, but to America!

Not to sit upon deerskin and memorise scripture, but to enter factories and learn their techniques!

Not to gather sacrificial sticks and learn the excellence of *sandhyavandanam*, but to gain technical knowledge of how a boiler works and how a generator is assembled!

Can the Thathachariars stop them?

“The T. V. S. family could establish and operate a large motor factory in Tamil Nadu. There is no doubt it would succeed if they began one,” says Thiagarajan, inferring that this will be their future plan.

When members of the Brahmin community itself are moving toward this condition, what purpose is served by gathering at conferences in temple halls and speaking about the excellence, greatness and glory of the Vedas?

26 August 1956 — *Dravida Nadu*

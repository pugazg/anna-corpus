## English Translation

Beside the pond stands a lofty, flourishing marudha tree. Some of its branches bend as though wishing to touch the water. A boy climbs the tree, facing the pond. “What a fearless heart he has! Look—he is climbing higher and higher up the marudha tree, leaping towards its crown!” the girls playing beside the pond exclaim in wonder. The adults, meanwhile, speak in voices mingling amazement and fear: “The little scoundrel! See how high he has climbed!” The boy who has mounted the marudha tree stands upon a branch. Like a pole-acrobat, he launches himself towards the water below—he falls—a “thud” is heard. The elders are startled. But the rascal plunges beneath the surface, soon emerges again, and even brings up sand in his hand.

Will the other boys fail to applaud the child who has climbed to the crown of the marudha tree, stood upon its bending branch, plunged with a thud into the pond below and emerged again? And there are girls beside the pond too. Some run over and embrace him! He embraces them in return. He joins hands with some of the girls and plays. With others he runs and romps! Some hide, and he finds them, drags them back and adds to their delight. Beside that body of water, joy overflows. An age ignorant of the world; a heart into which deceit cannot enter; a beautiful pond; a towering marudha tree! The instant the old man stands beside that pond, just such a scene appears before his mind’s eye.

A staff in his hand! A body that trembles even while he stands leaning upon it! When he speaks, his voice has no strength; his words come only in little fragments; a cough troubles him from time to time. In this condition the aged man stands beside the pond. It is he who sees this scene: the mischievous boy climbing the marudha tree and jumping into the pond; the girls playing with him; even the “thud” made when he leaps into the water seems to ring in his ears. To that old man, it seems he can even see the adults on the bank recoiling in alarm and wondering what will happen when the boy jumps from such a height. Standing there and watching this vision that has appeared before his inward eye, he delights in it at first—and then reflects.

“Unknowing youth! Yes! That is how joyfully we spent the unknowing days of youth. Now the body trembles! Back then—ah!—how swiftly and fearlessly we climbed and leapt from so tall a marudha tree. Now there is a staff in the hand! And then? We plunged beneath the water and came back with sand in our hand. Now there is coughing! Then there was laughter—song! Now we stand upon the bank with a staff in hand, the body trembling and a cough tormenting us. Then we sang and played with the girls upon the sand.”

Thus he reflects. He looks at the height of the marudha tree, then looks again at the pond. He looks at the branch and at the place all around. He even hears the “thud”! Youth! The state in which one played in delight, knowing nothing! Youth will never return! When he recalls the joy of those youthful days, when he remembers that it is a youth he can never regain, he even feels sorrow.

The old man thinks in this way—but who was the boy who climbed and played upon the marudha tree? He himself! The man who leapt with a “thud” then is today an old man, troubled by an intermittent cough and speaking only in short phrases. With the hand that once sprang up and seized the topmost branch of the marudha tree he now braces himself upon a staff. Gone is the state in which the branch swayed and he swayed with it. Now, even while he stands supported by the staff, his body trembles. He stands upon the bank, recalls his youth, and sorrow burrows into his heart.

There is a poem expressing this thought in the *Purananuru*, composed by the distinguished poet Todithalai Vizhuthandinar.

We have cited this poem, which explains the nature of both the condition and the thought, not merely to reveal the cast of mind of an ancient Tamil poet, nor merely to speak of yearning for youth in old age. We cite it so that this fine Tamil poem may serve as a goad to reflect upon the many “angles” within the general truth that thought accords with circumstance, and to consider the many questions that arise from it.

The old man standing with a staff in his hand could have stood even beside the marudha tree while absorbed in thoughts of something else. But before his mind’s eye appears a scene from days gone by. Yet in those days—when, as a boy, he had climbed upon a branch of the marudha tree and looked down at the pond, ready to leap—would the decrepit age in which he stood with a staff in hand and his body wasting in tremors have appeared before the boy’s eyes? It would not! In old age, memories of youth can arise; in youth, no thought arises that old age will make one become thus and so.

Although a longing that youth would not return may have arisen when the many deeds of his youth appeared to this old man as scenes, that longing would change the next moment. For the passing of youth and arrival of old age are nature’s doing, not a misery he brought upon himself. Nor is he alone: must not every young person become old one day? Therefore he can find peace of mind by reflecting upon nature’s law, by which youth changes and old age arrives. But what would become of him if he acquired old age while still young? The sorrow that there once was a time when he stood upon a branch of the marudha tree, and that today the time has come when he stands upon the bank with a staff in hand, may arise—but it will vanish the next moment. Suppose, however, that while the boy stood upon a branch of the marudha tree, with the girls crying out in wonder and the adults recoiling in fear, and while he plunged with a thud into the water and rose again, he suddenly became an old man, trembling with a staff in hand, coughing and spluttering, “Alas, get me to the bank!” What would such an abrupt change be like?

Those well versed in the Sangam works must investigate and tell us whether any poet has imagined such a sudden transformation and composed a poem about it. We do not know. Yet we are not writing this merely to indulge in such an idle fantasy. We do not know whether it existed in the Sangam age, but in our own days we now behold the sight of a valiant young man who climbed the marudha tree and leapt down, emerging from the pond with the decrepit body of an old man, all aquiver.

Where? When? You will ask, “Why are you spinning such a tall tale?” Here, we send the fantasy away. We state the truth that stands embracing it. Then tell us whether what we are about to describe is, or is not, like the youth upon the marudha tree becoming an old man with a staff.

Pandit Jawahar has suddenly aged ten years!

That is, old age has suddenly come upon Pandit Jawahar—not in nature’s course, but for another reason!

We trust that the poem will help us understand the character of old age and the thoughts it produces.

It is natural for that old age to grow day by day; it cannot be avoided. But for the Pandit, old age did not come in keeping with nature. It arrived suddenly and unexpectedly. As much ageing as ten years could bring has suddenly overtaken him!

Friends may imagine that we are mocking the Pandit whom all the world praises. But our claim that Jawahar has suddenly grown old is no fabrication of ours: Sardar Patel said it last week.

When old age comes in nature’s course, some sorrow may arise, but peace follows, because one thinks that this is something that can happen to anyone. But when unexpected old age arrives in youth, it gives rise not only to sorrow but also to fear.

“What is wrong with you? You have suddenly grown thin as a splinter!”

“Typhoid. It is only by God’s grace that I survived. Did you not know?”

We know conversations of this kind take place.

“What is this, man? Your face looks somehow different. You look like an old man.”

“It is nothing! I have not shaved—that is all.”

We know this conversation too.

“Look how perfectly that young man has been transformed to resemble an old man!” We have heard such remarks in theatre halls.

Patel says that Pandit Jawahar has suddenly acquired ten years of age. Why? Worry! Worry! Old age in youth is the result of the disease of worry.

The assaults of imperialism, repeated imprisonment and the sorrowful separation from his companion—none of these could assail Jawahar’s youth or impose old age upon him; they tried and failed. Yet wretched worry has been able to make Jawahar’s youth suddenly fade and bring upon him, all at once, as much old age as would otherwise come only after ten years! This worry has been able to inflict greater harm than the white man’s harsh methods of repression.

Like a man possessing the fearless heart and youth to climb and leap from a marudha tree, the Pandit ascended the seat of government. He leapt and sported even amid the circumstances of international politics—and suddenly, old age!

When events turn out contrary to expectation; when one begins an undertaking confident it can be done, only to learn midway that it is exceedingly difficult; when one’s calculations prove false; when what was sown fails to sprout; when enmity arises from friends; when, while painting a picture, the vessel of colour breaks; when sandal paste turns into a medicine that irritates the body as one applies it; when the petals of the rose turn to thorns; when a string of the veena snaps and the broken string flies swiftly into one’s eye—will old age not come in youth?

With what confidence—with what power and opportunity all around him to nourish that confidence—the Pandit ascended the throne! India alone? People said he would guide all Asia. In keeping with that claim, the Asian Conference was held. His colleagues became governors! His sister was in Moscow! Comrades who had acquired political kinship like elder and younger brothers were in the political arenas of Europe!! In circumstances like these, how much confidence must have been born in him; how many plans must have flowered in his mind; how many beautiful pictures of the future must he have painted! He must have imagined that independent India would flourish as prosperous India, that *swaraj* would flourish as good government.

He said all this. Where did it all lead? It brought about the condition in which the unflappable Patel says that old age has arrived in youth.

When the Pandit ascended the seat of power, telegrams arrived from foreign countries saying, “We congratulate you—we applaud you.”

Now what arrives by telegram is: “Come! Send aircraft! Help evacuate the refugees.” There is unceasing trouble in the North; fighting in Kashmir; a food shortage; difficulty in textile production; suffering among workers—not one trouble or two, but a thousand. Unexpected assaults come not from one side but from every direction at once. Except for the smile he laboriously summons when he sees Meera, the thought “The calculations I made are proving false” gnaws at him at all other times, kindling worry and bringing him old age.

How much better was the state of the poet who, in old age, sang sorrowfully that youth had gone and would not return! This man’s state brings still greater pain. Not only is youth becoming old age in defiance of nature; he is also assailed by the anguish, “What am I to do? No undertaking succeeds.” They said the Kashmir war would end in the blink of an eye. Instead it demands four and a half lakh rupees a day, and the war keeps on demanding. The expense caused by the refugees has become enormous. The Finance Minister does not refuse to offer reassuring words. But his speech does not dispel the people’s worry. An emancipated country has no money to spend on the very things that should create a new awakening in its people. Plans exist in bundles! Experts exist, forever dreaming! The great nations of the world are watching, waiting to judge the quality of the Pandit’s government. Here military expenditure is rising—and rising in a way that can only continue. Baldev speaks. During the Hindu-Muslim conflict the provinces speak with one voice, but at other times Assam refuses to trust Bengal, Bihar despises Bengal, the thought that Maharashtra must blossom begins to bud, and Dravidam awakens and asks, “Why, sir, should I retain a connection that brings me pointless trouble?” Thus arise problem after problem.

In this situation Kripalani, who had been president of the Congress, stated bluntly that the course of the government was wrong and that he therefore could not remain Congress president. He can only issue a statement; the Congress has enough strength to prevent him from doing anything beyond that. Newspapers supporting the Congress have cleverly mounted a campaign to keep people at home from reflecting upon the deeper meaning of Kripalani’s statement. To that extent, the Congress has indeed succeeded. But the world does not remain idle after merely seeing the headlines of newspapers published here. Consequently, although Kripalani’s condemnation may find no market at home, abroad it has become a mirror revealing the Congress’s present condition. The young Congressmen celebrating their festivals do not know this. The Pandit, however, knows its meaning and its consequences well; he cannot help feeling exhausted.

Moreover, although the Socialist Party’s campaign here continues to include the greatness of Gandhi and the courage of Nehru as constituent parts, to the outside world the Congress government appears to support capitalism and encourage reactionaries. Workers, students and radicals do not like this. Their departure to found a new party creates the impression that the Congress is, more or less, like the Conservative Party in Britain. The young Congressmen do not know that the growth of this impression is harmful. The Pandit knows it very well, and so this condition causes him worry. That is why old age has unexpectedly overtaken him in his youth.

I said, “Beat the drum!”—did I not?
Not knowing they were hunters’ heads,
I trusted plans would make all this shine;
Now there is a ruinous darkness.
“This rule is evil,” says my friend;
“It has no affection,” say many.

The Pandit is in a state of melting anguish and speaking in this way. It is not like the old man’s recollection of youth that we see through the Sangam poem; it can bewilder the mind. It has taken old age out of the category of a change of season in life and brought it into the category of disease.

Those who know medicine say that indigestion is the foundation of every disease. Excessive quantity and food whose nature harms the body both cause indigestion. Even honey is harmful when the measure is exceeded; even a little unripe fruit is harmful. Just as bodily injury occurs in this way, the harm that assails the mind, confuses thought and brings old age in youth is also caused by indigestion in the political sphere. Admirers refuse to recognise this because it would obstruct their own attachments and desires. The responsibilities, worries, troubles and afflictions that Pandit Jawahar has now taken upon himself, together with his colleagues, exceed all measure—they are enough to cause indigestion. From the problem of relations with the world to the problem of measuring out a small measure of rice, the Pandit’s government today holds everything in its own hand. The cart is loaded until its axle is ready to break! Can such a condition work? From the problem of the Assamese tea estate to that of the Kodaikanal coffee estate, they have retained authority because they insist that their supervision must extend everywhere. Provincial chief ministers await the “post” and supply information to the people under their direct administration. So great a “concentration of authority” could disrupt even ordinary conditions—what will happen amid the present confusion? Must we not reflect upon it?

Every province possesses the facilities and ability needed to attend to its own affairs; it has not been given the opportunity. Quite apart from the resulting damage to the provinces’ welfare, the size of the “burden” placed upon the Central Government demands the closest attention. Indigestion has set in. From industrial development to the prevention of troubles, every province can conduct its own affairs properly. Why take everything and heap it upon one’s own head, invite torment by force and make old age enter youth? If we examine the stories of the ancient empires of this subcontinent, we can understand what immense dangers this indigestion created. What caused Aurangzeb’s empire to decay? The Central Government kept every power in its own hands and collapsed beneath an unbearable weight of responsibility. The Pandit’s government has now mistakenly decided that the very system rejected as unnecessary everywhere else in the world—the concentration of all powers in the Central Government, the system of “Over Centralisation”—is the one suited to it. They suffer from indigestion.

The plan that there must be a powerful Central Government is the emperor of emperors within a democratic system! Before republican government began to bud, the king assumed responsibility for every matter. His duties extended from repelling a foreign invasion to protecting the home of a husband who had gone abroad. In just that way, when only the elementary lessons of democracy were known, the idea arose that there must be a powerful Central Government. In practice, political thinkers saw that this bound the Central Government with strength, starved the provinces and produced a system ruinous to both. They condemned it and advised that the Central Government should possess strength and authority only for a few specified tasks that could not be accomplished separately and required joint effort; the Centre must not be invoked for everything.

The English were foreigners. They made the Central Government powerful to facilitate repression. We neither need it nor will it do us good. At first, naturally, the mind will refuse to recognise this. For the person seated at the centre of the Central Government will certainly take a special delight in saying, “Everyone is subject to my command—every action must be taken only after asking me—I alone am the primal power—I alone am the beginning and end of authority.” But that delight, that temptation, has brought old age in youth.

The British have today turned the empire on which the sun never set into independent countries. Why? Foresight! They understood that they could not hold and rule them; that there was no profit in holding and ruling them; that doing so brought needless trouble; and that the peoples would not submit to restraint. They cured their indigestion. Here, however, the Pandit’s government has welcomed that disease. We say this with the best of intentions: authority on this scale will assuredly cause harm. It is the reason the Pandit has suddenly grown old.

If the condition in which youth departed and old age arrived in nature’s course caused such yearning in Todithalai Vizhuthandinar, how much grief must old age in youth have caused the Pandit! The poet climbed the marudha tree, leapt into the pool, rejoiced and played with the women; later, he took up a staff. Even that caused sorrow. Here, the Pandit climbed the marudha tree, leapt into the pool, and rose to find that his youth had gone and old age had come. Since we do not know how to compose poetry, we stand bewildered, not knowing how to describe this condition.

Bending where the high-branched marudha meets the bathing-place,
climbing the step-ledge close to the water in splendid style,
as those upon the bank stood amazed and the waves scattered,
into the deep-water pool, plunging with a thud,
bathing and bringing up sand—my unknowing youth!
Is it lost? Where indeed has it gone?
Leaning now upon a strong staff with ornamented head, trembling,
uttering a few words broken by coughing,
we have become the very old!

This is the poem found in the *Purananuru*. It is the portion that portrays the old man’s yearning over where his youth has gone. To describe one who acquired old age while young, we propose to use this very poem with a slight alteration:

Bending where the high-branched marudha meets the bathing-place,
climbing the step-ledge close to the water in splendid style,
as those upon the bank stood amazed and the waves scattered,
into the deep-water pool, plunging with a thud,
without a strong staff, trembling,
uttering short words broken by coughing,
I have become a very old man—what is this condition!

(The marudha—the towering tree—is the Indian subcontinent. The pool—the pond—is political authority.)

There is indeed a way to change the distressed condition expressed in this fashion. Abandon the scheme of concentrating every power in a single place. Entrust each territory to the administration established there, and arrange matters so that political indigestion cannot arise; then there will be no difficulty. But where is that thought? Is it going to arise soon?

“We shall no longer permit India to be partitioned!”

Thus speaks Sardar—and Dr Subbarayan plays the same record here! The word “partition” implants a mistaken idea in people’s minds. What is being asked for is not cutting the country to pieces. The plan is: avoid indigestion; eat in due measure; act with regard to place. Sardar distorts it by calling it partition. A Dravida Nadu? It cannot be granted! Maharashtra? Do not ask! Provincial autonomy? Do not speak of it! This is how Sardar speaks. “We shall not permit India to be partitioned!” Valour is lodged in that roar—not wisdom! A great army supports that roar; no foundation of reason does. He supposes that we will be astonished by it and cower in fear. But because the roar seems familiar, as though we have heard it somewhere else before, we feel neither astonishment nor fear. Yes! Churchill roared, “I did not become leader in order to dissolve the British Empire!”—the empire dissolved!!

(*Dravida Nadu*, 25 January 1948)

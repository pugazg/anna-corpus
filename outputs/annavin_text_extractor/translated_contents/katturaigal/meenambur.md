## English Translation

Last week, police opened fire in the village of Meenambur in South Arcot district.

Though shootings have become commonplace under the Congress government, the incident in this village is shocking.

Nine people were shot dead. About twenty people were injured.

Nine lives became corpses, all in one place and within a few seconds: nine human lives!

The guns are said to have thundered for thirty rounds.

This is not merely information that has reached us. It is stated in the government's own report.

Meenambur is a tiny hamlet. Yet an incident occurred there in which thirty rounds were fired and nine people lost their lives.

The reason given for this horrific event is that customs officers, having received information that various goods had been smuggled from Pondicherry into the Meenambur area, went with police assistance to uncover them; the villagers resisted, and a disturbance consequently arose.

Although Pondicherry is a small territory under French domination, it still remains outside Indian rule. Goods imported there from foreign countries are therefore available very cheaply within that little territory. Because they are so cheap, many people are tempted to bring various goods from there, sell them here, and make money; and many do so.

No one would say it is improper for the government here to seek to prevent this and take appropriate measures.

But if, in the name of prevention, a village is battered with bullets and many people are killed, no decent person can refrain from condemning it.

Many members of the Madras Legislative Assembly have insisted that a public inquiry be held into the Meenambur incident.

We too wish to make that same demand.

Preventing improper activity may be the government's responsibility. Running amok for that purpose is not conduct consistent with humanity.

The government says that firing became necessary because the villagers attacked the police officers.

Police officers too are human beings, and it is certainly essential that their lives be protected from danger.

But why did that require firing thirty times? The villagers were not armed combatants. Even if they held knives and sticks, would not one gun roaring into the sky have been enough to suppress their disturbance?

Why, then, was it necessary to fire so many times?

It is the government's responsibility to answer this question.

*Dravida Nadu*, 9 September 1951.

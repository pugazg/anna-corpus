## English Translation

"What beauty! What skill! It looks like a living figure! How can one adequately praise the talent of the sculptor who made this statue?" There is no one who does not praise it. It is the statue of a great warrior seated upon a horse, sword in one hand and chest thrust forward. Everything from the horse's hoof to the sharp edge of the sword is manifest in the statue. Yes, the sculptor made it with consummate skill. But the statue alone has no head.

Those who see it grieve and say, "It is certainly a beautiful statue, but it has no head." How might the face of so majestic a warrior seated upon a horse have looked? Would not a sculptor of such ability also have shown the fire flashing from his eyes, his brows drawn tight, and his moustache quivering? "What a pity! The statue has no head!" An event that leaves Tamils lamenting in this way has taken place in the land of Thanjavur.

It is reported that a festival for Rajaraja Chola—one of the three crowned Tamil kings, a patron of the threefold Tamil arts, and a ruler who caused the flag of the Tamil land to be respected in many parts of the world—was celebrated at the Great Temple in Thanjavur on 7 November 1943.

Festivals of this kind can help instil Tamil feeling and love of country among Tamils, together with the awakening that comes from understanding ancient excellence and distinction. Yes, it was a worthy undertaking. But the manner in which it was conducted makes us say that while the statue is beautiful, it has no head.

The purpose was good and the plan elegant. But should the day have been reduced to a barren affliction of reciting texts? If qualified speakers at the Rajaraja festival had explained the courage, greatness, and intellectual ability of the Chola kings, their valour in planting the flag of victory in Burma, the history of flourishing trade in their age, and the splendour of their arts and enlightenment, Tamils might have acquired self-awareness and courage.

Festivals honouring heroic Tamil kings such as Cheran Senguttuvan, who defeated Kanakavijaya and brought back stone, Rajendra, who conquered Burma with a navy, and Kulothunga, who conquered Kadaram, should be used to create fresh energy in the weakened bodies and minds of Tamils, dispel fatigue, kindle enthusiasm, and make strength surge like a spring.

Today Tamils celebrate all manner of festivals, become playthings in others' hands, lose their nature, and suffer hardship. Is it not the duty of responsible people to commemorate properly the great days that reveal Tamil Nadu's distinction, in a manner that gives rise to genuine devotion to the Tamil land? They forgot this.

There was a festival at the Thanjavur temple, with an Ananthanarayanaiyar presiding. There was reportedly a discourse on the story of Kannappar and a torrent of words from Kirupananda Variyar. Is it the work of Tamils to connect this puranic rubbish with a festival for Rajaraja? Should Aryanism, ever ready to say "Give me the slightest opening and I shall enter," be given a place at the festival of a distinguished and heroic Tamil king?

Are there no speakers in Tamil Nadu who could address subjects fitting for a Rajaraja festival instead of giving puranic discourses? Are there no experts in the history, wealth, valour, and commerce of Tamil Nadu? Giving a place at festivals of Tamil kings to religious expounders who know nothing beyond the story of the man who lost his eyes and the story of Ramalingar, who wore sacred ash up to his ears and sang of Kanakasabesan's dance, may bring those speakers distinction and may please people who merely want some noise for their ears. But we ask: how will it enrich Tamil Nadu?

Are Maraimalai Adigal, Bharathiar, Kathiresan, and Kalyanasundaram lying about with no one to hear them? Do they not know Rajaraja's history? None of them was to be seen. There was no exposition of Tamil history and no festival honouring valour. It seems that in the land of Thanjavur they found two puranic preachers to lecture and an Aiyar to preside.

What further example is needed to show how greatly the land once ruled by Rajaraja has deteriorated? Is it not because Tamil Nadu remains in this condition that, at nearby Tiruvaiyaru, students of the Sanskrit college refuse to dine with Tamil students and now receive their allotments of rice and other provisions to cook and eat separately?

With this discrimination existing so close at hand, it is said that Guhapriyar and Kirupananda Variyar preached the doctrine of religious harmony. We ask whether a Rajaraja Chola festival is anything but a calamity for a Tamil Nadu whose heart does not tremble and whose skin does not prickle even upon witnessing this discrimination. Shame, shame!

(*Dravida Nadu*, date printed with OCR damage as 28-]11-43)

## Translator's Notes

The article is a historical polemic contrasting Tamil historical commemoration with puranic and Sanskrit-centred programming. English attributes its claims and rhetoric to the essay rather than treating them as independently verified facts. The sculptor's gender shifts in OCR; English uses a gender-neutral noun. The damaged publication line likely contains a date, but it is retained without silent correction.

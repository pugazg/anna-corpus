## English Translation

"We must prevent disease from arising. It is better to protect people from its arrival than to stop it after it comes. If people are given sufficient clean water, good milk, enough food to fill their stomachs, and good housing, how can disease arise?"

She is a lady of royal lineage. Many people wait to serve her in her palace. She does not know hunger or starvation. She may have seen with her eyes the sunken gaze, the heart ready to burst, and the stomach throbbing and boiling, but she has never known them through experience.

Yet she delivered this rare address: Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, a member of the Kapurthala royal house and still seated as a minister in the central government without any reduction in royal comfort.

The princess's gracious words are greatly to be welcomed. It is certainly far better to protect oneself from germs before they arrive than to seek them out, let them enter the body, and then run to a doctor.

When she issued this advice, she also expressed anguish about India in her capacity as Health Minister:

> I am very frightened. The ration available to the Indian people continues to decline. We do not know what to do. This condition has persisted in India not for one or two days but for many years. Will this lack of sufficient food not ruin the people's bodies? If the body deteriorates, how can disease be prevented from attacking them?

She also spoke sorrowfully: "Even famine may be relieved, but it is impossible to prevent this ruined condition."

The place where she spoke was neither the Indian legislature nor an interview in Delhi.

It was London.

She expressed this anguish in London, capital of the white imperialism against which they once sounded the trumpet, saying that only when it disappeared would a life of happiness blossom.

What must the people upon the banks of the Thames have thought upon seeing the princess's sorrow?

"The princess was deeply distressed, it seems."

"She said there was no food to eat. Her speech showed that she did not know what to do."

"Indeed! When we were there, so many troubles did not arise. How is it that they do so only now?"

"We too governed the same India that exists today. Are not the officials who served under our Viceroy still in office?"

"Why then did this disaster arise?"

Could such mockery and ridicule have failed to arise?

When those who once spoke heroically now plead, "I will even wave the white fly-whisk for you if you will remove our suffering," will observers not laugh? Especially when people who established a new government after declaring that imperialism was intolerable express pity and sorrow in the capital of that very empire!

The princess surely knew that ridicule and sarcasm would arise there over the words of today's rulers. Even so, she expressed pain and sorrow.

Perhaps she had the good intention of describing India's misery there to obtain their sympathy and thereby secure food supplies for India.

If the princess spoke as a result of that good intention, her heart deserves praise.

If, desiring to wipe away the people's suffering, she described India's distress there in support of her efforts, disregarding ridicule and mockery, her good intention deserves respect.

Many problems arise under these rulers today precisely because false pride makes them fear that telling the outside world the true condition would disgrace them.

They have devoted immense effort and attention to advertising abroad that they can accomplish everything and that the people's lives prosper under their government.

Disasters increased because, considering truth shameful, they failed to reveal the suffering.

It is therefore praiseworthy that the princess avoided becoming a victim of this empty pride among Congress ministers and described India's anguish.

But in the same London, Krishna Menon, serving as India's representative, released certain information only four days after the lady raised her anguished cry:

> It is true that India has a food shortage. But there is no famine.

> Claims that many people are dying without food are contrary to the truth.

This fine gentleman, enjoying many thousands of rupees from Indian tax revenue in comfort and luxury, gathered foreign correspondents and announced this information among them.

It seems there is no famine in India. It seems no one dies for lack of food.

Living six thousand miles away among white people as one of them, V.K. Krishna Menon casually released this information.

Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, a minister of the central government, expressed anguish about the food situation.

Menon, however, spoke to foreign correspondents with empty boastfulness.

This empty boasting is the cause of many of the troubles India now suffers. To win the praise of others, can one call a turkey a peacock of the forest? Can one say a golden lotus blossoms in a dried-up pond?

Even if someone said so, who would believe it?

Yet Menon scattered such empty pride.

No one today is unaware of conditions in India.

It is no secret that the Indian government continues appealing to foreign countries for food.

Nor is the whole world unaware that many people collapse and die from starvation.

Many countries have come forward sympathetically to help India. People in many places regard helping the Indian people at this moment as an honour and wish to send whatever they have at hand.

To that extent, they have understood the suffering here. That is why they have come forward to help out of humanity.

Yet Krishna Menon says there is no famine here and no one dies of starvation.

The Health Minister, eager for suffering to end, explains the distress endured by her people.

The Indian High Commissioner, however, boasts emptily: "There is nothing of that sort. Do not think so."

Both are envoys of the Congress government, yet they offered contradictory views in a foreign country.

What will people in London think? Will they not conclude that these rulers not only created distress through incompetence but did not even acquire the decency to tell the truth?

Will the ridicule not then increase? More than that, will enthusiasm for helping relieve India's food situation not diminish as a result?

If exposing a wound is considered a loss of honour, the outcome will become disastrous rather than improve. Covering the truth will increase danger, not extinguish it.

It is exceedingly necessary that today's rulers understand this truth.

For each person to roam about speaking as he pleases will only harm the country. Along with that, the country's honour and respect will be scattered to the wind.

Congress leaders must impress this truth upon the representatives who wield power, especially those who serve as India's representatives abroad.

Empty boasting has produced many calamities.

Let the misery caused by flattering claims that milk and honey flow everywhere grow no further.

Do not screen off the truth. Do not be ashamed to describe what exists. Regard the people's welfare as paramount. Enough storms have arisen in their lives from the empty desire that the world should praise the rulers as "great geniuses." Let them continue no longer.

Even if they do not know how to govern, let an honest heart at least become visible.

An unseen internal wound is always dangerous. Internal swelling causes immense trouble.

Covering it will only widen the suffering and increase the disaster.

There is benefit in revealing the truth, as the princess did in London.

What gain comes from concealing it for false prestige? A cry of longing will spread: "There is no famine, no starvation, no hunger!"

We appeal to the Congress leaders who, raised to the throne by circumstance, roam about declaring that whatever they establish is law: do not bring disasters through empty boasting.

*Dravida Nadu*, 17 June 1951.

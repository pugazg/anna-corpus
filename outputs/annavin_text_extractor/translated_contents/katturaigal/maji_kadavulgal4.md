## English Translation

Why does rainwater that nourishes crops and people turn saline upon reaching the sea? Who first cut out this vast basin? Its waves smash ships, and even a flooded river destroys villages. How powerful must the sea be; what if it crossed its bounds? Early humanity trembled yet longed to know its origin and end. Waves and storms raised waves of confused thought until an imaginative storyteller created Poseidon.

Surely a mighty god must restrain this powerful sea and prevent it destroying the world. He must possess primal power like Zeus. Thus imagination bore Poseidon; religion developed the invention; temples rose, devotees gathered, and offerings accumulated until reason’s light appeared.

Because Greeks travelled by sea often, they had more occasions to invoke Poseidon than Zeus. They remembered and worshipped him amid high waves, sacrificed to him in danger, and performed special rites after returning safely.

His image had a massive body, a crushed enemy underfoot, and a trident in hand, almost like Mahesa trampling Muyalakan while bearing an axe. Ask in Greece today for the temple of almighty lord Poseidon and one receives a smile and the explanation that he existed before society entered the school of reason. Poseidon is a former god.

Our festival-goers may wonder why atheists abandoned him, while Greeks would wonder why anyone seeks Poseidon in the twentieth century. Religious advocates here ignore the world’s laughter and still seek the dancing foot with dancing women’s aid.

Before knowledge grew, Poseidon enjoyed no small fortune. Corinth held a splendid temple; seafarers piled offerings like mountains and priests prospered. Corinth and descendants of his devotees remain, but the worship does not. They recognized Poseidon as one image born from ancestral ignorance. What had been God became imagination.

After overthrowing his father, Zeus assigned realms to his siblings on condition that all accept him as chief. Poseidon received the sea. He ruled from a great underwater palace, long-bearded and holding a sharp trident. His chariot’s horses had golden manes and jewel-like hooves.

Before him, the Titan Oceanus ruled there. Seeing the new god’s power, the old god surrendered his kingdom and praised Poseidon. In Greek divine history, “old god” and “new god” were not jokes but sacred sport. Gods lost wars like kings and sometimes gave women in marriage to settle disputes. Today one may ask whether divinity behaves so; then belief alone was reason and theism.

Royal brothers envy the eldest’s throne, quarrel over lesser domains, and wage war; divine families were portrayed identically. Stories made gods slaves to jealousy, intrigue, violence, theft, and lust, yet priestly power forbade doubt on pain of death. Divine character ought to purify human minds, but these tales instead made vicious heroes objects of worship.

Today questions have multiplied, but ancient Greece treated them as sin. Some here marvel that such an age existed. The real marvel is that in our age of aircraft, motors, and nuclear ships, tales of war and rivalry among gods remain sacred, recited as sources of devotion and liberation, with even government-backed orthodoxy ready to attack critics.

A hunter bearing bow and quiver is ordinary; a fashionable man carrying them in an expensive motorcar is strange. Likewise, ancient Greek mythology before intellectual development is unsurprising. The wonder is our coating ourselves with modern civilization while still believing Madan, Kadan, Mannar, Mari, and gods whose stories glorify competition, war, and pleasure.

We still call sacred the story that Brahma and Vishnu competed over supremacy and Siva became an endless column of light, challenging them to find his top and bottom. Annual fire festivals at Tiruvannamalai commemorate it. Like an adult village guard finding his childhood companion still spinning a top in old age, developed nations may look upon us with concealed amusement. We now travel by modern car instead of cart to see the Karthigai flame; appearance changed, not disposition.

When knowledge dawned in Greece, Poseidon became ridicule and then a former god. Before that, festivals, crowds, temples, and stories flourished.

Envious that Zeus ruled the cosmos while he had only the sea, Poseidon plotted secretly to overthrow him. Zeus discovered it, cursed his brother, and cast him to earth as a mortal. Poseidon approached King Laomedon of Troy, who promised royal reward for a strong city wall. Thus the sea-god became a mason, like our Lord carrying earth for pittu to build the Vaigai bank, though Greece no longer celebrates this tale.

Apollo, likewise banished for opposing Zeus, offered help. His magic flute made mountains and rocks roll into ordered walls. When the dishonest king refused payment, Poseidon created a monster to devastate Troy. One asks why a being capable of creating a monster needed Apollo’s help to build a wall, but consistency is forbidden in sacred stories.

An oracle prescribed sacrificing a beautiful maiden annually to stop the monster. Eventually the king’s daughter Hesione was chosen, whereupon Hercules killed it. Had this been our sacred story, its festival might continue today.

After his curse ended Poseidon returned to heaven and resumed rule with Zeus’s permission. His wife Amphitrite, one of fifty sisters, initially fled his frightening form. He sent a sea-horse to persuade her and married her. Their notable son Triton was half human and half fish.

Poseidon once lent his chariot to the mortal Idas to elope with Marpessa against her father Evenus. They escaped the father but encountered Apollo, who desired her. A divine voice ruled that she choose. Wisely, she chose mortal Idas: Apollo would remain young when she grew old and abandon her, whereas Idas would age beside her. Poseidon rejoiced at Apollo’s defeat.

Homer sang Poseidon, artists made statues, and cities built temples. With knowledge people recognized that ignorance of the sea did not justify inventing stories. Science supplied understanding; Poseidon vanished and became former.

Greeks and Romans once portrayed and worshipped handsome male gods like Krishna and Murugan. Their beautiful sun-god Apollo was no less attractive.

## Apollo

Devotees here praise lotus-eyed, cloud-coloured Krishna and tell critics to ignore implausible stories and admire the beauty of image, sculpture, and poetry. Many falsely imagine portraying God as beauty is our exclusive cultural property and that no other imagination produced figures like Krishna and Murugan.

Apollo’s paintings and sculptures show that Greek artistic sensibility was refined, yet he became a former god. Greeks did not retain worship merely because the art was beautiful. They judged Apollo an irrational invention incompatible with true theology. Those claiming only our land could carve captivating gods should look at him; no honest observer can deny his charm and gracious eyes.

Apollo was Zeus’s son, not by the chief queen but from his broad kingdom of love. Zeus loved Latona, who bore twins: Apollo and a daughter, both peerlessly beautiful. Hera learned of it, sent a great serpent after pregnant Latona, and frightened everyone from sheltering her. Poseidon fixed the floating island Delos in place so she could give birth there.

Hera continued persecution. When thirsty Latona asked people beside a pool for water, they mocked her. Zeus transformed them into frogs. After further suffering, Latona gained honour and temples at Argos, Delos, and Egypt.

Apollo mastered archery, chariot-driving, and music and drove the sun’s golden, storm-swift chariot from morning to evening, then travelled in a golden boat. Avoiding moon, stars, and earth was a task where one mistake meant destruction. Despite this responsibility, he found ample time for love.

He loved the mortal Clymene and fathered a son who became a physician able to revive the dead. Temples rose and pilgrims believed one night there cured disease. Jealous Zeus ordered Pluto to obtain a thunderbolt and kill this new god. The healer who raised the dead died. Apollo raged against the makers of the bolt, so Zeus expelled him to earth for a time before restoring him.

Questions abound: who drove the sun chariot meanwhile, why was the son never introduced in heaven, did Apollo marry the mother, and why did supreme God kill one doing good? Priests prohibited these as atheism until people exposed their deception in Greece.

Apollo often suffered in love. When he mocked Cupid’s bow, Cupid shot him with desire for Daphne, daughter of the river-god Peneus, and shot her with aversion. Apollo pursued while she fled until, exhausted, she begged her father to transform her. He made her a tree, leaving Apollo frustrated and Cupid triumphant.

## English Translation

You wrote, under the title “Down with Varnashrama,” that the study of art might become an Aryan pit into which Tamils could be pushed. An admirer has asked: “Since Sir Shanmugam is interested in Tamil art, will that not bring good rather than destruction? Explain.”

The Aryans know well that, as people go on reading the ancient epics, their awareness of the Aryan-Dravidian struggle will fade. That is why, even when they speak and write about modern intellectual questions, they connect those questions with ancient texts. They have two purposes, and both create obstacles. One is to prevent the birth of a modern outlook by claiming that there is no new knowledge in today’s world and that everything already existed in antiquity. The other is that, if people remain governed by such reverence for antiquity, Varnashrama will endure. Both purposes help keep the country’s people oppressed and turn flatterers into uncrowned kings. That is why I said that an Aryan crane sits anxiously beside the stream of art, and asked you to keep watch upon it.

“Let no opportunity escape! Fail not to turn everything to your advantage!” This has been the Aryan ideal for two thousand years. In accordance with it, they made art itself an instrument for introducing their domination. A fish leaps in delight because food sways before its eyes, but the hook becomes its gallows. In the same way, Tamils who dance before antiquity thinking that they are merely going to behold artistic beauty become caught in Aryan domination.

Here is a small incident. It occurred in the home of a gentleman devoted to the modern world. It is not imaginary, though propriety requires me to mask the name; I ask your pardon for doing so.

“Come in! We did not see you until late yesterday evening. Where had you gone?” asked the modern-minded man.

The devotee of antiquity replied, “I went to see Bharatanatyam. I experienced supreme delight. The performer was first-rate, the expression magnificent and the music graceful.” A desire to see the dance arose in the modernist. It was solely for art’s sake! He went. The dancer beautifully interpreted a song beginning, “Thief who stole my heart the moment I saw you.” The lady too had arranged it only for art’s sake; that is another matter.

Later the antiquarian told the modernist that Nataraja embodied the philosophy of dance. An idol was purchased. The modernist wished to discover the meaning of dance in the image of the figure dancing with raised foot, and nothing more. Each morning he looked at the statue and contemplated artistic beauty. Then the antiquarian said, “You are letting the idol go to waste. If you do not bathe it, moss will gather and spoil it.” The modernist agreed. He began bathing it, not as ritual ablution, but simply to keep the statue clean.

“Tie a strip of silk around the gleaming statue and see; its beauty will be incomparable,” said Mr Antiquity. Mr Modernity replied, “Is that such a great matter? Bring a piece of Kollegal silk and a dhoti.”

“The flowers that wither in your house because no one plucks them may perfume your room. Pluck them, make a garland, and place it upon the dancing figure,” said Antiquity. That too was done.

“With all your work, how can you attend to every one of these things in the morning? Employ an Iyer to do it,” Antiquity advised. The Iyer duly arrived. Mr Modernity began going there each morning to see whether the man did his work properly. The Iyer held out a packet of sacred ash. Within a couple of weeks Mr Modernity had learned to sing the hymn “With Bent Brow,” to stand at a distance and worship the dancer, and reverently to accept the vermilion and sacred ash supplied by the family-temple priest. Mr Antiquity wrote in his notebook, “We have extinguished one spark.”

I have seen many people begin with art in this fashion and proceed until they fall into the Aryan net. That is why I say, “This is the stratagem the Aryans conduct in the name of art. Beware!”

The *Mithran*’s statement that acquiring artistic appreciation by reading the old epics is a poison that will kill racial awakening deserves deep thought. There are many ways and many methods by which one person may enslave, bewitch or overthrow another. Warriors fight with the spear, making their strength the staff of their authority. Those without courage but with deceitful hearts hide and strike, declaring that any method is acceptable for destroying an opponent, as the Aryan Rama killed Vali from behind the trees.

There are scoundrels who pose as friends and accomplish their purpose through treachery. There is the method of buying an opponent’s brother or servant and defeating the warrior with the weapon of a Vibhishana. An enemy may be destroyed by seductive speech, or through knowledge of his foolishness. The world contains many kinds of men. Even a warrior who can seize an elephant by its trunk, whirl it round and dash it down may fall like a tall palm severed at the root beneath the sidelong glance of alluring women or beneath the weapon of a smiling lip.

The gods feared that, if nectar were given to the asuras won by Mahabali, the divine world would become an asura world. Hari therefore took the form of a beautiful woman, danced, sang and deceived the asuras. In a country where that trick is praised as divine play, there is nothing surprising in others treating the form of Mohini as a spear with which to bring warriors down. If the Almighty himself is said to have destroyed adventurous asuras with cunning as his discus, will ordinary people refrain from employing the same method?

Just as there are various means and instruments by which individuals overthrow one another, there are many methods by which one race subdues another. The torch and the sudden assault form one method. At times mutiny has been created in an army and its camp destroyed. At times people have been ruined by approaching them closely and embracing them. Throughout the world, different methods have been employed in conflicts between peoples.

The Aryan people did not defeat the Dravidians with any army; those outsiders did not possess the ability to wage such a war. The method the Aryans used to turn the Dravidian people into Aryan servants was to push the Dravidians into the stream of art. Saying, “Behold the beauty of our art,” they enchanted the Dravidian people, made warriors into cowards and defeated them. They did not merely defeat them then. Even today, and forever hereafter, the Aryan people believe that the maddened elephant called Dravidam can be controlled by the goad called art. There is some truth in that belief.

Malaiyandi resolved to renounce the illusory world. He put on ochre robes and went into the forest. He opened his eyes only when severe hunger overtook him and continued his hard penance. One evening he saw jasmine in bloom, enjoyed its fragrance and murmured, “Good, good.” At night he prayed for the Lord’s grace and slept. In the morning he went to where he had seen the jasmine, but the flower was gone. He forgot Maheswara for a few moments and began wondering who had plucked it. Seeing a footprint nearby, he became somewhat angry. He decided that he would catch the thief red-handed the next day.

“This body is false,” he said, closing his eyes beneath a fruit tree. In the evening he went to the jasmine bush. There he saw a full-moon-faced woman standing with a jasmine smile, gazing at the flower. “O speaking flower that plucks the speechless flower!” he called. The woman bowed and went on her way. It took the master of devotion a long time to draw his eyes away from that direction.

The next evening, before the maiden arrived, he plucked the flower and lovingly gave it to her when she came. As he offered it, hand met hand and thought met thought. On the following day the ascetic roamed the whole forest from morning onward, gathering flowers of many kinds. In the evening he ran to the jasmine bush, plucked the blossoms, and asked his heart why that lovely woman had still not arrived. When she came he displayed the heap: “Here is jasmine; here is mullai; here is a cluster of champak; here is even a red lotus.”

“O holy man, so much trouble for this maiden?” she asked. Because she asked that day, he abandoned the sacred ash he had earned through penance, as well as austerity, daily worship and meditative discipline. His time and thought were now devoted to gathering flowers, making garlands, seeing the woman and giving flowers to her. Through the benefit of “Shakti worship,” he passed from the hermit’s order into the householder’s order, abandoned the meditation “I desire no bond with the illusory world,” studied domestic virtue, and became the best flower seller in that town. So runs a story.

Given the present condition of art, I think the fate of one absorbed in it ends just as Malaiyandi’s did. A life beginning with artistic appreciation turns a person into the companion of a learned ritualist, then a devotee, and finally an Aryan slave. Art has been constituted in that fashion.

Aryanism entered even Sangam works wherever it found a gap. After the last Sangam, works in which Aryan doctrine was the inner substance and art its armour multiplied. In Sangam literature we see a hero comforting his beloved, who was frightened by the cry of an elephant. Later we find the story of Velan sending his elder brother in the form of an elephant to frighten Valli and marry her entering every home. The Sangam portrait is known only to a few, while those who do not know the Puranic rubbish are very few indeed.

In Sangam literature we see the loving male monkey plucking fruit and feeding its mate. Later we encounter Hanuman, the all-knowing scholar who uproots the Sanjivi mountain. Few know the noble work that spoke a truthful image; those who celebrate the fabrication are very numerous.

For the Tamil who read poetry portraying the lovely fidelity of a stag standing by its doe, the story of Maricha’s enchanted deer was later transformed into art. Once there was a nature that knew and delighted in the qualities of the mullai, kurinji, neithal, marutham and paalai landscapes. Later came the condition of writhing in the poisonous pool called the four varnas. Under the name of art, Aryan imaginings were scattered into Tamil thought.

Knowing this well, the *Swadesamitran* thinks: “Let Sir Shanmugam read the ancient epics to acquire artistic feeling; by that very means he can be crowned.” It supposes that the method which brought down crowned kings and warriors will surely catch Sir Shanmugam too. If a venomous snake lies in a basket of fragrant flowers, will not its poisonous fang strike the hand that searches for blossoms? If love for the old epics increases, there is a path by which Dravidian character can be ruined.

The *Swadesamitran* cast out that suggestion because it knew this. I do not prevent Sir Shanmugam from plunging into the ocean of epic literature to savour artistic excellence. But Aryan waves beat in that sea; sharks called Sanatana roam there; and there is a wide-mouthed whale called Varnashrama. If I failed to warn him, I would have forgotten my affection for him.

His movement through the world of art must not carry him into Aryapuram. Absorption in artistic enthusiasm must not turn him into a Muthurangam who asks, “What does it matter whether one is Aryan or Dravidian?” Nor must it turn him into a scripture-reciting devotee who says, “Why remain so troubled about this earthly world? Should not our thought and action concern the next world?”

6 February 1944, *Dravida Nadu*

## Translator's Notes

The Reporter's Predicament

“Was the crowd large?” asked the editor, sitting in his revolving chair.

“Yes. St Mary's Hall was filled to overflowing; the crowd spilled out along the outer veranda and into the street like a flood. It was like one of those meetings the anti-Hindi campaigners hold,” replied the reporter. Besides telling the editor that he had done his work conscientiously, he wanted to show that he also possessed the ability to describe the news vividly. The editor was not pleased. “You simply have no habit of answering briefly. When I ask whether the crowd was large, instead of answering in a single word—yes or no—you stretch it out like a novel. Who can keep listening?” he scolded. Previously, this editor had issued strict orders to give clear, detailed, explanatory accounts without leaving out a single thing. The reporter did not understand why he was now getting angry. Even if the anger was groundless, the reporter had to put up with it, did he not? This was no unpaid service!

“What was special about the meeting?” asked the editor. The reporter did not know what to say. He did not know what to omit and what to mention. The whole meeting had been special. The crowd was special. The decorations were special. The sight of the leaders seated on the platform was special. Desikar, Devasena, Bhagavathar, the gathering of admirers insisting that he sing just one song, N. S. K., his debate song about women and men, the worthies who had forgotten even their prohibition campaign for one evening and come there—everything had been special. Fearing that the editor might get angry rather than listen if he related all this, the reporter answered the question about what was special at the meeting with “Nothing.” “Idiot! You say there was nothing special at all! Was it to come back and tell me this that you went there? By ten o'clock, you must write up the whole matter clearly and in detail and send it to my house. By ten o'clock I mean tonight, not tomorrow morning. I am going to a party; I shall return to the bungalow before ten strikes,” the editor said angrily. Muttering “Tamil, indeed! Music, indeed!” he bounded down the stairs two or three steps at a time and slammed the car door—perhaps to vent his anger at the reporter, or, thinking “Have all these people nothing to do? Why do they go off to this Tamil music conference like this?”,

because he could not contain his anger! When the car had disappeared, the reporter scratched his head, worried about what to write.

The Reporter’s Tribulations! The Arrogance of Editors! Journalism and Self-Respect! The reporter could have written a whole book under one of these headings. So many thoughts occurred to him that night. But what the editor had told him to finish writing was an account of the Tamil music conference. What the editor had told him to finish writing was an account of the Tamil music conference. At home, the reporter began directing his anger at the editor towards his wife. But his son was sitting on the raised veranda beside the street, singing N. S. K.'s song, “Can you match a man, can you match a man wearing a dhoti?” He had been to the Tamil music conference. The reporter forgot his anger and anxiety and laughed. Along with his laughter, an idea came to him. Hurriedly he opened his notebook and looked through it. “You all understood what I sang because I sang in Tamil. Because you understand, you are happy. That is why the Tamil music movement says that if one sings in Tamil, a Tamil person will know and understand.” So it stood in his notes. It was a passage from the speech Comrade N. S. Krishnan had delivered that day. After supper the reporter began writing and finished within an hour. At ten o'clock he went to the bungalow to see the editor. He tossed a complimentary cinema ticket to the attendant who said he could not see him, and went inside. A dog barking loudly made him tremble. Repeating its name, “Jimmy, Jimmy,” as though chanting a prayer, he subdued its fury. To the editor's voice asking “Who's that?” he gave the customary answer, “It's me.” He handed over the bundle of papers and cleared his throat. Seeing the editor's hand speak, he left the bungalow and went home. From the house opposite he heard a record of Bhagavathar's “Is there anyone who does not lose their heart at the sight of you?” playing in sublime bliss. He went into his house.

Madras, 23 December 1943

Today (Thursday, 23 December 1943), at 4.30 in the afternoon, the Tamil music conference took place with great splendour at St Mary's Hall, Madras. Just as people frightened by the sounding of an air-raid siren rush towards the A.R.P. shelters, pushing one another aside, each trying to get ahead of the other, vast numbers hurried towards the hall. There was not room for a sesame seed to fall inside. If there were more than twenty leaders on the platform, the crowd that had come to the conference that day was large enough to make twenty separate enormous meetings! Such a magnificent gathering! The crowd extended as far as China Bazaar Street. So many stood in the street, thinking that even if they could not see, they could at least hear the speeches,

Sir R. K. Shanmugam, Thiru V. Kalyanasundaranar, C. R., Dr Subbarayan, Kumararaja V. V. Ramasamy, Coimbatore Palanichamy Naidugar, Sir Venkatasubba Rao and his wife, T. P. Meenakshisundaranar, S. Muthiah Mudaliar, Abdul Hamid Khan, C. Thathulinga Mudaliar, Kachapakesa Mudaliar, Indrani Balasubramaniam, Meenambal Sivaraj, the editor of Kalki, Kumaran's editor Murugappa and many others were seen on the platform. They had gathered in the province. Muthamizh Kala Vidwarathna T. K. Shanmugam had also come, as had Ramasamy, an actor in their M.S.P.S. troupe who was currently acting in the film Poompavai.

To relieve the people of the misery of being jostled and having their ears tormented by noise, a feast was provided even before the conference began! Desikar sang! In Tamil! Kanakasubburathinam—Bharathidasan—sang of sweet Tamil as the fine nectar bestowed upon our life: was that a lie? Those who heard Desikar sing that day lost themselves in the sweetness of Tamil. Desikar himself, elated at the thought that Tamil, hitherto used only for thukkadas, had drawn such an immense gathering to Tamil music, forgot himself, embraced Tamil and poured forth music. Perhaps knowing that the king of comedy, N. S. K., was later going to preach equality between men and women at the conference, Devasena Ammaiyar and a lady from Thiruvananthapuram sang after Desikar. When this musical welcome ended, the conference began. Rao Sahib P. Sambandha Mudaliar, who delights in immersing himself in the pleasures of Tamil's three arts, related the history of Tamil music in his welcome address. Tamils know that the Rao Sahib is no mere admirer. He is a playwright and an actor. His plays can be found throughout Tamil Nadu. He does not enter the whirl of politics, but he is not unaware of the pace of the times. He is a lover of art, but not a stubborn man who holds that the art of the Stone Age alone is suitable for the present age too. His observations are therefore no empty talk. However good the undertaking we have taken up may be, many people want to know whether it is practicable. Apart from a few, people will not enter into an undertaking unless they acquire the confidence that it can be accomplished. To them, the chairman of the reception committee said: “What was the condition of Tamil music fifty years ago? What is its condition today? Even a brief examination of these questions will make them declare that the present is a fortunate time for it.” The Rao Bahadur pointed out that Tamil music was spreading in the four fields of Tamil music concerts, Tamil kalakshepam, recitation of the Tevaram, the Tamil stage and cinema, and regretfully condemned the practice, on Tamil theatrical

stages, of singing in Telugu. He welcomed those attending the conference with the good news that Tamil music was necessary, that awareness of this necessity had spread, and that constructive work was taking place. The Raja of Chettinad inaugurated the conference. Why speak only of the conference? Was it not Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar who had inaugurated the Tamil music movement itself? He knows the principle, “Think before undertaking a task”; he knows how to make an undertaking succeed. His enthusiasm for the music movement supports the Tamil people's renaissance.

If the Raja of Chettinad's sole aim were to hear Tamil music and obtain its pleasures, conferences and public meetings would not be needed to secure it. Nor would he need willingly to endure the resentment of people harbouring some other prejudice in their minds. With Desikar and Bhagavathar beside him, Raja Sir could spend all his time enjoying Tamil music. Why, even those supposed “oceans of musical gems” who oppose Tamil music—if he offered them a hundred instead of fifty, would they not come forward to delight him by singing exquisite Tamil songs? Raja Sir does not wish to hear and enjoy Tamil music all by himself. He has the public aim that Tamils should hear Tamil music. That is why he founded the Tamil music movement. As he said in his speech, “It is both the duty and the right of every Tamil with a feeling for Tamil to take part in this service to the arts.” Not to perform that duty is foolishness! But what are we to call the conduct of those who, besides failing to do their duty, oppose those who do it? It is sheer cruelty. It is because such beings have no rightful place in Tamil Nadu that Raja Sir said, “There cannot be a Tamil who prevents Tamil from resounding in Tamil Nadu.” The meaning implicit in his “cannot be” is “must not be.” Are there not people in Tamil Nadu who ask, “What is all this nuisance?” Recognizing this, the generous benefactor said kindly, “Tamil music is not new. The Tamil music movement is no invasion, nor is it a sacrilege. Tamil is the language of this land. Tamil music is the musical wealth belonging to this land. Tamil music has flourished since two thousand years ago.” Sri Tiger Varadachariar, who sent a message to the conference, likewise explained this point: “They have reckoned the Tamil panns at 1,991.” Yet some oppose even this Tamil music movement, which is neither new nor an invasion, and whose merit is to restore the splendour of an ancient culture—this thought aroused

anger that made Mrs Indrani Balasubramaniam say, “Musicians must sing in Tamil in Tamil Nadu. Otherwise, I warn them, they will be taught a fitting lesson.” If anyone still opposes Tamil music after Raja Sir's clear and kindly speech, we can only say, as Comrade Krishnamurthi, the editor of Kalki, put it, “From 1944 onwards, no one will sing that discordant refrain.” A certain Subba Rao is said to have declared that language is not important to music! Of him Raja Sir said, “Mr Subba Rao too has become somewhat confused by partisan zeal. Having measured music with a crooked yardstick, he sees as ugly something that favours beauty.” See how delicately the saying “Everything looks yellow to the jaundiced eye” has blossomed here! Raja Sir also explained why the Tamil music movement had needed to arise. “Songs in other languages appeared only very recently. Some people enamoured of songs in other languages erected a barrier.” Knowing that this barrier would not endure, the Raja of Chettinad asked, “How long can this barrier against nature stand? How long can children be kept apart from their mother?” Through these questions he tells Tamil Nadu that the barrier will not last, that it will crumble. Having given many arguments in support of Tamil music, Raja Sir also shows its opponents an easy way—perhaps wondering whether they would have time to consider all these points properly and reflect at leisure, since angry people are so impatient. When the Raja of Chettinad said, “Those with eyes can see the public support for Tamil music. Those who wait can hear and enjoy Tamil music in every direction,” the people's enthusiasm was considerable. Yet some have the habit, even after seeing and hearing, of letting their minds say one thing and their mouths another. Even after the music conference, their drumming mouths have not fallen silent.

There is no need to argue one's case or ask forgiveness of those raising a din against Tamil music. If I stand upright and ask for justice, what is wrong with that? Such was the view that day of Coimbatore's son, Sir Shanmugam. “Whether Tamil is a language suitable for music or not, whether it contains beautiful kritis or not, whether Tamil music existed two thousand years ago or not—I do not know all that, nor do I care to know. Whatever music exists in my Tamil is enough for me. Only when I hear songs in my mother tongue, Tamil, does my soul find satisfaction. I need neither give reasons for this nor ask forgiveness,” said Sir

Shanmugam, and the hall shook with the people's joyful cheers. In those words he portrayed the Tamil people's heart. There was no Tamil who did not burn with anger at the thought: “Is there to be obstruction and opposition even when a Tamil asks for singing in Tamil?” What is the Tamil person's condition today? Listen to Sir Shanmugam's speech. “If you want a job, you must study English. For marriages and funeral rites you need Sanskrit. To earn the name of patriot, you must study Hindi. To enjoy music, you need Telugu. Where is Tamil?” When he finished saying this, the people's applause would have tormented even deaf ears. Our friend Achariar, who had been turning a deaf ear, was right beside him. Sir Shanmugam described Tamil's condition with overflowing humour.

“‘I considered the matter you mentioned yesterday; I shall tell you my idea’—isn't this the Tamil we hear?” he asked. Should this strange state of affairs not change? He referred to the Chinese scholar Lin Yu Tang's remark in America that, in food and music, people like what is their own. He then said that, while eating that country's exquisite food in America, he had remembered idli and sambar. Sir Shanmugam's speech clarified another matter. There was no political conspiracy in this movement. Even if there were, if this conspiracy had helped bring him and the distinguished Rajagopalachari onto the same platform, it was a good thing. He concluded by saying that there should be more such political conspiracies. Opinions may differ about whether such political conspiracies are desirable, but no one can disagree that there should be many more beautiful, sweet Tamil speeches like Sir Shanmugam's that day, and that they should be heard often.

What would it be like to listen to “music” on gramophone records worn down by long use? The song would sound like a voice rising from inside a well. If a person's ability to speak has deteriorated; or, though the ability remains, the ideas in the mind have become distorted; or, though the ideas have not deteriorated, the person is unwilling to say what is in the mind without concealment or diminution; or wishes to smile upon both parties—the quality of that speech will be like the song on an old gramophone record. Achariar's speech that day was just like that. Who was the cause? Beginning with something and ending with something else; thinking of someone and saying something or other; putting forward an idea and cutting it down with another—such was his manner of speaking. To put it in musical terms, he was out of tune that day! Not only was he out of tune; the accompaniment did not come together either. Even as he began speaking, many people cried, “Go! Go!”

History of an Ideal

“I have been saying for ten years that the Dravidar Kazhagam is not a party as it was when it bore the name Justice Party, but a movement for spreading its ideas. That is why I have been shaping the Kazhagam into an organization for propagating those ideas, so that people will regard it as a movement rather than as a party. For it is the Self-Respect Movement, which we began in 1925 and have been conducting since, that has taken over the Justice Party and is carrying on the movement's campaign; it is not in the hands of a separate body of party politicians. There may perhaps be some small alteration in the Self-Respect Movement's programmes, but in the Dravidar Kazhagam there is no change whatsoever in that movement's foundations or its principal policies. The Annadurai resolution at the Salem Conference already made this clear. Thus, the point is that the people for whom the Justice Party was founded and conducted—the Dravidian people—have accepted the Self-Respect Movement in its entirety and have largely become adherents of Self-Respect, while those who were not adherents have gradually slipped away. We may therefore say that, under the name Dravidar Kazhagam, the Self-Respect Movement is chiefly carrying on its own work of spreading the movement's ideas. That is why I repeatedly say that the Dravidar Kazhagam is a movement for propagating ideas, and not a party organization like Congress, the Communists, the Socialists, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Varnashrama Swarajya Sangam, the Brahmin Protection Association, the Liberal Association, the Muslim League, the Christian Association, the Depressed Classes, the Scheduled League, and others. To explain: this Kazhagam is an organization that, in people's intellectual life,

social life, religion and beliefs about God, works to remove the follies, obstacles to progress and deficiencies that have persisted for so long; to bring all people together as one society; and, especially, to eradicate the social degradation, religious superstition and divisions of caste that afflict us, the Dravidian people, and to free us from Aryan oppression. These are the things it urges upon the people.

The Dravidar Kazhagam has neither an imaginary doctrine that we must obtain self-rule nor the mistaken notion that we must stand for election, secure a majority and capture ‘political power.’ For what prevails in the country today is itself called self-rule. The domination of the foreign white man—the British—has gone. In the Dravidian country, Dravidian people themselves are the first minister and the second minister; indeed, the cabinet has a Dravidian majority, with ten of its thirteen ministers said to be Dravidians. In these circumstances, what further meaning can there be in obtaining self-rule? Who is to obtain it? Capture political power? Who is to capture it from whom? How can political power hereafter pass into the hands of people who are not Dravidians? Thus, to speak of obtaining self-rule or capturing political power is henceforth meaningless talk, and to pursue these things is meaningless action.

In this province, we ourselves are now going to constitute more than ninety out of every hundred voters. Most ministers and officials are going to be Dravidians. There is no reason to doubt it. But the objection that may be raised is this: will that bring us a separate Dravidian country? Will the Dravidar Kazhagam's policies and programmes be put into practice? Let us suppose that this will not happen easily. But if we—the members of the Dravidar Kazhagam—enter the legislature, capture political power or become ministers, will a Dravidian country thereby come into being? Will the Kazhagam's policies and programmes

**Victory Is in Sight**

“Our work is to remove our degradation and the obstacles to our progress, to free ourselves from Aryanism and superstition, and to become rationalists and a society with self-respect. In doing such work, dealing with ordinary people is like dealing with fire. We must win their trust. We must reform them. This requires effective propagation of our ideas. It requires cooperation. We must unite. We have to bring together the people who lie scattered and shattered, gather everyone's strength into one, and put that strength to the test. ‘We shall no longer live as Shudras’ is the declaration of our ideal, our very breath.

Victory is visible before our eyes; it is no mirage. I am certain that, if we give it thought and care, we shall assuredly attain it.

Leader: Periyar

**Written in Tears**

**Dravidian Kings**

They did not, like Dharmaraja, lose their country at gambling.

They did not, like Harishchandra, give their country away to a sage.

**Dravidian Poets**

They did not write cosmic falsehoods about Atala, Sutala and Patala.

They did not compose tales claiming that God assumed every imaginable form.

### Image 5: 005-72b3b98a70.png

They did not say that rolling at someone's feet in worship would win divine favour.

They said: one community, one God.

**The Dravidians**

They did not surrender their spirit merely to stay alive.

They did not live as mean-spirited people without resolve.

They did not take deceit and intrigue as their weapons.

They gave even their lives for a life of freedom.

**Dravidam**

It had not been enslaved under foreign rule.

It had known neither famine, disease nor anguish.

It had not stood by and tolerated the suffering of working people.

It rose through labour and won its rightful place in the world.

**Dravidam: Today**

The Dravidian kings have disappeared; the people have forgotten their character.

Valour has withered! Worthless men have gained dominion.

Dravidian art and languages command no respect.

Dravidian industry lies dormant. The Dravidian sobs.

Dravidian wealth has become locked up in temples, flagstaffs, drumming and fanfare, useless activities, and foreign lands.

The Dravidian suffers as a labourer on tea, sugarcane and rubber plantations.

In every nook and corner of the Dravidian country there are Marwari shops, Gujarati warehouses, Multani establishments and Seths' companies.

All the great businesses are in the hands of northerners.

In the factories, there are no longer British masters—there are Banias!

### Image 6: 006-51593aaf54.png

Dalmia has a whole town near Tiruchirappalli.

In the Tiruppur–Coimbatore region, all the mill kings are reduced to singing the praises of the Asher Seths.

In Madras there is Sowcarpet; there is the Bharat Bank.

The bell that rings every day belongs to a northerner, Goenka.

Today the great buildings in Madras belong to northerners!

In a Dravidam reduced to this condition, everything from clothing to nails comes from the North. To say that it comes from there is to say that enormous sums of money go from here to the North.

Dravidam has lost its prosperity; it has poured even the little wealth that remains into the North and is wasting away with the disease of poverty. For it to raise its head, for its people to enjoy a full life, for those who went abroad as labourers to return, and for the standard of living to rise, there is no programme other than ours: a Dravidian country for the Dravidians!

We ask everyone outside the Dravidar Kazhagam to view this question, which has economics at its foundation, with eyes full of love for the country, rather than through a merely partisan lens.

Look at the Marwari who has wound coloured cloth around his head in coil upon coil and sits beside heaps of goods, examining his interest accounts and stroking his belly; and look at the Tamil who pulls a heavily laden cart in place of an ox! Look at the Seths riding in procession in motorcars whose polish has not faded; and look at the Tamil, in torn clothes, with a parched head and eyes that have lost their light, waiting for a ship ticket to go abroad as a labourer.

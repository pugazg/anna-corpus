## Part 1

17 June 1943

### Dose No. 1

“Every country has stories that cannot be believed; we have such stories here too. Without recognising this, why do you analyse and expose the Puranas and other works of the ‘earthly gods’? Why do you pick out their obscenities and portray them? Who, my friend, respects the stories in them? That age has climbed the mountain and gone. What exists now is the ‘relish’ in the literature—that is what we drink and enjoy! And Kamban’s nectar grows sweeter the more one drinks it, Bharata! Try a dose yourself and you will know its worth and greatness! Feeling, enthusiasm and awakening will surge within you. ‘If one wishes to become a connoisseur of art, one must drink Kamban’s nectar!’ a sweet-spoken, learned friend advised me with affection.

My comrade told me that what the *Ramayana* contains is Kamban’s nectar, and that I ought to consume it. I administered one dose to myself. Yes—Kamban’s nectar itself! Ah, how shall I describe the delight I discovered? Even if one sang, “Sing a four-line song, Kudambai! Let us loose its arrow. At every line there is relish, Kudambai! Marvellous erotic relish,” it would not suffice to describe its character. Without discovering the experience of the connoisseurs of art, you would even lament that you had taken a flaming torch into your hand. If only you too tasted that nectar of Kamban! Very well: here is the first dose. Drink!

“Divine nectar, Rama’s nectar, the glory of chastity, the destruction of unrighteousness, the flourishing of righteousness—whoever does not lose himself in the glorious *Ramayana*, that wondrous wish-fulfilling gem of rare virtues and miraculous nectar of devotional sport which makes one merciful enough to utter, ‘I am the servant of the servants’—can such a person be human? Is he not merely a block of wood?” I have heard exponents of the Puranas say this. Yet I was bewildered, not knowing what truly made them lose themselves in it. That bewilderment vanished once I discovered Kamban’s nectar.

I have heard eminent scholars such as T. K. C., P. Sri and Sethupillai speak of Kamban’s poetic gifts. Until now, however, they never revealed the secret to me. They would say, “See how Kamban depicts Rama’s heroic speech! Observe his power to present the beauty of nature just as it is! Feel the delight his similes provide!” But they never said, “This is the true nectar of Kamban.” Lacking the great purpose that the world should enjoy the pleasure they themselves had received, they kept calling all manner of things beautiful and writing about them. They did not tell the truth. Had my friend too failed to make me understand the truth... the very thought fills me with distress. Today I shall relate only the first dose of Kamban’s nectar that he showed me. The others—later!

“You scoundrel! Is this how you stare fixedly at women? Have you no sense, watching maidens pass without so much as blinking? A woman’s waist may yield to the wind; her upper garment may shift as she walks! Can you remain there staring? Lust-crazed fellow!” Today even people possessed of the most ordinary good sense rebuke those who leer whenever they see a woman.

Even if some lecher were to tell his friend, “She was walking along, and I followed beside her, watching all the while. She had covered herself completely in beautiful clothing. Then a great wind blew, and the garment...,” the other man would say, “Enough—stop it! Someone may overhear you.” This is the natural conduct where ordinary people live.

Ayodhya is no ordinary city. It is the sacred place where Mahavishnu himself assumed his holy incarnation. Through the royal rule of the Ikshvaku line, it is a place overflowing with the eight forms of prosperity, virtue, learning, wisdom and ascetic discipline. Its people are the best of the good, and Vasishtha himself is the palace preceptor. What, then, must be the thoughts and conduct of the people of so excellent a city—especially those within the palace, members of the royal family? Must they not be paragons whom lust and anger cannot approach? What respect they would show women! Could they ever be crazed after women? Must they not be very shoots of virtue?” If I asked this, devotees of the *Ramayana* would answer, “Yes, of course! Is there any doubt? Need one even speak of the auspicious virtues of those who lived in the sacred city where Ramachandramurti took holy incarnation?” But what is the truth?

I say that nowhere else could there be people as disreputable as those of Ayodhya—people with so uncivilised a mind and conduct, savages blinded by lust. Before raging at me, connoisseurs of art should listen patiently to what I say. What would you call men who pine because the garments worn by the women accompanying them prevent their *alkul* from being seen, and who, when it does become visible, think, “I have seen it! I have seen it! I have attained delight”? Would you not denounce them as degenerates and lust-maddened men? You would say that staring fixedly at women is not human decency but folly! Yet we are told that the people of Ayodhya, where Hari incarnated—and the palace men among them at that—grew dejected because they could not see women’s hidden place, and rejoiced when it became visible.

Some will laugh and say, “A pure lie! The people of Ayodhya could not have been like that. They were noble people who regarded woman as divine.” Placing the burden upon Rama, I invite them to look at verse 56 of the Guha chapter in the Ayodhya Kanda of the *Kamba Ramayana*. Here is that verse:

> As hunters urged on either side
> the boats that sped in fitting course,
> their swaying oars flung drops of spray;
> when these struck women’s fine-spun cloth,
> the radiance of the broad *alkul*
> that rested hidden there shone forth,
> and to the mighty youths whose hearts
> had wearied, gave reviving breath—ah!

“To the mighty youths whose hearts had wearied”—that is, to the warriors who had sunk into dejection—

“Reviving breath”—relief from suffering;

“gave”—brought about.

The warriors had fallen into dejection; something brought them relief from their suffering! What was it? Listen to this disgrace!

The *alkul*—the female genitals! This, it seems, was what dispelled the sorrow of Ayodhya’s greatest heroes! Is that all? Look further at Kamban’s nectar!

What these “virtuous men” saw and delighted in was no ordinary object. The poet calls it a “broad *alkul*”! That means a large *alkul*, one possessing breadth. How is that for Kamban’s nectar? Is that all? There is more. The broad *alkul* that they saw and enjoyed was “resting concealed in fine-spun cloth”—hidden beneath a thin garment. At that time the mighty youths were weary. But what happened to banish their suffering and bring them delight? Listen to Kamban! The “radiance” of the “broad *alkul* resting in fine-spun cloth” “shone outward”: the radiance of the *alkul* appeared outside! The poet claims that the warriors who had been distressed saw the radiance of the large hidden place beneath the fine garment become visible outside, and were thereby relieved of sorrow! But by what magic did what lay hidden become visible? Listen to what the poet says.

“The boats that sped in fitting course”—the boats, meaning the large vessels, that possessed an apt and very great speed; “on either side”—on both sides; “as hunters urged”—through the hunters’ propulsion; “swaying”—moving; “oars”—by the oars; “flung”—thrown up; “drops of spray”—water droplets; “women’s”—belonging to the women; “fine-spun cloth”—in the thin garment; “resting concealed”—remaining hidden; “the radiance of the broad *alkul* shone outward”—causing the radiance of the broad hidden place to appear outside!

Read the word-by-word gloss once again, and then come to the paraphrase.

In the large boats travelling at great speed, hunters on either side drove the water back with their oars. The spray rose and soaked the fine garments of the women aboard. The radiance of the large *alkul* hidden inside those garments then became visible outside. The dejection of the warriors in the boats vanished at the sight!

How is that, comrades? What intelligence the people of Ayodhya possessed! The garments of the women travelling with them in the boat became wet, the hidden place became visible outside, and thereby their sorrow disappeared! What dignity! What wisdom! What worthy men these were!

Those travelling in the boat were Dasaratha’s royal wives and their attendants! The men were Bharata and his soldiers! This is a description of the royal family travelling by boat! And amid it, this obscene relish! Why, moreover, were they travelling? To enjoy the scenery of the forest? To hunt and prepare a feast? No, no! Was the poet portraying some occasion when dancing girls or courtesans had drunk wine and gone off in a stupor for a revel? Did the poet set out to describe mighty youths who had boarded a boat with amorous companions for rest and pleasure, and whose hearts rejoiced when a hidden place became visible? No! No! They travel sobbing. In Ayodhya the king is dead! In the wilderness Rama remains! Bharata goes with his army and his mothers to ask his elder brother to return to Ayodhya! For this purpose Guha readied the boats, and they travelled in them: the chief queens, their attendants, Bharata, his ministers and his soldiers! At just such a moment comes this description! To depict the boat’s passage, the poet describes silk garments becoming wet, the broad hidden place flashing into view, and sorrowing warriors savouring that sight. On reading this, what are we to grieve over? That people read the story of men so blinded by lust as a sacred history? That those who lived where “the Lord” was born were so uncivilised? That the poet felt compelled to describe such an obscene company? Or that even today there are people who live by uttering harsh words and insist that this manuscript filled with such obscenities must be borne and preserved?

You tell me! Would the people have failed to understand the savour of the *Ramayana* and the greatness of Rama’s incarnation if this verse had not existed—is that why Kamban fashioned it? Did he compose this poem because depicting nature was insufficient to display his poetic skill, and he felt he must also describe the radiance of a hidden place? Must there be erotic relish in the story of a god? And must it be an erotic relish writhing with obscenity of this kind? Connoisseurs of art! Honourable people possessed of poetic hearts that lose themselves in literary beauty! Mighty youths prepared to renounce everything for art! Is the nectar of Kamban supplied by this poem—a poem that explains the glory of the hidden place—something the people need? Place a hand upon your heart and answer. Erotic poetry may have a place as a pastime, to excite the nerves, for talk upon a soft bed, for the glance of a maiden’s eye, or for the melting of the heart produced by such sights. Even so, is a verse like this necessary in the sacred incarnation story of God for men of the age when “the belly sags, the hair grows pale, and the body gains its weight of flesh”? Answer, if only briefly.

I think it is the stupor produced by consuming this erotic flavour that has transformed many into mighty youths determined to defend the *Kamba Ramayana* at any cost.

### Dose No. 2

I showed how the mighty youths of Ayodhya possessed a mentality lower than that of drunken youths, and how Kamban’s nectar is nothing but erotic relish. The reason the Aryans, who once drank soma and lay about in revelry, came to dominance was that, with the aid of poets possessing this mentality, the ancient Aryans injected their poison into Tamil hearts.

# The Treasure of the Periya Puranam — consolidated translator notes

Status: working completion material, not a canonical bilingual document. The prose has been comparatively reviewed. Two printer footers remain unrecovered; their eventual verified readings and explanations must be added before canonical assembly. Detailed scan-by-scan review notes remain in the English working draft and recovery record. The term register contains 48 entries for this work.

## Translator's Notes

- **Title and dates.** The title retains the Periya Puranam as the named work and the ironic suggestion of treasure sought within it. The opening page bears 1 July 1945. Later passages cite the Indian Express of 22 August '51 (Part 3 Image 2), Dinamani of 6 October 1948 (Part 4 Image 4), and Dinachari of 25 January 1949 (Part 5 Image 2). These differing dates are preserved. They suggest collected material of different dates, but do not establish its publication history. The cited newspaper issues have not been independently verified for this translation.
- **Historical claims and narrative voices.** Statistics, scientific descriptions, newspaper anecdotes, wartime events and generalized historical arguments are translated as the source presents them, without updating or independently endorsing them. Satirical divine dialogue, quoted editorial criticism and the author's own commentary remain distinct. The dream at the end of Part 3 belongs to Viran. Quoted medical claims belong to the characters speaking them.
- **Names and mythological variants.** Printed variants—including Karuppannasami/Karuppannar/Karuppanna, Pichcha/Pichchai, Damodaram/Damodaran and Sanjna/Sanja—are retained. Puranic stories are not replaced with more familiar versions. This includes inconsistent limb counts in Part 2, the agency in the devotee's eye-plucking in Part 3, and the horse's nostrils in Part 7. The monthly lists of sages, serpents, singers and dancers follow the source's forms. Vaivachu Tamadu (வைவச்சு தமது, Part 7 Image 3) is a provisional transliteration, not an identification with another version's name.
- **Tamil and literary vocabulary.** Muttamil means the three branches of Tamil: literature, music and drama. Pann denotes musical modes; pranava the sacred syllable. The literary categories முதல் நூல், வழி நூல் and சார்வு நூல் are rendered original, derivative and ancillary works. Pasu, pati and pasam denote soul, Lord and bond. “Three impurities” retains மும்மலம் and its doctrinal and bodily resonance in the verses.
- **Social and monastic terms.** Cheris are socially segregated settlements associated with oppressed castes, not simply poor housing. Thambiran and pandara sannidhi are monastic titles. Pandarams in the Palani example are religious mendicants. Ganapati in the satire denotes a Vedic recitation scholar, not the deity. “Gods on earth” renders the ironic priestly designations பூசுரர் and பூதேவர்.
- **Units and calendar.** Yojana (distance), veli (land area), palam (weight), padi (volume) and nazhigai (time) are retained without imposed conversions. Lakh is one hundred thousand and crore ten million. Vaikasi, Adi and Karttikai are Tamil months. Dhatu remains the source's named calendrical year rather than receiving an unprinted Gregorian date. Jewellery valuations are historical source claims, not modern appraisals.
- **Ritual and occupational vocabulary.** The sixteen ritual services translates சோடசோபசாரம். Mandapam is the hall where the deity is displayed in the confinement episode. Dakshina is a ritual payment; darbha is ritual grass. Kodumpavi is the rain-seeking effigy; pongal the cooked-rice offering; inam a grant. A compounder prepares prescribed medicines. The Mani-Mantra practice in the story combines medicine with ritual healing.
- **Political expressions.** “Self-Respect” preserves the movement reference. Su-Na-Ma-Na (சூனாமானா) is its spoken-letter nickname in the satire. “Erode fever” is a political sneer, not a diagnosis. Aryanism renders ஆரியம் in the author's ideological argument. The dated designation “Red Indians” and references to the Todas preserve the author's rhetoric; they do not assert that those peoples have ceased to exist.
- **Wordplay.** In Part 1 Images 4–5, அஞ்சு supplies the five/fear reinterpretation of the “five-eyed one.” In Part 2 Image 5, சாமி shifts between “master” and “god.” “Give them a puja, boys!” (Part 3 Image 1) is a sarcastic order for a beating, clarified by the following baton action. பாமாலை/பூமாலை distinguishes verse-garlands from flower-garlands. பித்தா/பித்தம் supplies “Mad One”/“madness.” The Arutpa/Pusam/பூசுர proximity in Part 6 cannot be fully reproduced in English.
- **Part 1 anomalies.** திருத்தாவிட்டால் (Image 7) literally means “if it is not corrected,” contradicting the surrounding definition of reform; the negative is deliberately retained and marked by quotation marks. கலனாகி is interpreted as “dilapidated.” விஷ ஜுரம் is “virulent fever,” without assigning a specific modern diagnosis. The one-third food shortfall and annual 2,664-pound American food figure remain unchanged.
- **Part 3 interpretations.** சொல்லம்பரின் (Image 4) is provisionally interpreted as “of mere word-spinners.” The unusual wording around Paraman's question (Image 8) is read as his repeating “What have they given?” பிறனுடைய நலத்தால் ஏமாறும்போது (Image 9) remains “when deceived by another's goodness,” not “wickedness” or “self-interest.” Military ranks and the names Subedar Subramaniam, Cassino and Wingate are retained without historical correction. No identity is imposed on “the smiling man” or “pleasure-giving women.”
- **Part 4 uncertain forms.** Kokilattami (கோகிலாட்டமி) and Suras (சுரஸ்) follow the printed forms; their identification is not assumed. நரி பாய்வது remains “a fox leaping,” rather than the fox-to-horse story found elsewhere. “Bundles of sugarcane” retains the erotic sweetness image without adding anatomical detail. ஓட்டப்பட்ட (Image 10) is awkward in the printed syntax; “should drive these away” is a disclosed contextual interpretation of the duty being urged. Tamil remains unchanged.
- **Part 5 interpretations.** “As rarely as the fig flowers” preserves the rarity idiom. “The king” retains அரசன் in the imagined fasting speech. சுவாபம் is interpreted as “disposition/habit,” அறிவுத் தெளியும் as clarity of understanding, and இதம்புரிச்சாலைகள் as institutions of relief. The anomalous ஆழாழி கரையின்றி remains “the deep ocean ... without a shore.” Umaiyan's fort is not assigned an unverified identity. Hessonite and vermilion-red ruby render கோமேதகம் and குங்குமக் கெம்பு in the ornament catalogue.
- **Part 6 scientific and poetic wording.** ஒலி சக்தி remains “sound energy,” not radio waves or light. Piccard's age of sixty-three, proposed two-and-a-half-mile descent, the mosquito-trap dimensions and its two-hundred-dollar cost are unchanged. வான மழை remains “rain of the sky,” not “rain cloud.” கொலுமொழி is provisionally “resounding speech”; காமவவேள் is understood as the lord of desire. Printed சொல் and புனல் பாடுதல் remain “speak” and “sing in the water.” The repeated நோக்கத்தை is reflected in English.
- **Part 6 medical-dialogue uncertainties.** உடம்பா லொத்தது is provisionally interpreted as a bodily cause of illness. இரட்டை is retained as irattai, an unidentified object tied in the healing episode; it has not been replaced with இரட்சை or assigned a definite form. “Temple of the new world” preserves சாமியாலயம். பிச்சப் பிள்ளை விளையாட்டு is provisionally “foolish child's play.”
- **Part 7 astronomical wording.** நாங்கள், literally “we,” is interpreted contextually as nagas in the serpent-name list; this is a translation interpretation, not a Tamil correction. The eight arms and five named held objects are retained. பரப்பு and கனம் are rendered “extent” and “bulk”; their numerical comparisons are not silently reconciled with modern area, volume or mass values. The eight-planet list, solar figures, star counts and orbital speed remain historical source claims. “Eyes of wisdom” and “eyes of science” preserve the repeated contrast; “strange-minded adepts” retains the ironic விசித்திர சித்தர்கள்.

The Treasure of the Periya Puranam
1 July 1945

### Image 2: 002-b11b4056a8.png

The Treasure of the Periya Puranam

We hear that an angry friend has sent our articles on the Periya Puranam to the leaders of associations of sincere Saivite devotees in Tamil Nadu and to scholars learned in the three branches of Tamil! A good thing has been done. Anger of this sort brings a profit! What will those “leaders” think after reading our criticism? “Alas, Kadamba! These are no longer times when we can have people impaled!” they will lament. What else will they do? Those who know how to curse will occupy themselves with that; but they will not venture to point out a particular weakness in our argument. Having heard that our roll of criticism has been sent to many such “dear friends,” let us give them a further feast by resuming our explanation of the Periya Puranam where we left off. Those busily trying to see whether they can keep the Puranas and epics alive for at least a few more days—by claiming that everything, or the greater part of everything, that the world of youth today considers necessary can be found in them—say that the Periya Puranam contains ideas of reform. Thus, even before we begin examining whether those Puranas contain reformist ideas, one truth becomes clear. These people acknowledge that reform is necessary; they recognize that many are engaged in working for it. They know that people must take somewhat greater interest in reform than in other matters. It is also quite clear that public thinking has now reached a point where, if these people want their names inscribed on the list of the intelligent, they must show that they too are interested in reform and engaged in its work, and that they wish to say: “Poor fellows! Others wander about searching everywhere for reform, while here we have discovered it!” If some say that inoculation is the right method, and others say that it is unnecessary,

### Image 3: 003-a5dd986b72.png

then, even before we examine which of the two views is sound, one thing is established beyond disagreement: a deadly disease such as plague or cholera has spread through the town. In just the same way, today's disagreement over whether the Puranas contain reformist ideas reveals that there is one view in the country about which there is no room for disagreement: reform is necessary. This is a welcome state of affairs.

Everyone—everyone except those with the disposition of a bullock endlessly circling an oil press—agrees that reform is necessary. Among those who agree, some do not think about what to do. Some think, but grow confused without gaining clarity. Some think, discover the path, and then become frightened when they see it full of pits and jagged rocks. Some travel halfway along it, then take fright and run backward faster than they advanced. Still others resolve that, whatever the path may be like, they must follow it, and set out boldly. It is precisely those who proceed with such fearless hearts whom friends learned in refined Tamil affectionately call back: “Stop, younger brother! The sound ideas needed for reform are here.” And they show them a few volumes that have escaped the bookworms. Before we begin examining whether the books they show contain reformist ideas as they claim, what gives us pleasure is that they too welcome reform. Surely we must appreciate their enthusiasm! But there is something amusing about this. You know the story of the thief who climbed a coconut tree and, when chased away, said, “I did not go up to steal coconuts; I climbed up to get grass for my cow”? Their argument is of that very “kind.” When someone asked, “Where is there grass on a coconut tree, brother?” the thief is said to have replied, “It is because I discovered there was none that I am coming down.” Just so, if we begin to ask, “You have gone looking for reformist ideas in the Puranas; can there be any there?” they say, “I examined all these books precisely to discover and report that there are none.”

### Image 4: 004-be27bc19e1.png

The Puranas are books for the path of devotion. Whether or not they possess literary charm, one can say that by reading them one may appreciate the greatness of the “Lord,” the precious qualities of the “Mother,” and the distinction of the “devotees.” But to imagine that ideas of reform can be obtained through them is a futile thought. First, the “true devotees” must boldly acknowledge this. Instead of acknowledging it, some think that saying, “This is no mere Purana! The treasure buried within it is immense. It contains history, geography, science, philosophy, mathematics, reform, and much else,” is evidence of an inquiring mind. That is not the truth. If they did not have the temptation to think that claiming to possess many things might win over “at least a few,” and the desire to offer a long list so that people would turn to the Purana for whichever subject interested them, they would speak precisely, clearly, and within defined limits. Those who realized that intricate philosophies would not serve to simplify religious instruction and stir even the minds of ordinary people devised the Purana as a clever means of spreading whichever religious pursuit interested them. Their thinking was a feature of their times. Such teachers of the Puranas, therefore, are not to be blamed. The pious company that comes under criticism today consists of those who stubbornly carry about what those teachers did in their time believing it appropriate; those who believe that the same Puranas are sufficient to quench people's present thirst for knowledge; and those who speak beguilingly, saying that every new idea that has arisen in the world is already in the Puranas. Our anger is not directed at the mother who mingles affection with rice as she feeds her child, frightening it with, “Here comes the five-eyed one; eat up!” It is directed at the old woman who tells him to believe in the “five-eyed one” even after he has passed beyond childhood and become a young man; at those who say, “When I consider whether a woman of such advanced years would say this without actual experience, it seems to me that there may indeed be a kind of being called the five-eyed one”; and at those verbal conjurers who declare, “When our Avvaiyars speak of the five-eyed one, alas!

### Image 5: 005-8ff9f9ecbe.png

Listen, reformers who rage, ‘What foolishness is this!’ ‘Five-eyed’ does not mean five eyes. What does ‘anju-kan’ mean? An eye that makes those who see it afraid! That alone is what the mother says. May we disparage our mother's words?” It is with these people that the world of reason is angry—it condemns precisely their approach. In the Puranas, religious instruction has been arranged through entertaining stories. To seek in them the ideas needed for other fields is wasted labour—or an attempt to deceive others. How can someone leafing through the South Indian Railway guide tell us the departure times of trains for Delhi and Haridwar? Even if it is a guide to the very same route, looking through an old guide will be of no use. Why do those great intellects who unravel the intricacies of such imposing doctrines as pasu, pati and pasam struggle to understand this simple matter? They must acknowledge that the Puranas are books of religious instruction and boldly admit that this is the purpose they serve. Would any sensible physician say of his own medicine, “A powder that combats 124 diseases, from constipation to chest pain! You may take it even if you have no illness, for bodily health. And that is not all: if you use this powder to scour brass vessels, they will shine like pure gold”? Even for one and the same medicine, experienced and skilful physicians will say that people of one bodily constitution should mix it with honey, and those of another with milk. Religion is said to be the medical system that cures the affliction of birth! For that purpose there are many kinds of medicine—rasayanam, sherbet, medicinal paste, decoction, pills, and so on. Among them, the Purana is an intoxicating medicinal paste; philosophy is a decoction; stories with poetic charm are like sherbet. Is it becoming, is it reasonable, to boast, “This medicine of ours will cure every illness. It not only cures illness but dispels misfortune; and beyond that, it turns brass into gold”? Can such a thing be considered an act of sound judgment?

### Image 6: 006-946c6be650.png

Why have the poets who depicted imaginary worlds come under criticism? Because the company of artists and scholars who imagine that they are supporting those poets, instead of acknowledging their depictions as mere imaginings, insist that these are no imaginings: history shines within them, and ideas of reform blossom there. Just as the anger and petty actions of a minor official provoke people to disdain and condemn the administration itself, the conduct of these so-called “scholars” has made the poets objects of criticism! Those who do the poets the greatest harm are precisely those who pose as friends, loudly proclaiming that they inhabit the realm of poetry and are duty-bound to protect its jewels. It is generally true that pond water can be used for drinking. But does that apply to every pond? No! Some are fit only for bathing; some only for washing cattle! And that is not all. Even a pond containing drinking water becomes unfit for drinking when intense heat dries it out and leaves it covered in mud. Who would say, “It is a pond, after all; this is drinking water”? Likewise, the Puranas take many forms: pools full of mud and hollows where cattle wallow, fit only for a low level of understanding. The world longs to find spring water, while these people bathe in muddy pools, silted-up sacred tanks and hollows of astringent water. To make matters worse, they invite the young as well: “Come and bathe!” Will they come? In a well-run municipality, a resolute commissioner would order the stinking pools filled in, thinking that otherwise someone might bathe there or drink the water, fall ill and die, and that disease might consequently spread through the town itself. Similarly, if there were self-government, and it were in capable hands, they would close these obscene pits that cannot quench the thirst for knowledge, discover new springs, and establish waterworks. Those who today consider it a great service to stand guard over a pond whose stench turns the stomach

### Image 7: 007-a8deb1e0ac.png

could obtain work at those waterworks and find happiness. Why should they not prepare themselves for this? Why must they remain like the “guides” who, even today, point here and there to ruined forts, collapsed walls and silted-up moats, saying, “This is where Draupadi ground turmeric,” “This hollow was made by King Bhima's foot,” or “This is the stain of the blood that flowed from Ekalavya's thumb,” and ask for money to make a living? We grieve to think how much intelligence is wasted on this empty activity; that is why we criticize them. What else is there to it?

Why would they have inserted ideas of reform into the Puranas? These Puranas were written to introduce miracles—a cow and a tiger drinking at the same watering place; Paramasiva arriving mounted on his bull; a severed head attaching itself again—and to conclude that all these were the fruits of loving the Lord, preaching: “If you too wish to obtain such exalted rewards, the Lord's grace, keep practising devotion.” What necessity could there be for reform to enter into this? What is reform? Correcting what exists so that it becomes sound. Something is in a bad state, but is still capable of being corrected; “if it is not corrected,” the defect will disappear and it will become sound—is that not what it means? What did the devotees say was in a bad state? Did they declare the need for reforms such as these: caste distinctions run rampant in society and must be abolished; all people must be united; the distinction between rich and poor must be removed; the cheris must be improved; the cultivator must be paid in proportion to his labour? Did they argue for these things with the kings who showed them affection? Did they campaign for them? No! Their entire concern was that devotion should spread, that the Supreme Lord should be worshipped, and that people should know the greatness of divine images, sacred places and pilgrimages to holy waters. Did they concern themselves with what we today understand as “reform”? Could they have done so?

### Image 8: 008-ad63a290d9.png

Suppose a building has become dilapidated. We begin renovating it, and the official responsible draws up a plan. First he will ask how many years it has been since the building was constructed. We will say that our grandfather told us it was built before the famine of the Dhatu year. “Oh, is that so? A very old building! That is why it has become dilapidated,” he will say. “Old though the building may be, how many feet deep are its foundations?” he will ask. We will say ten or fifteen feet. If he asks how many cartloads of sand were put in, we will say forty or fifty. The question will also arise whether it was sand from the Palar or from the Vegavati. We will say it was Vegavati sand. “That sand is mixed with saline soil. That is why it has deteriorated so quickly. It must be removed and sound sand put in,” he will say. In just the same way, the building called society is dilapidated and on the verge of collapse. The base of our society is unsound. It stands on foundations made of saline soil, and so it subsides with the passage of time. Moreover, as we keep adding storey upon storey for better ventilation, it keeps collapsing like the “Prithvi Insurance” company building that collapsed in Madras. Standing on the scaffolding of the collapsed building, our great scholars try to prevent collapse by reciting the Periya Puranam and the Kamba Ramayana. Our nationalist comrades, meanwhile, are eager to prop up the collapsing building with the takli and the spinning wheel. The Vedantists believe that the Gita, the Vedas and philosophical inquiry will cope with the collapse; they agree. But the dispute is over how to remedy the subsidence, how to construct things so that they do not collapse.

Comrades! I tell you, this society is collapsing; it continues to collapse; it has been collapsing for a very long time. We live in such a society, bereft of vigour, broken into pieces and scattered. Society rocks and sways. In this condition, unless we understand ourselves, recognize our needs, and set about reshaping our society,

### Image 9: 009-55caaba0f4.png

we will be engulfed without even realizing that society is swaying. What I say is no joke. Nor is it something that has never happened, ought never to happen, or cannot happen. Where today are the “Red Indians” said to have lived in America? Where is their history? Where today are the Todas, described as the indigenous people of our country? How do they live? Think about it.

* * * *

Religion does not concern itself with life in this world. Its whole attention is on the next world. While that is its character, present-day social institutions destroy equality among the people and cause harm. The system of caste divisions, child marriage, the practice of joint-family administration, the system of charity and the like all stand as obstacles to people's social progress and the development of industry.

The caste system is a principal cause of social backwardness and industrial stagnation. There is a kind of contemptible arrogance in caste doctrine. As a result, some acquire a useless pride, while many feel diminished and lose courage. In such circumstances, therefore, labour loses its true worth. People consequently lose the motivation necessary to seek an occupation and take up work. As a result, most of the principal occupations are left to those called the lower classes. They lack sufficient education to carry on those occupations. Nor does the general public support such trades or consider them of any consequence. Thus, many handicrafts remain in the very condition they were in thousands of years ago. The same industries in other countries have flourished and developed in remarkable ways. In other countries, chemists, skilled craftsmen and experts in commerce take up and conduct industries. With modern scientific equipment and the latest technical methods, they

### Image 10: 010-5d3a94e856.png

are developing industries in astonishing ways.

Joint-family life has also been a cause of the decline of handicrafts in India. Even after marriage, many continue to live together with their parents as one household, supported by the family's common funds. Though there is some benefit in this, under present social conditions its harm has come to predominate. It damages industrial development. In joint families, those who earn have far less desire to accumulate wealth. Among the others who pass their lives dependent on them, idleness and an unfree disposition to do as others tell them grow. They do not wish to take the lead and direct anything.

The forms of charitable service carried on in religious institutions create room for idleness to grow among many people. Such charitable institutions, their noble purpose and their principle of helping others are indeed highly commendable. There is no doubt that good results can come from them, to the extent that society and the government take on the administration of such charities. But present-day charitable institutions are being put to improper uses. Able-bodied people benefit by invoking religion. Through the people's superstition and gullibility, it is the idle who prosper. Many who are actually in need have no one to look after them.

Today, industrial efficiency depends on people's mental powers. Industrial development flourishes in accordance with their proficiency, technique and innovative ingenuity. In ancient times India had a great reputation in industry. In those days, with the help of algebra, geometry, astronomy, experimental science and the like, handicrafts were in a good state in India. But the country suffered many troubles. Through hostile invasions, enemy victories, foreign rule and so forth, Indians

### Image 11: 011-65a82357b9.png

came to lose their intellectual strength and moral excellence. One might well say that, for a very long time, Indians have lacked favourable conditions in which to display their full abilities in handicrafts and benefit from them. India's industrial condition is unfit to be compared with that of any advanced country.

Food production falls short of India's requirements by one-third. It is thus clear that Indians do not get enough to eat. An average American, however, has 2,664 pounds of food available each year. Moreover, the Indian diet is very poor in nourishment. Such conditions of poverty, too, have prevented Indian handicrafts from advancing.

Like starvation, diseases such as cholera, smallpox, virulent fever and tuberculosis keep diminishing people's physical and mental strength. Thus, in an industrial world where highly knowledgeable people of robust health and strength compete fiercely, can we expect a community that lacks food to eat, is afflicted with illness and deprived of understanding, and stands guarding religions, to succeed in that field?

There are some friends whose hearts are truly pained, thinking that we are scoundrels who revile the gods, find fault with religion and condemn it. There are many others whose sole purpose is to abuse us on one pretext or another. We are not referring to those people; we are speaking to those who are genuinely distressed. The imaginary stories constructed about our gods and in the name of religion—do they not stir disgust in anyone who reads them and reflects a little? Have they not attached obscenities incompatible with reason to the Lord's name and made them pillars of religion here? As people's general knowledge grows and they acquire the ability to investigate, how can they read those stories with devotion or believe them? Meanwhile, the world advances rapidly.

# The Begging-Bowl Conquest!
Tamil title: திருவோட்டுத் திக்விஜயம்!

### Image 1

“Why, Veeran! Why are you running here so breathlessly? What news have you brought? You said a minister was coming and there would be a meeting, and that you were going to see it. Is it over? What did the minister say?” I asked.

“He said it wonderfully: that the bottle gourd has no salt. If I'd known it would be like this, I wouldn't even have gone,” said Veeran.

“Why are you speaking with such disgust? What happened there to disgust even you?”

“Why ask about that shameful business?”

“All right, tell me what happened.”

“Shall I tell you?”

“Out with it!”

“It was a fine crowd. I wondered how it had grown so large, pushed and shoved, and squeezed inside. There I saw a poor old woman showing four little children: one blind, one without a hand, one mute, and one with a swollen body. She held out the begging bowl in her hand and begged....”

“What did I ask you, and what are you telling me?”

“Wait. Show a little patience! Where do you think this happened? Right in front of our moneylender Seth's shop. The old woman's form, her crying voice, and the children's dreadful condition were unbearable. Would the Seth be moved and give her anything?”

### Image 2

“He carried on his customary help to poor people who came for money: taking and keeping whatever articles they brought. People like me, moved by the old woman's suffering, dropped a quarter-anna or half-anna into her bowl. The great landlord's motorcar came that way. The crowd blocked the road, so the car could not pass. Sir glared angrily at the driver. Someone in the crowd explained the matter. ‘Could they not find a pond or tank to fall into and die?’ he said, and left. A policeman arrived at once, barked an order, and the old woman disappeared with the children.”

“Alas, poor thing!”

“The scene still seems to unfold before my eyes.”

“Is that all? Why did you come running so urgently?”

“I somehow hardened my heart and went to the meeting. The minister came. A fine man. He raised the flag. The great landlord himself chaired the meeting, and some even praised him as compassion incarnate. The minister began: ‘Rice seems to be in danger. We could not procure paddy as expected. No one will sell at the low price. Therefore, those willing to sell will receive a bonus immediately upon measuring their paddy. To save people from starving to death, those with stocks must come forward. I address this especially to members of the legislature.’

“At once the great landlord swore before the meeting that he would supply more than five thousand sacks. Others like him beat their chests and promised to prevent famine. Then the minister left for another town.”

### Image 3

“Was that all? Why did you come running and panting over it?”

“Won't I tell you?”

“Tell me, then!”

“After seeing the meeting and hearing the minister, a doubt arose in me.”

“What doubt?”

“Though he is a minister, every day he goes from town to town, gathers crowds, speaks in every possible way about famine, and obtains paddy. That is my doubt!”

“Why should that make you doubt? People's ministers behave this way.”

“If so, I strongly support making that old woman a minister!”

“Away with you, insolent fellow! Do not speak so about great people. How would you know that the Public Safety Act exists?”

* * *

Our province has more than fifty million people who must be fed. Production here is insufficient, so we must depend on other provinces and foreign countries. Food bought abroad costs more than domestic food. Even after paying more, inadequate shipping makes bringing it here extremely difficult. Expenditure rises, yet securing the goods remains difficult. The people therefore suffer from food scarcity and must eat less than they need.

### Image 4

The province previously provided ten ounces per person, but from 13 July 1947 this was reduced to eight. Annual procurement had been estimated at 1,787,600 tons. By the end of May only 913,479 tons had been obtained, leaving 874,121 tons. Only 80 percent of that, namely 659,300 tons, was expected during the season.

On 1 June the Provincial Government held 247,216 tons of grain and expected 50,000 tons from abroad. Even maximum procurement would not exceed 699,300 tons. At ten ounces per person, the government needed 850,000 tons through 31 October 1947, plus a reserve of 250,000 tons after October's expenditure. Altogether, there was a deficit of 109,484 tons.

When Omandurar's ministry took office in March, a minister said the ration would rise to twelve ounces. It did not rise; they could not even preserve the existing amount, and reduced it by two ounces.

Why did this decline occur? Did floods reduce the harvest? Did absent monsoons destroy the crop? No. Production did not fall. Why, then, the food shortage?

The defect lies in government procurement: official negligence and a useless procurement apparatus. Profit-hungry hoarders refused to sell and hunted money through the black market. Officials saw everything yet abandoned their duty. These are not merely our charges; they are faults unanimously admitted by the ministers.

### Image 5

Omandurar's ministry replaced Prakasam's distorted procurement plan because it had failed. Entrusting procurement to rice-mill owners ended like roasting a crab and appointing a fox to guard it: food was handed to hoarders and overseers. Procurement fell further, the ration dropped to eight ounces, and ministers had to frighten people by pointing to Bengal and saying even that ration was endangered.

Ministers and every rank of official now travel town to town, baring their teeth and pleading. They sing long poems about scarcity, devotional verses about famine, venpas about Bengal's fate, and linked verses about the danger landlords face if the people perish.

Let them avert the approaching danger through persuasion or fear; we shall be glad. But whether their present method of increasing procurement is desirable deserves thought.

This scarcity will not end for at least another four or five years. It arose not from nature but because great landlords demanded higher prices. Since the Indian Government refuses their desired price, procurement is conducted by paying a bonus. Provincial autonomy free of Indian Government interference is dear to us, yet paying an enhanced price here is undesirable. A bonus resembles a bribe and creates the hope that manufacturing another crisis will yield an even larger bonus.

### Image 6

It thus encourages great landlords to hoard again.

Did not the government establish a rationing rule requiring large landholders to surrender everything beyond one year's food and seed? How far was it enforced? Those now yielding sack after sack in response to ministers' faces and sweet words had violated that very rule. Officials procuring today knew the truth earlier and failed in their duty.

Many now supplying paddy may be good, even compassion incarnate. Yet they deliver grain hoarded in violation of the rationing rule and immediately collect a larger bonus. Those who broke the government's own law, illegally concealed food essential to the people, and gambled with human lives deserve punishment. Instead, the ministers who issued the order support them. What form of government is this?

When rationing rules dance like demons against petty shopkeepers without mercy, why do they hide from landlords? Perhaps even the anti-hoarding law sees their skill and hides in fear!

To secure a larger bonus, great mirasidars will refuse to sell during harvest.

### Image 7

They will create scarcity, deprive cultivators of work, starve others, and reduce production. They will wholeheartedly enact the fraudulent theory that reduced production improves the position of what remains. To avenge themselves on cultivators demanding an essential share of the crop for their labour and life, they will leave their own land fallow. They do not care that the public suffers famine. Saying there is no benefit in abundant production without a good price, they will grow only what they personally need. The ministers' present procurement policy will lead precisely there.

What is the remedy? Where there is a will, there is a way. Rulers must consider the majority and the cultivators' welfare. If increased food is their object, temple lands and the wet and dry lands of monasteries must immediately become government farms. Modern implements and fertilizers must increase production. Land must belong to cultivators. Pattas of hereditary magnates who never even look toward their land must be confiscated. Farm lords exhausted from constantly ordering labourers about should be relieved of that mighty burden. Only then will production rise.

The mirasidar's great anxiety is that his motorcar, perfume, jewellery, silk garments, and women's mirrors may diminish. The bonus relieves that anxiety. It brings the cultivator no profit and the public only loss.

### Image 8

Paddy procurement will reportedly cost nearly thirty million rupees. Who bears it? Poor taxpayers. Must taxes paid by the poor protect hereditary classes that keep them poor forever?

Premier Omandurar has said he cannot concede that every cultivators' demand is just, though he cannot deny that improved living standards have increased their needs.

Everyone can accept that the worker should enjoy the fruit of labour. A system created in an age of ignorance, enabling a few to rise, command status, and live richly, is not policy suited to the present. Advising exploited workers to wait until they become enlightened and find liberation is deceitful. Those whose status is bound to domination will not voluntarily free those ruined and enslaved in their grip or acknowledge the justice of their demands. History shows that victims have gained freedom only by understanding their degradation, resolving to cast it off, uniting, and waging a struggle for rights.

Omandurar admits cultivators' needs have grown with their living standard, yet dares say all their demands are unjust. Why?

### Image 9

The Premier cites Pattinathar's verse that inequality is not innate, but does not explain which cultivators' demands violate justice. Perhaps he merely adapts himself to circumstances.

Cultivators ask to meet their needs by displaying their bodily strength, by turning the red blood flowing in them into water, and by showing lives worn down through labour and poverty. Is that unjust or dishonest?

What of landlords? Apart from sweating over other pursuits, have they shed one drop in a field? Have their hands touched even a splinter that harms a crop? Their hands have other work. Apart from holding the power to rule the village, what rightful claim can those ignorant of labour celebrate? Is seizing another's labour justice or a mark of ownership?

Blaming individuals is useless; the system drives them. The farm system that afflicts everyone must change, so all may live without hunger, life from another's labour may end, inequality may disappear, and Pattinathar's teaching may become reality. A minister who understands the times must perform the duty history places upon him and immediately tear up the farm system, taking Pattinathar's teaching at least as an ally. Only then will food production rise and people's needs be met.

Spain, Italy, and Japan reportedly produce respectively 7,500, 5,000, and 4,000 pounds of rice per acre, while here the yield is only 1,300 pounds.

### Image 10

If all 1.2 million acres in Thanjavur were properly cultivated, there would reportedly be no room even to speak of famine in the province. Omandurar said this at the Tiruthuraipoondi meeting.

If the aim is to reduce cultivators and ship them to tea or rubber plantations, Omandurar's method may serve. Increased production will not benefit cultivators otherwise. Only reforming the farm system, granting cultivators rights, and providing government means for higher production can increase output and enrich people's lives.

Food imports cost the Indian Government thirty crore rupees annually; Madras Province spends nine and a half crore. Omandurar said this too.

Much of the world now faces food shortage. Exporting countries have high demand, so we must pay more; we have no alternative, for they are beyond our authority. These mirasidars, however, are subject to it. Famine threatens the country; a law reportedly forbids rice hoarding; and public anger may harm them. If they still withhold paddy, trap people at death's door, defy the law, and sell only at higher prices, there is no reason for surprise or weariness when foreigners charge us more.

### Image 11

The government has not enforced rationing rules or acted against officials who failed to uphold the law. It prefers concessions and prizes for lawbreakers to win their affection. Ministers travel town to town doing what should be done from their offices. How much does this touring cost? That too will fall upon the poor.

Acting firmly against hoarders and black-market profiteers would not cost Omandurar's ministry public affection; people would support it still more.

Many say Omandurar is very good and loving, and undertakes this worthy task so people do not die in famine. We do not doubt his character. We point only to the contradiction, danger, and consequences in his method. Doubt arises when we compare measures taken against workers during the Madras mill strike in the name of law with concessions shown to those who broke procurement law. Anyone seeing the government sit upon the throne of authority against the former but behave as a mere private citizen before the latter must share Veeran's doubt.

That is why Veeran says this begging-bowl conquest must not occur, least of all to increase procurement!

14 September 1947

---NOTES---
`திருவோடு` is a mendicant's begging bowl; `திக்விஜயம்` is a triumphal conquest in all directions. Their deliberately grandiose pairing mocks ministers touring from town to town to plead with landlords.

The opening dialogue is strongly colloquial. Its idioms, including “the bottle gourd has no salt,” convey Veeran's dismissive judgment that the minister said nothing useful.

`மிராசுதார்`, `நிலச்சுவான்`, and `பண்ணைப் பிரபு` denote landed proprietors within the agrarian system criticized by the article. They are rendered contextually as mirasidar, landholder, and farm lord.

"Violence" is too small a word for that event; it deserves to be called a war of liberation. But what did the Pethick-Lawrences of that day do? They crushed the revolution. The same Bahadur Shah whom Hindus and Muslims together crowned Emperor of India in Delhi was arrested, deported, and died in Rangoon in 1862 at the age of eighty-seven. In our own time, Babu Subhas Chandra Bose shed tears over his grave!

Imperialism, which refused to yield during that great revolution, now claims that it is yielding because it fears the growth of violence. This is an attempt to make us forget both history and the British character. We refuse to fall under that delusion. The British pronounce "We are leaving" as though they were afraid. That manner of speaking is intended to implant a false idea in us. They do not say it out of fear; they merely act as though they are afraid.

What, then, is the real reason for these words? Let us set aside the argument that fear is not the cause. One naturally asks what else the cause could be. To discover it, we must again look back to the age of Bahadur Shah, the last emperor.

After the Sepoy Mutiny of 1857 was suppressed, the East India Company, which until then had administered India, presented the country as a gift to the British Crown in 1858.

"We came to gather profit through trade. In the place to which we came, trade yielded profit; but because there were ineffectual crowned rulers, witless ministers, and people with no means of achieving unity, we also acquired the opportunity to establish a government. We began to rule accordingly. But rebellion arises, and each rebellion must be suppressed.

### Image 2

"Although we are able to suppress it, we must bear the labour, expense, and burden involved. Why abandon the trouble-free accumulation of commercial profit and wander about carrying rifles? We do not need this work. Let us confine ourselves to trade. We need no crown, nor an army to defend it. We need no turmoil, for it only damages the profit we obtain from commerce. Let us therefore 'donate' this business of ruling to the government of our own country."

The East India Company of that age reached this conclusion. As a result, the British Queen became Empress of India, and British Crown rule replaced Company rule.

Today, too, the existence of a rebellious spirit in the country is presented as the reason. Those who suppressed the soldiers' war and left Bahadur Shah to die in Burma now speak as though they fear present-day violence. It is mere pretence. What is their real thought? It is the same thought the Company held then:

"The work of ruling brings difficulty, trouble, and wasted time. Wrestling with those who oppose our rule brings further hardship, expense, and nuisance. If we remain occupied with these matters, we have no time to attend to the chief, fundamental, and profit-yielding concern: trade. Why neglect profit to concern ourselves with the futile task of 'ruling a kingdom'?"

This is how today's British think. They are drawing up a profit-and-loss account. According to that calculation, when imperialism exercises governmental power in politics while also dominating the economic sphere through trade, its net profit declines. Therefore, to reduce the trouble while retaining the profit, the London government has come forward in 1948 to give the Indian realm to Indians, just as the East India Company, a commercial corporation, gave the "Indian realm" to the Queen in 1858.

#### A Peacock's Neck

The colour differences among green, red, black, and yellow are easily recognized. Not only a dealer in pigments but even an ordinary person can identify them at sight. But differences such as those between leaf-green and parrot-green, or between orange and yellow, are not so easily understood.

The differences from one Englishman to another are not like the bright and obvious difference between green and red, which even the unlettered can grasp. They are not even like the difference between orange and yellow. They are stranger still. If an example is needed, consider the colour of a peacock's neck: the longer one looks, the more colours become visible - green, blue, green mixed with blue, blue mixed with green, and a green tinged here and there with a muted yellow. The English character presents itself in just such a multitude of forms.

It is therefore difficult to discover and explain clearly how one Englishman differs from another, or what notable distinctions of character can be seen at a glance.

### Image 4

The difference between a Chinese person and an English person may appear as plainly as the difference between green and red. But differences of character among English people themselves are difficult to identify. Just as the colour on a peacock's neck cannot be described decisively as blue, green, or yellow, the English character cannot be pinned down and declared to be of one particular kind.

Does this apply only to the English and not to others? This quality exists among other peoples too, but among the English it is both much more pronounced and much more widespread. That is why, on many occasions when they seemed certain to be convicted before the tribunals of the world, the English have escaped by behaving in a manner that made the judges themselves say, "Good heavens! This man could never have done such things."

In our time we hear of naval commanders such as Mountbatten, and see people praise their courage and valour. Our hearts are startled when history tells us that, only four or five centuries ago, the English nation now distinguished by such commanders also contained men who practised piracy efficiently and skilfully. Yet it is true. Without even a clear sense of direction on the open sea, English pirates established their power with bands of ruffians, killers, and mercenaries.

That is not the only surprise. After gaining power, gathering wealth, and returning home, they received honour and were respected even by the government. Although piracy itself was wrong, their extraordinary skill and courage in practising it were praised. English historians have even written with pride that the sea routes they discovered and the countries with which they established contact were later used to earn profits in a civilized fashion.

### Image 5

Why do we say all this? To explain that the distinctive traits of the English are many and resemble a peacock's neck: one cannot state clearly that it is any single colour.

There is a profound significance within this play of colours. It is the underlying strength that enabled the British Empire to become established. Because their distinctions of character are as difficult to discern as subtle differences in colour, leaders throughout the world have often remained bewildered until the very end, unable to discover the true reason for British actions.

Are they inclined to yield, or are they obstinate? Soft-hearted, or iron-hearted? Self-interested, or moved by concern for others? If one begins asking which quality explains a particular action, no exact cause can be found and no verdict reached. One must remain bewildered or arrive at the wrong conclusion. Such is the character of their conduct.

Consider an amusing example. When the English first came to the Indian landmass for trade, there existed the Mughal state, the Bengal state, the Maratha state, the Carnatic state, the poligar domains, and many others. The English erased them so completely that their former place could no longer be seen. If we considered only this event, we would have to judge the English destroyers of the ancient monuments and institutions of India.

Yet the same English who dissolved all those governments carefully preserved ruined forts, silted moats, sprawling buildings, collapsed outer walls, gardens reduced to wasteland, damaged stone images, and many other old remains. They spent money for this purpose and even created a separate department. The men who sent the Emperor of Delhi, the ruler of Bengal, the Maratha Peshwa, the Nawab of the Carnatic, the Raja of Thanjavur, and the poligar chieftains into retirement assumed responsibility for preserving, without destruction or damage, the monuments built by those rulers' ancestors. Why? That is their distinctive character, their play of colours!

### Image 6

If we judged the English character from this event alone, we would be inclined to exclaim: "Ah, they are righteous people and protectors of antiquity! Even in a conquered country they dig up buried objects and generously enable us to recognize our own ancient greatness." We would feel moved to praise them.

They prohibited sati, but did not prohibit untouchability. They suppressed the Thuggee bands, but allowed the bands of religious brokers to flourish. They provided new medicines for disease, but allowed caste distinctions to grow. They prevented the rise of great kingdoms, yet enabled petty rulers to live in splendour. They caused many cities to rise, but left their stinking slums unreformed.

Their actions in those days appear strange and painful: like clearing moss from a pond while demolishing its steps; placing fruit in one's hand while stationing a fierce monkey opposite; fastening a shackle to the foot while refusing to treat a visible wound on the neck. Many who studied these actions were bewildered. Why did they act in this manner? Their conduct made it impossible to declare decisively whether their character was good or bad. A peacock's neck is not of one colour alone; neither is their character.

It is difficult, though not impossible, to find the true reason for their actions. The truth becomes clear if each action is studied separately, compared with the others, and carefully considered. They are people who understand circumstances and act accordingly. Their measure is not whether something is good or bad, but whether this or some other thing should be done at that moment. They do not think very deeply even about whether an action is necessary, unnecessary, welcomed, or opposed. They treat the question of what must be done now as paramount.

Their eyes and minds have remained fixed upon identifying circumstances and doing what those circumstances require. So it was, so it is, and so it will remain. The wandering Thuggee bands caused robbery, destroyed wealth, disturbed peace, obstructed trade, and reduced profit.

### Image 7

The British therefore decided that the Thuggee bands had to be suppressed at once. Could they not also have understood that, even if religious brokers did not rob like Thuggee bands, they lived by exploiting people's credulity, dissipated wealth, and ruined working people? Of course they could. Why, then, did they not intervene against the religious brokers? Because that trade did not damage their own. Religious brokers did not approach the "white master in a hat." The British therefore said, in effect, "What trouble does this cause us? Let it continue," and remained quiet.

At every moment they assessed the circumstances and acted in this fashion. Their actions were not guided by searching old texts, following maxims of instruction, or applying theories of sacred law, compassion, and justice. The single explanation visible in them is action suited to the circumstances, though this is discovered only with great difficulty. A peacock's neck: its colour cannot be made out!

This method is not confined to this country. It appears everywhere the English have established contact.

Once this truth is understood, the difference between 1858 and 1948 becomes clear. In 1858 they struck and subdued; in 1948 they say, "I am leaving." To understand these completely contradictory and astonishing actions correctly, one must grasp their underlying trait: the English act only according to the demands of the situation.

We will deceive ourselves if we interpret today's action as British cowardice, the Labour Party's liberal outlook, Attlee's affection, or Pethick-Lawrence's magnanimity. It is their accustomed and distinctive method, developed with the aid of a truth they discovered by studying why empires had perished:

### Image 8

Act according to the circumstances. That is the reason for their present course too. Many other reasons may be offered, like pointing to the crow sitting on a palm fruit that has already fallen. In Pethick-Lawrence's day we hear the declaration "We are leaving." To regard it as the product of magnanimity is useless; it conceals the full truth.

There have been many kinds of Lawrence connected with the Indian landmass. Each acted according to the conditions of his own day. The Lawrences were many, but their fundamental purpose and underlying method were not many; they were one. Today we see Pethick-Lawrence releasing India. We did not see the Lawrences who worked to bring India under British rule. We present them not as a spectacle, but to demonstrate that every Lawrence took the action suited to the circumstances of his time.

Pethick-Lawrence is the fourth prominent Lawrence in British-Indian relations, the one belonging to our own day. Before him there were John Lawrence and Henry Lawrence; earlier still came Stringer Lawrence.

Even schoolchildren learn that the British acquisition of power in India began in Clive's time. When Clive arrived in Madras as an ordinary soldier, the commander of the unit he joined was Stringer Lawrence, head of the force maintained by the Europeans who had established a trading fort in Madras. Major Stringer Lawrence was praised as a brave man and an able commander.

What were the circumstances in his day? It was the moment of entry. Courage in our country had not been extinguished; it lay like fire under ash. At the same time, the English were not alone. The French too had sniffed out profit and arrived for the hunt. Major Stringer Lawrence therefore had to undermine not only the native people's sense of their rights,

### Image 9

but also the ambitions of the competing French. He adopted measures suited to those circumstances: when factions arose, enter between them; when one claimant rose against a Nawab or Rao, send "men who shoot" to one side; after victory, remind that side of the help rendered, assert a relationship, and obtain commercial privileges; if the French joined one side, immediately cultivate the other.

These were the methods of Stringer's day: action suited to the circumstances. When a town divides in two, it is a festival for the performer. But it did not end with celebration. The performance began in the costume of a merchant and grew until it assumed the costume of a viceroy.

Major Stringer Lawrence acted according to the conditions of his time. He introduced English domination and employed methods that shattered the plans of Lally and other French commanders who sought to establish French domination. In those days there was none of the language of "our Indian friends," "an ancient and glorious nation," or "45." The questions were: Should Chanda Sahib be supported, or Muhammad Ali? Let the fellows fight and die. Ask how much money they will pay!

In Stringer's day one kind of action served that purpose: intervention in civil conflict. For that same purpose, today's Lawrence has adopted another kind of action: the act of presenting us with the feast of freedom. There is a change of method between the Lawrence who hunted and the Lawrence who celebrates kinship, but no change of purpose. Their purpose is to determine what manner of conduct will increase their profit.

The method used in Stringer's day continued to serve the British for a long time, for internal divisions here were that deep. Many conspiracies were formed to bring one another down, and the British used those occasions to strengthen their power.

### Image 10

Every petty ruler wished to become an emperor. Lacking sufficient strength of his own, he sought either British or French assistance. In a place to which they had come to trade, this "hunt" presented itself. Those who had come seeking gold found a land where gold grew. As petty states and great kingdoms alike fell, white rule sprouted.

In these circumstances the revolution erupted in 1858. There were Lawrences then too, and they likewise adopted measures suited to that situation. The question was no longer whether to support Chanda Sahib or Muhammad Ali. It was a case of "saving one's head is grace enough from God." The British had long nourished the pleasant belief: "We have an army to suppress the people, and that army is obedient to us." But in 1858 it was not the people but the army itself that began the revolution. Both Hindus and Muslims within the army revolted.

The revolution spread to many places. Senior white officers were terrified. War was everywhere. In many places Europeans faced grave danger. Many trapped within towns and distressed by attacks cried, "Why did we ever come here?" Others suffered while trying to escape.

With the revolution taking this course, the Lawrences of that day concluded that the proper action was to strike back: whether Hindu or Muslim, anyone in India who opposed British domination had to be resisted and destroyed. They acted accordingly.

During that great revolution, the two brothers Henry Lawrence and John Lawrence undertook measures of limitless severity, launched many attacks, and tore apart the Hindu-Muslim ranks that fought for freedom. Henry Lawrence died while fighting at Lucknow.

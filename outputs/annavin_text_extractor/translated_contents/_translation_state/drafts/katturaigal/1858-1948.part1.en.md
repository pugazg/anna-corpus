# 1858-1948
Tamil title: 1858-1948

## Part 1

### Image 1

#### A Strange Question!

"Does imperialism still exist, or is it dead?"

Today, it has become necessary to ask this strange question.

It is not altogether surprising that such a doubt should arise among those who were caught and crushed in imperialism's grip, then rose in resistance and continued the assault against it. But today the same doubt has arisen in imperialism itself! That is the astonishing part.

It is not merely the hunter who wonders, "Is the tiger dead, or is it hiding behind the bush?" The tiger itself has begun to wonder the same thing.

"Am I alive, or am I dead?" imperialism now asks itself!

The comedy does not end there. Four years ago imperialism roared, "How dare you tell me so boldly to leave India?" Now it announces, "I have fixed the date on which I shall leave India."

The comedy may yet assume its fullest form: imperialism may say, "I am leaving," while those injured by its rule plead, "Do not leave." The arbiters of the world may then have to intervene and say, "What a pity! When they beg you so earnestly not to go, you stubbornly insist that you must leave. That is not right. Stay there, as they wish." The situation seems to be moving in just such a direction.

### Image 2

What is the cause of all this comedy? Is there no explanation for this strange situation? There certainly is. Neither imperialism nor the front-line force opposing it is sincere at heart. Each keeps one thing within and says another aloud, changing its speech to suit the occasion and speaking one way today and another tomorrow. This is the cause of the comic and peculiar situation.

The British, who seized our land at Plassey and ruled with their flag flying until the massacres in Punjab, have declared: "In June 1948 we shall leave India. It would be well if, before then, you resolved your internal disputes yourselves. If you do not, we shall put in place whatever arrangement appears expedient to us, in whatever manner seems right to us, and then depart."

After a subjugated people have summoned revolutionary strength, displayed their courage against their rulers, and dared to establish their rights, those who exercised domination announce, "We grant you freedom; we are leaving." This great event ought to bring boundless joy. It ought to fill every home across the land with celebration: poets composing songs and maidens singing them; young men sounding the drums of victory and marching through every town; festive arches rising everywhere; people's hearts and faces blooming; delight dancing in every place. It ought to feel like a bud on the verge of withering suddenly flowering, like dimmed eyes beginning to shine again, like a waning moon suddenly becoming full. Those who lost a life of freedom are about to regain it. The British have announced, "In sixteen months, I shall leave."

Yet this precious and momentous event, the kind that cannot occur repeatedly in the life of a nation, has today become flat and flavourless. It lacks even the power to draw the public's attention and inspires neither joy nor hope.

### Image 3

It has become merely one among the thousands of reports appearing in the newspapers. It is not being treated as "news" that inspires the stirring thought, "The joyous dream is becoming reality!" But it is not merely news. It is history! It is not merely an announcement, but a pledge of new life.

When German domination ruined his country's condition, de Gaulle left France and came to Britain. Later, the Allied forces shattered German rule. France regained the freedom it had lost. What joy there was when it did! Did they not celebrate it as a festival? Here, an event that ought to be a festival has failed to create even excitement. Not only is there no happiness; it has also stirred anxiety. Why? Because each side keeps one thing within and says another aloud. Both sides are guilty of this, not merely one.

"We are leaving!" says imperialism. At the level of the words alone, there is no difficulty in understanding their meaning. It is perfectly clear. But the difficulty and doubt lie not in the words, but abundantly in the way they are uttered. The British have acquired enough skill in that marvel of intonation to bewilder the whole world. Statesmanship is their atom bomb! "We are leaving."

Try saying this sentence yourself, many times and in different ways, allowing a different feeling to sound each time.

"We are leaving" can be said fearfully.

The same words can be spoken in a way designed to inspire fear.

They can be said with contempt.

They can be said with weariness.

They can be said with determination.

They can be said in the spirit of wanting no further trouble.

### Image 4

Thus the very same sentence can be spoken in many different ways. Each manner of speaking carries a different inner meaning. Consider, for example, what "I am leaving" would imply if uttered contemptuously:

"You fool! Why do you keep shouting, 'Go, go, get out'? Is it written on my forehead that I must remain here? Have I no other place to go and no other work to do? Is the profit here so great that I must insist on staying? You make such a great fuss, shouting 'Go, go!' Very well, I shall leave; then you may live like a great king. I shall not be ruined merely because I depart from here. Do you think I will not watch from outside to see how splendidly you manage to live with your flag flying? Enough, then. Do not shout so much. Here I go."

All this meaning can be conveyed by uttering the sentence "I am leaving" in a tone of contempt.

Likewise, when the anti-imperialist forces say, "Get out," that sentence too can be uttered in many ways, each carrying a different meaning.

That is why, when imperialism says, "I am leaving," those who raised the cry "Get out" have been forced to wonder whether they should rejoice, and even if joy arises, whether they should show it openly.

Attlee's announcement could not produce even the degree of enthusiasm and happiness usually generated by the Independence Day celebrations Congress holds at public meetings every year.

The leaders gather repeatedly for talks, whisper among themselves, and say that the matter must be examined closely. They speak as though anxious that others should not overhear them.

### Image 5

Some leaders speak in precisely that manner. Some refuse to speak clearly and conceal what they know; others genuinely lack clarity themselves! Many cannot determine the tone in which British imperialism says, "We are leaving." Is it fear, an attempt to frighten, disgust, sorrow, fatigue, contempt, resolve, or a trick? Not only are they unable to identify it, but there is a further difficulty. We uttered "Get out" in one particular way and with one particular intention; imperialism now replies, "We are leaving." We cannot decide whether its departure will fulfil the intention with which we spoke.

For example, there is a profound difference between Gandhi's utterance of "Get out" and Babu Subhas Chandra Bose's. Gandhi's words carried several ideas: "Get out! It is unjust for one country to rule another. Military power cannot defeat moral power. It does you no credit to remain among people who do not want you."

Was that what the Lion of Bengal meant? No! The meaning of Subhas Babu's declaration was: "Imperialism that seizes and torments the weak! When internal hostility and division existed in my country, you established your domination through military force and statecraft. The day has come when your arrogance will be subdued. We have acquired the determination to strike your military power, and I am bringing an army to our aid. Therefore, get out!"

Thus there are many tones, and every voice carries a different meaning.

What does Birla mean when he says, "Get out"? "You fool! I have already established the rule of money here. Why should you remain? You can accomplish nothing by staying. Why labour in vain? The age of your domination has ended. Therefore, get out!" This is the meaning when Birla speaks.

### Image 6

The orthodox Sanatanists, too, impart their own meaning to the cry "Get out": "You came here posing as a most honourable man, to rule us. We imagined that you would uphold justice, propriety, and the sacred law without allowing them to be damaged. But in the end you began talking like a wretched atheist, saying that child marriage must end and caste observances must end. You are ruining the old ways and slaughtering varnashrama. May you perish! Because of you, we have lost the respect we once enjoyed. Because of you, all these fellows of the lower castes have grown bold enough to threaten us, the upper castes. Why remain here doing such things? Get out!"

Every group that said "Get out" uttered it differently and held a different intention. In the same manner, imperialism, which says "I am leaving," addresses each audience in a different tone. Hence all this confusion and all these doubts!

This is what we meant when we said that neither side was sincere at heart. Because both keep one thing within and say another aloud, the public is left struggling, unable to reach a sound conclusion.

The Conservatives ask the Labour politician and Secretary of State for India, the venerable Pethick-Lawrence: "Why have you decided to leave India and even fixed a date for doing so? Is that just? Is it proper...?"

If he were sincere at heart, what answer should he give? Only this: "Sir, our rule over India all these years was itself contrary to justice. Somehow, we profited from it until now. Today the demand for rights has arisen throughout the world. We ourselves sit in an assembly of many nations and speak of justice. Under these circumstances, it would be improper for us to desire continued domination in India. Rule ought to rest with those to whom the right belongs."

If heart and lips acted as one, would not this alone emerge as Pethick-Lawrence's answer? But what did he actually say?

### Image 7

"A disposition toward violent revolution had spread within Congress. There was also a belief that revolutionary methods must be employed to attain complete independence."

This, Pethick-Lawrence says, is the reason for announcing that they are leaving.

Reduced to fewer words and stated plainly, what does it mean?

"They have learned how to kick us; therefore we must leave." That is the substance of Pethick-Lawrence's speech.

What does this statement reveal? Does it show that this venerable gentleman acts out of respect for the consciousness of rights? No. He says that they are leaving the country because they fear violence. Whether that reason is true or false can be considered later. When someone offers such a reason, we may feel derision toward him, but surely it cannot inspire the belief that he is a noble man who respects our birthright. We can offer him not our salutation, but only our mocking laughter. This sentence shows that the Pethicks speak not because they heard Gandhi's intonation of "Get out," but because they heard Subhas Babu's.

We might even take pride in this attitude and exult: "Look! The mighty British Empire trembles before us." Yet this attitude, too, is not the embodiment of truth. Pethick-Lawrence rejects the claim that Congress adheres to non-violence and passive resistance, and proclaims to the world that Congress has acquired a disposition toward violent revolution. Gandhians cannot regard this as a favourable testimonial offered in good faith. Bose's followers may rejoice, as may anyone who holds that the method which succeeds in winning rights is the righteous method. But Pethick-Lawrence specifically identifies Congress as a violent movement. In other words, he condemns Congress.

### Image 8

Along with that condemnation, he offers the prospect of escalating violence as the reason for Britain's departure.

At least after this, does his position remain consistent? No; there is a notable change. Did the old gentleman conclude, "They have developed a violent disposition; there is no longer any work for us there, so let us go"? No. It appears that he considered, investigated, and examined whether that violence could be overcome.

"Even after considering every aspect, the advice received from responsible officials in India indicates that, after 1948, British rule cannot be conducted with sufficient efficiency on its present basis."

This too is Pethick-Lawrence speaking. It shows that he considered whether the violence could be suppressed. Why should he wish to suppress those who struggle to obtain their rights? Is it magnanimous to consider whether one has the power to suppress them? Certainly not. Would a chaste wife say, "My husband never sleeps, so I cannot go and meet the handsome lover"?

Did he stop after merely considering it? No. He consulted the officials. Only after they said it could not be done did he decide to leave. All this lies buried within his speech. Not even a fool would call any of it a noble quality capable of earning respect for him or his people. The only verdict it will receive is the marketplace remark: "They discovered it would not work, so they are making their escape."

Let that pass. Is even this argument true? A little investigation is needed here, for only by escaping our mental delusion can we perceive the truth.

### Image 9

How might such a delusion arise? If we imagine that the all-powerful British Empire has become terrified of us, we shall only be deceiving ourselves. If we hear Pethick-Lawrence say that violence has spread and senior officials have declared it impossible to suppress, and then rejoice, delusion is certain to follow.

We must ask:

1. Did violence truly occur on such a scale and in such a manner?
2. Did the British leave that violence unsuppressed?
3. Did they lack the ability to suppress it?
4. Had they never before employed violence in our land?
5. What did the British do on those earlier occasions?

These questions deserve our thought.

The whole world was in turmoil. At that moment came the August movement: moving trains were derailed, post offices burned, telegraph wires cut, and officials attacked. All the leaders were in prison. Young men were dragged as far as the gallows.

But what happened in the end?

British imperialism suppressed the August movement.

In a still more organized effort, and with outside assistance, Babu Subhas gathered an army and advanced. What happened?

British imperialism suppressed that effort too.

Britain did not hesitate to use its strength to defeat these attempts, nor did it fail to prevail.

### Image 10

If rule were truly being surrendered out of fear of violence, it should have happened in 1942. Bose's army, entering by way of Burma, should have advanced to Delhi's Red Fort with its victory drums sounding. But on that day, when war raged across the world and Britain's own homeland was in danger, imperialism suppressed the violence. Pethick-Lawrence was there then. Attlee, the leader of his party, served as Churchill's deputy. British imperialism suppressed the violence.

After the darkness over the world had lifted; after those of the August movement had consented to enter government; after Bose had disappeared; after Japan, which had aided Bose, had surrendered; and after Germany, which had thrown the world into turmoil, had submitted: under these conditions imperialism now poses as an innocent saint and claims that violence is growing and that it cannot govern efficiently by suppressing it. Only by considering deeply whether this is true or merely another instance of keeping one thing within and saying another aloud can we dispel the delusion that might naturally overcome us.

Imperialism is now striving intensely to induce precisely such a delusion in us.

Suppose a wrestler approaches with a garland, saying, "Good heavens! You are a mighty champion! How could I stand against you? Here, the victor's garland belongs to you." If a witless man delights in this and bows his head to receive it, will the garland fall upon him, or will a blow descend upon his head? One cannot say with certainty what will happen.

The claim that Britain fears violence is a drug intended to induce delusion.

In Stringer Lawrence's time the required methods were bargaining, entering into divisions, assisting one of two opposing sides, sitting as advisers in royal courts, supplying men for conflicts and receiving payment for them. In the time of Henry and John Lawrence, what was required was warfare: counterattack, attack everywhere, extinguishing the spark of revolution, and striking anyone who resisted.

Today's Pethick-Lawrence has no need of those methods. A new method is required. A smile is required, and there is a reason for the smile. The profits that had to be won through fighting have already been obtained. Profit now lies neither in fighting nor in governing. The new method required in Pethick-Lawrence's day is to hand government over to them, remain nearby and observe the difficulties that result, enter again when opportunity and invitation arise, and, even if that opportunity does not arise, celebrate a new relationship through which profit can be obtained without injury.

The actions of these four Lawrences show that the fundamental purpose is one and the same. Today's action is therefore caused neither by fear of us, nor by a sudden affection for us, nor by a sudden concern for international justice. Under present circumstances this action is required for the same purpose that required their many different actions in the past: action suited to the circumstances, but conducted in a manner that does not injure the fundamental purpose. This is their present policy.

### Image 2

When several members of the House of Lords asked probing questions, Pethick-Lawrence gave an answer that provides a striking example of British character:

"Do not press me to reveal too many details. Every word spoken here will reach India!"

What does that mean? There are many words that must not reach our ears! Secrecy is required. The explanation of that secrecy is what we have given above. Had Pethick-Lawrence spoken openly, he would have said: "This new arrangement is entirely for Britain's benefit! I am not a madman; I am a Briton. Wait and see the result of my action."

He did not say this. Yet in the House of Lords, Pethick took great pains to make all of it understood.

Whenever the question of India arose, Britain pointed to the conflicts and divisions here, saying that all these troubles had first to be resolved.

Whenever the great powers of the world clashed, one government found it necessary to condemn another's conduct. Britain attracted the greatest condemnation because it had committed the greatest number of territorial seizures. On every such occasion Britain repeated: "The disputes between Hindus and Muslims in India must first be settled. Only then can India become fit for self-government." This refrain continued for many years.

To escape it, Indian leaders argued: "Britain must announce a fixed date and declare that after it Britain will no longer remain in India but will leave. Once that announcement is made, the differences and divisions here will disappear and unity will blossom."

It took some time for this argument to gain influence in world councils. Now it has done so, and Britain has immediately begun a new song.

### Image 3

Britain now says: "Was it because you did not know that we would certainly leave by a fixed date that you could not resolve your internal disputes? Why place that blame upon us? We shall fix the date of our departure. Before then, unite magnificently among yourselves and devise a plan to govern your own country."

This is not an answer addressed to us. It is Britain's answer to the indirect hints of the great powers. Tomorrow Britain wishes to speak proudly before the tribunals of world justice: "We even fixed a date for our departure. What fault can now be found with us?"

"Once Britain leaves, how will India be defended?"

"That is India's responsibility, though we have a special concern in the matter."

"The British forces will leave too, will they not?"

"Yes, certainly."

"If an enemy country attacks India, does India possess the military strength to meet it?"

"India is an ancient and glorious subcontinent with forty crore capable people. The courage displayed by Indian soldiers during this war was extraordinary..."

"Spare us the description, sir. Have the army, navy, air force, and modern weapons of war been properly organized?"

"They can be organized."

"What is the present position?"

"Surely it is known."

"If another country attacks under these conditions?"

"There will be difficulty. Freedom can be sustained only through tireless labour."

### Image 4

"Spare us the sermon. What will actually happen?"

"If a great power were to oppose India in its present condition, there would certainly be hardship. But if India agrees to remain a member of the British Commonwealth, we shall gladly assume responsibility for India's defence."

"And if India does not join the Commonwealth?"

"If any danger befalls India, we shall watch with sympathy. Since danger to India could later trouble Asia and then grow into a world war, we, who are deeply concerned that peace and tranquillity prevail throughout the world, must observe India's future closely."

This is how prominent British figures of every party speak.

In other words, even as they say "We are leaving," they prepare the way for a new relationship. They point to the world situation and to the fact that suitable military strength has not yet been organized in the Indian subcontinent. They tell us that a new relationship must be established, and then silence the other great powers by elevating it into a lofty principle necessary for world peace.

And is that all? More than five hundred princes live in India. They range in rank from a miniature pageant of a king possessing six horses, sixty queens, forty dancing women, and eighty villages, all the way to the Nizam of Hyderabad. For a long time Britain has deliberately maintained two categories: British India and Princely India. Is that not why the office here has had to be styled Governor-General and Viceroy?

When the British now say that they are leaving, they mean British India, not Princely India. The kings of kings will not merely remain in their respective realms;

### Image 5

the British have not taken leave of them.

In Bangalore Cantonment the British may say, "We are going now," but nothing prevents them from staying in Bangalore City. They may leave Karaikudi and establish quarters in Pudukkottai; they may leave Andhra and remain in Hyderabad. They can stay and exercise influence in more than five hundred princely states across the Indian landmass. The declaration that they will leave in 1948 does not in any way restrict Britain from forming separate relations with those states.

This does not mean that a British camp will exist in every state. Some may try to remain independent; Hyderabad appears to hold such an intention. Some other states may seek relations with the democratic governments to be formed here. Many others may remain connected with Britain. In other words, the princely states possess the right to shape their future according to their own wishes. Britain will later negotiate this matter directly with those states.

It is not merely that the handsome courtiers can provide places for the British to stay. Even in the regions they specifically say they will not remain, they announce only the loss of their right to govern, not the loss of their commercial relations. Those commercial relations are far from ordinary; they yield substantial profit.

The relationship of rule damages that commercial relationship. Measures taken by the gentlemen who serve as governors and governors-general in order to govern diminish the profits of the gentlemen who arrive as company directors. That is why they say, "The work of ruling..."

### Image 6

"...must be abandoned. Let us attend to our trade; that is where the money lies." The British have reached this decision.

Napoleon once mocked the British as "a nation of shopkeepers." That mockery contains a truth. To bear the world's envy at their "ruling India, ruling a great subcontinent" on one side, while suppressing the resistance arising here with cries of "We want freedom" and "Foreign rule must end" on the other; to endure the fresh infamy created by that suppression; and amid all these difficulties still attend to commerce and gather profit: all this has raised imperialism's expenditure and reduced its income. That is why the new course begins with a smile.

Sir Stafford Cripps said this week: "The British can no longer remain in India. If we decide to remain, we shall have to stay for twenty years. We would have to govern India with more British troops and officials than at present. British soldiers would not wish to remain in India for so many years, nor would the parties in Britain consent. It is therefore best to fix a date and withdraw."

This is the profit-and-loss calculation buried in his words, the same calculation we explained earlier. Government brings expense and difficulty. If rule is abandoned and friendship celebrated, expenditure falls while income rises. We could heap up statistics to explain this further, but that is unnecessary. A few general truths will suffice.

The British gathered enormous profits from major industries such as textiles and iron. Older Congress members will remember the platform rhetoric of those days: "India's nine gems are being heaped up in Lancashire and Sheffield..."

### Image 7

"Our wealth is being plundered. The foreigner rules with his flag flying, while our countryman has been reduced to a loincloth!" So they thundered. Lancashire was the city of textile mills and Sheffield the city of ironworks. Through these two industries the British grip upon India was extremely tight.

Government was used as an instrument to preserve those two grips. Now those grips no longer exist, and the instrument that protected them is no longer required. What use is the stable after the horse has been stolen? Why keep sweeping it and locking it up?

The "fortune" once enjoyed by Lancashire has now passed to Birla and Bajaj. The money that once flowed to Sheffield now reaches Tata. What surprise is there if today's Lawrences in London think: "There is no need to retain a political grip; it brings no profit. The commercial profit now enjoyed by those northerners depends upon peace in their country. Why should we labour to preserve that peace for them?" That is why they say, "I am leaving."

The Governor and Governor-General will depart, while colonels and majors sit in splendour in the princely states, and Cottons and Patons continue gathering profit as company executives. Political imperialism is eroding with time; economic imperialism is arriving in a new form and by a new method. This is the true meaning of "We are leaving!"

Wavell goes, but Shone arrives. Mr T. A. Shone is the British High Commissioner. His arrival signals the new Indo-British relationship. That relationship will take an economic form, and commercial profit will be its purpose. Wavell must go if the hold of men like Shone is to grow stronger in India. The white man's declaration "I am leaving" is neither the victory of love nor the fruit of blood shed by heroes. It is inherited British character, an example of the fact that although there are many Lawrences, their objective is one.

### Image 8

It is a major example of British mastery in taking action suited to circumstances. Let us leave their affairs aside. What of our own people? What should be the course of the people of India? Joy or bewilderment? What state of mind should be ours?

#### The Thundustan Plan

"So many people have spoken so many times of a final struggle. But imperialism is already dead. What is the use of preparing to beat a dead snake?"

Kripalani at Patna, 17 February 1947.

"The next struggle will be a united struggle of the common people, led by peasants and workers. I believe India will attain freedom only after one final struggle."

Acharya Narendra Deva at Jharia.

"The gateway to freedom has opened. Great changes have already occurred in our political and economic spheres, and further great changes are taking shape. At such a critical moment, students must recognize their responsibilities and prepare themselves to render the service required to reconstruct society along new lines."

Maulana Azad at New Delhi, 21 February 1947.

"The British conspiracy has now been exposed. It is not yet too late for Congress to abandon the work of the Constituent Assembly and...

### Image 9

"...begin a revolutionary struggle.

"The principal duty of students is to prepare for the final struggle and unite the strength of workers and peasants."

Ruikar at Kanpur, 21 February 1947.

Kripalani says there will be no struggle, while Acharya Narendra Deva calls for forces to be assembled for the next one.

Who is right? Both are influential leaders. With such a contradiction between them, how can the public avoid confusion?

Maulana Azad says self-rule has already been obtained. The leader Ruikar says nothing of the kind has happened and that the British are attempting a new deception. Whom are we to believe?

Such difficulty, confusion, and bewilderment prevail in the country today. This is not a stage that inspires delight; it inspires alarm. Imperialism says, "I am leaving." But we do not understand what will happen here after it leaves. Does it mean dawn has broken, or will it mean that war has begun? Each leader speaks differently.

They strongly condemn disorder and violence, yet disorder has become a daily event. Fire is visible everywhere. Bodies fall. Division grows. There is no peace. In these circumstances imperialism says, "Very well, very well. Quickly reach an arrangement; we are going to leave." What kind of arrangement exists?

The League says, "We will accept no plan that could harm Pakistan."

### Image 10

We have been under the British grip for two centuries. It is now about to be withdrawn. As it withdraws, we must not cultivate futile disputes among ourselves and give the great powers an opportunity to sniff their way back. Congress has not thought: "Whether right or wrong, necessary or not, let us accept the League's demand; later, by living together and demonstrating the benefits of a single government, we shall persuade them to reunite of their own accord." Congress rejects Pakistan.

What has the obstinacy of the two parties produced? In 1946 alone, 12,400 corpses, the result of communal rioting, and enormous destruction of property.

If such a dreadful condition prevails now, when British troops can march and martial law can be proclaimed, anyone who considers what may happen after June 1948 will be terrified. Such is the condition that has arisen.

Education Minister Pir Ilahi Bakhsh says that when the British leave in 1948, Sindh should proclaim itself a separate region. A situation has arisen in which, after the British departure, the implementation of every plan will depend upon the balance of strength prevailing at that moment.

The taunt once made in jest - "Do Muslims want a separate state? Then let all Muslims living in the various Hindu-majority provinces migrate to Pakistan" - has now become something like a programme of action for the League.

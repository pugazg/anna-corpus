## English Translation

"Oh, come now, sir! Undertake any work you like, but anyone who knows better should never enter upon the business of building a house. One could conduct ten weddings without difficulty. To finish a single house, however, one dies and returns to life.

"What suffering is required to obtain even one article! How much running around, how many places to visit, how many men to plead with! You pay three times the price, endure the fellow's airs, and still every timber dealer, iron merchant and supplier makes you dance to his tune: 'Come on the ninth and we shall see. Your time seems unfavourable; I could not get it where I went. I have asked another man. Let us see. Come at noon tomorrow.'

"I had more than enough, sir. I wondered when this nuisance would end. Only now that it is finished can I breathe freely. But from tomorrow morning, men will begin arriving to collect the balances owed to this shop and that one.

"As if that were not enough, other visitors will question every piece: 'What price did you pay for this? Is it teak, redwood, cactus wood or mango?' The municipal fellow will keep sniffing around too. A great nuisance, sir, a great torment.

"People outside will merely say, 'What does Mahadeva Mudaliar lack? He has built a two-storey house.' But only I know all the hardship hidden in it."

Mahadeva Mudaliar had indeed suffered greatly to build the new house. He was therefore entitled to feel exhausted after completing it. Yet the anguish of construction was a thousand times less than the earlier anguish of desire, when the longing to build had burrowed into his mind and deprived him of sleep.

Inwardly he was delighted. The house had turned out beautifully. Just as one adds a little sourness to a dish so that it will not be excessively sweet, he mixed a little anxiety with his joy instead of enjoying it in naked form.

He made his declaration, "Do any work you like, but a man who knows better must never build a house," only after celebrating his housewarming, feeding those he wished to honour, and listening with delight as they praised the elegance of the house.

At first he had been proud. Was he not a capable man? Did he enter the enterprise without knowing that anyone who wished to build an upstairs house must walk endlessly to timber and iron shops? Not at all. He knew every hardship involved. When some pointed them out, he had argued, "Can we let that stop us? Naturally there will be difficulty. But if we keep the house as it is merely thinking of that, it will become a crumbling wall in six months."

Only after finishing the new house and rejoicing did he begin recounting his troubles. He spoke as quoted above to a close friend. Thus did Mahadevan become "Upstairs Mahadevan."

Another man spoke very differently:

"Must living in an upstairs house fill a man's head with arrogance? What magnificent palace has he built, as though no one in the town or world possessed another? Men who once lived like kings in mansions, halls and temple-like towers have, when their time came, wandered for gruel without even a rag to wear.

"This fellow behaves as though he has built three storeys. So he has built an upper floor! Even a sparrow builds its nest at the top of a tree.

"The place is no bigger than a loincloth. It has neither garden nor well. The foundations are dreadful. He threw in cactus and brushwood, covered every old article from the market with paint and polish, and displays it to the public as a new house.

"For such a thing he has borrowed a hundred here and two hundred there. Yet he roams about swollen with pride as though he built a great bungalow.

"See that green new house in the next street? Do you know whose it is? A man who sells snacks and quarter-anna coffee in the city built it. Fortune comes and goes to all kinds of men in this world. But this fellow says he has built a new upstairs house and wanders about with his head full of arrogance."

This was Irumbuliyar's furious response as soon as the milkman mentioned Mahadeva Mudaliar's house. The milkman too bore no extraordinary affection for Mahadeva. For the housewarming feast, the Mudaliar had bought six measures of milk from Milkman Pandaram, "Pandaram" being his pet name. To express his happiness over the sale, the milkman told Irumbuliyar, "Our Mahadeva Mudaliar has built an upstairs house and moved in. It is beautiful."

The pressure of work had caused Mahadevan to omit Irumbuliyar from the invitations. Irumbuliyar became angry even without cause; one can imagine his state on this occasion. He seized the subject while it was hot and delivered a whipping both to Mahadeva Mudaliar and to the new house.

Another observer lamented:

"The father-in-law lives upstairs like a bridegroom. Though prices are so high, he built such an impressive house. It looks excellent and contains fine materials. Every piece of wood is like a stone slab. He has used girders to make the hall. If one walks on the terrace for a little while in the evening, it feels as though one has gone to the beach.

"Thus the father-in-law rejoices in his upstairs house, while our Sonachalam lies here carrying nothing but the name 'Mahadeva Mudaliar's son-in-law.' Last night's rain dissolved and brought down even the back wall of his house."

Vilvanatha Sami, who had arrived before dawn for the housewarming and remained until the feast ended, made this lament. He wore no ochre robes. The reason "Sami" formed part of his name was that he had no family.

He wanted everyone to call him a good man. From dawn until dusk he therefore visited many homes, heard each household's grievances and offered consolation. At the same time, he administered appropriate quantities of the pills, powders and oils he carried with him.

Others merely said the upstairs house was beautiful. Vilvanatha Sami alone declared, "I will not call all this anything remarkable for our Mahadeva's ability. May he live like a king for a long life. What is this little house? Push it aside. Our Mahadeva will obtain far larger houses, gardens, fields and every kind of wealth."

Everyone offered many compliments to please Mahadeva Mudaliar. But who else knew how to speak like Sami? Everyone may follow one recipe for stew, curry or fried vegetables, yet does not each cook possess a special touch? Likewise, could every compliment equal Sami's? He possessed a special art of the tongue.

The only daughter of Mahadeva Mudaliar's first wife had married Sonachalam. In the third year of the marriage, a fierce quarrel arose between father-in-law and son-in-law. At the same time, the first wife died, and the hostility became complete. Mahadeva resolved not even to look upon Sonachalam's face.

Neither Sonachalam nor his wife Sokki attended the housewarming. Many knew their hearts would ache, but thought, "What can be done?" Would Vilvanatha Sami leave matters so?

After the celebration, he went directly to Sonachalam's house, sat on the street veranda and delivered words of affection. Someone who had seen him at the feast remarked, "Sami, you were there this morning!"

"Indeed, I too went and saw it. It was a grand occasion," Sami began, before administering verbal medicine to cool Sonachalam's burning heart.

The building foreman Kuttappan had his own grievance:

"I lie bedridden, yet that sinful man has not asked even once, 'Are you alive or dead?' See what times we live in!

"Everyone in town told him not to build when not a single article could be obtained. I said, 'Mudaliar, do not listen to everyone. I am here. Do not worry about materials. Wherever they may be, it is my responsibility to bring everything. Four people will say four different things. Pay them no attention and begin.'

"I gave him courage, ran about like a dog, ate neither at the proper hour nor in comfort, and wore myself to lime. I finished the house before the rains arrived. Everyone placed a finger on his nose in amazement. Only the plastering remained.

"All that roaming gave me seized limbs and fever. I lie bedridden. Yesterday was the housewarming, but not even a betel leaf or areca nut came to me. Though I suffered to finish the house, he did not say, 'Here,' and give me so much as a piece of cloth.

"See the man's character. I laboured like a bullock; he climbs upstairs and strolls about. I writhe here, and he has not looked back or asked a word.

"What is the use of blaming him alone? I argued with shopkeepers and obtained an article worth twenty rupees for ten or fifteen. Had I merely bought and resold the materials that passed from hand to hand, I could have saved fifteen hundred rupees by now.

"But I thought the man had trusted me and entered the undertaking, so I should not desire petty commissions. I worked in common honesty. Night after night I planned as though I myself would walk on that upper floor: shall we build it this way, set it this many feet, use this material?

"Now there is no one even to ask why I suffer. I have no money for medicine, so I open my heart to you. I must not speak a word to him. Let him live happily. He will live very happily indeed. Let him go. God watches everything each man does.

"Let the king remain in his upstairs house. No man who betrays me will prosper."

Kuttappan habitually began many houses, collected wages before even a quarter was complete, asked for a loan, quarrelled when it was refused, and declared, "Bring someone else and finish it. I dislike men of this kind.

"Working for misers is a great sin. Should they not think, 'This man labours so hard. Once he completes the house properly, are we not the ones who will live there comfortably? Will our family prosper if we make his stomach burn?'

"I asked only for a hundred-rupee loan. 'I shall repay it next month when I pay the brick-kiln owner. I am in difficulty now,' I pleaded. He flatly refused. Shame! I have decided it is better to beg at four houses than work for him. Let him bring anyone and finish the house however he likes."

Kuttappan had laid the foundation of Mahadevan's upstairs house, but was absent when it was completed, as usual. Even so, he believed that Mahadeva Mudaliar, who invited all manner of townspeople and fed them, ought properly to have invited him and presented him with a gift.

His belief was mistaken. But who could tell him so? Whose ears were strong enough to endure his shouting? No one contradicted him. Carpenter Thangavel attended the festival marking Mahadeva's move into the house. Kuttappan poured out his anger to him in the manner described above.

Rasayi Pillai, whose own life was barren, considered anyone who began to live with even a hint of prosperity his enemy. Finding faults, creating quarrels and sending anonymous petitions were his favourite pastimes.

He attended the housewarming. Mahadeva Mudaliar received him as Veerasami received everyone else. But the district municipal officer received betel on a silver tray, while Rasayi received his on a brass tray. The petition-writer grew angry, deciding Mahadeva had deliberately treated him with contempt.

Did he show his anger there? No. He took the betel. After returning home, he poured both anger and envy before a friend:

"I shall see how long that upstairs house stands! The fellow imagines that because he built an upper floor, all of us must rise, fold our hands and serve him. Keep this to yourself. In another week, see what becomes of that house.

"The fellow has done something believing no one in town knows. He has added three feet of municipal land. Did you know? The petition has gone to the municipality. Next week their man will arrive with a measuring rod and demand, 'Come, show me your property boundary.' Then the gentleman's story will become known.

"The front platform and the front portion of the upper floor will have to be demolished. Now he stands in the street or opposite house admiring its beauty. When the municipal man arrives, the true beauty of this upstairs house will be known. Wait a little. His death warrant has gone. So he built an upper floor, did he?"

The next-door neighbour, Patcha Mudali, was a distant relative of Mahadeva. His aim was that no person should disturb his comfort in any way. He was rather heavy-bodied and required plentiful air. It was he who selected the auspicious day for Mahadeva's housewarming. Yet could he tolerate the loss of air? He spoke, not to anyone, but to himself:

"Not a breath of air enters now. Earlier the breeze was so strong one needed a blanket. Money accumulated with this sinful neighbour, so he built an upstairs house. Once it rose, no air came to our home; it became a cave.

"He walks on his terrace while all of us roast here. I knew from the beginning that if this sinner built an upper floor, Saturn would descend upon us. It has happened exactly so."

## Part 2

Three friends walked down the street. One was a merchant, though his trade lacked prosperity. Another was an officer who collected taxes. The third worked as a broker and suffered because the proper opportunity had not yet arrived.

They saw the upstairs house and offered their opinions. No one had asked them; they volunteered the remarks themselves.

"Some blessed man! The Lord has shown him grace, and he has built a beautiful upstairs house."

"Look, man! We are officers, yet struggle because we cannot find even a rented house. See what black-marketeering achieves: a brand-new upstairs house!"

"He will build an upstairs house and even a palace. I too would build one, and so could you. We merely need some fool to fall into our hands as one fell into his. An absolute idiot entrusted him with a bale of yarn to sell. He took his proper cut from it and built the house."

Gopala Naidu, a man who had once lived prosperously and fallen, also attended the housewarming. He knew courtesy and wanted others to know that he behaved courteously.

Unlike those who merely looked up and down once, offered the conventional "The house is splendid," and left, he inspected the entire building carefully. After returning, he spoke to a friend in a sorrowful but entirely unenvious voice:

"I searched for a craftsman, brought him, roamed from shop to shop to buy redwood, sat beside him while he carved it, and made a doorway bearing the ten incarnations. Poor fellow, one must give him credit. He worked terribly hard and fashioned it skilfully because I wanted something no one else in town possessed.

"No one saw that new doorway without praising it; it was exquisite. But I am a sinner without good fortune. How could such things remain with me?

"When I borrowed on the house, did I imagine it would pass to the creditor? Does no one in this town and world borrow? I alone was the unfortunate sinner. I had to surrender the house.

"The creditor dismantled it and sold every item separately for twice its value. I do not know to whom he sold that carved redwood doorway or how many hands it passed through.

"This morning I saw it in the new upstairs house our Mahadeva Mudaliar built. He has placed some other doorway on the street side and used mine at the garden entrance.

"I went in the morning to receive the ceremonial betel. Truthfully, I even feared Mahadeva might see me. When I saw my doorway, my eyes filled. I had laboured with such love to create it and had taken such delight in looking upon it. In the end, that doorway became the back entrance of Mahadeva Mudaliar's house.

"What can one do? That was the extent of my fortune. The street doorway of my house has become the backyard doorway of Mahadeva Mudaliar's."

Kozhandai and Periyannan became close companions after meeting in prison. Once released, they conducted their trade together. What trade, you ask? Their conversation after seeing the upstairs house will tell you.

"Kozhandai, the house is new."

"Yes."

"Some newly rich fellow has built an upstairs house. What do you say? Shall we conduct our own housewarming tonight?"

"You have no sense, elder brother, none at all. He has put every little coin he possessed into building the house. Even the new lime plaster has scarcely dried. Would he have money or valuables inside now?"

"Go away. There will be rubbish in the corners, pans of lime and broken bricks scattered about."

"Come now, Kozhandai. A man who built so grand an upstairs house cannot be utterly penniless. There must be something."

"Why labour at midnight over a business that resembles horse-race gambling? Let it go, elder brother. Do not be bewitched by an upstairs house. I know many owners of such houses. It is usually empty boasting and outward show."

"Listen to me, Kozhandai. From morning onward, there must have been a tremendous commotion. Tonight every one of them will sleep as though struck down. Our work will be easy."

"Very well, elder brother. Let us go and see."

Their auspicious hour that night fell between one and two. As Kozhandai predicted, the new house contained few boxes or chests. The one or two present held door bolts, several kinds of nails, handles, rings and paint tins.

Mahadeva Mudaliar had lived in another house while supervising construction. His accumulated property therefore remained there. In the new house, valuables amounted to jewellery on the younger wife, a ring on his finger, a betel bag in the cook's lap and a little bundle tied in the cleaner's sari end. That was all. Kozhandai looked mockingly at Periyannan.

Suddenly the cleaner sneezed. Sneezing two or three more times, she said, "Curse it! The smell of lime in the new house does not suit me."

The cook woke and asked, "Cannot sleep?" Kozhandai and Periyannan hid in a corner. The two women began talking.

"Now that he has built a new upstairs house, no one can restrain her."

"Whom?"

"Her, the owl-eyed younger wife of Mudaliar."

"Was there an elder wife?"

"Yes. You are from another town and do not know. The elder one was very good, with the grace of Lakshmi. Had she walked here, she would have suited this house. While that golden-hearted woman lived, he had none of this status. After this face arrived, he built an upper floor."

"All of it is fortune."

"Not fortune at all. You would find it amusing if you knew how his money came."

"It sounds interesting. Tell me. The ways people acquire money are often strange."

"But you will never have heard anything this strange. People say some obtained wealth through Kali's grace and others through the favour of the Lord of Seven Hills. He obtained his property through a monkey."

"By Hanuman's grace?"

"Not the Hanuman in a temple. An ordinary monkey that jumps on rooftops.

"One evening around dusk, a monkey climbed onto the tiled roof and made mischief. In those days this was only a tiled house. The younger wife, the one with the elaborate coiffure, had been here about six months.

"As the monkey continued its antics, he grew furious. He was writing accounts. He called, 'Kuppi! Kuppi! Chase that curse away.' Would it leave merely because I chased it? It growled.

"He threw aside the account book and came to the doorway himself. He shouted only once to drive it away, then suddenly cried 'Ah!' in astonishment.

"'Kuppi! Kuppi! Kuppi! Is there half a coconut?' he asked. There was, and I brought it. 'Kuppi, go bolt the street door,' he ordered.

"Wondering what game this was, I went to bolt it. He stood at the doorway showing the coconut and called the monkey lovingly: 'Rama, come! Here is coconut.' Would that rogue come?

"He placed a little coconut on the roof. The monkey approached slowly and took it. He showed more. It accepted that too. Only then did I see a large gold chain with a pendant lying on the tiles."

"The monkey...?"

"Yes, the chain had been in the monkey's hand. When he went to drive it away, he saw the chain. He cleverly fed it coconut and made it drop the chain on the roof. Did that fool know the price of a sovereign? One could buy a coconut grove for the chain's value. The monkey sold it for half a coconut and ran away.

"He slowly climbed onto the roof, took the chain and returned. At once he called the younger wife, 'Venkata! Venkata!' and showed it to her joyfully. She held it, examined it, placed it around her neck, and both rejoiced.

"Only after their joy subsided did they remember I was there. 'Kuppi, do not tell anyone outside. I shall buy you a new sari,' he said. I agreed.

"They reportedly received more than a thousand rupees from that chain. Would they give me an account? He bought me one sari; its dye faded in three months. Venkata used the diamond from the pendant to make a nose stud.

"With the money the monkey gave him, he enlarged his business. Profit came, and now he has built a new upstairs house and rejoices. Because I alone knew the secret, he retained me here. I never told anyone. But her arrogant conduct today irritated me so much that in anger I revealed it."

"How astonishing! See the different ways money gathers. A monkey gave this man a chain. A monkey once bit me, and I had to go to hospital for treatment. But whose chain was it?"

"Would the monkey have removed it from its wife's neck? Some unfortunate woman must have taken it off somewhere, and the monkey carried it away."

"You villain!" Periyannan cried involuntarily.

Kozhandai immediately covered his mouth, but the sound had reached Kuppi's ears. She rose, shouting, "Who is it? Who is there?" The cook rose too.

Kozhandai struck his head. Periyannan stared helplessly. What could they do? Kuppi raised the alarm: "Thief! Thief!"

Calling "Kuppi! Kuppi!", Mahadeva Mudaliar ran in with a hand lamp. Venkata opened the street door. Many shouted that thieves had entered the upstairs house, and a few courageous men came inside.

Mahadevan opened the closed room door. In the lamplight, the two thieves became visible.

"Alas!" he cried.

Kozhandai panicked. Periyannan acted boldly. He sprang forward, dragged Mahadeva Mudaliar into the room and bolted the door from within. There were screams inside, shouting outside and noise throughout the street. Mahadevan struggled in Periyannan's grip.

Kozhandai had witnessed Periyannan's courage many times, but never anything like that night. He stood astonished.

"If you want your upstairs house to remain and your honour to survive, you must do one thing respectfully," Periyannan began ordering. Without waiting for an answer, he continued:

"You built an upstairs house, did you not? The chain the monkey brought became this house. Do you still have the pendant, or did you make it into a nose stud for your wife?"

Mahadevan trembled with fear. He thought the man who confessed everything that had happened must be a spirit or demon. He folded his hands and began pleading.

"If you hand me over now, I shall tell the whole matter in court," Periyannan threatened.

"What do you want me to do?" Mahadeva Mudaliar kept asking.

Could those outside remain idle? They split the door with a pestle. Periyannan and Kozhandai tried to bolt but were caught, beaten and dragged to the police.

"You thieving fellows chose a new upstairs house to enter, did you?" the sub-inspector scolded as he pushed them into the lock-up.

"You were bewitched by the wretched upstairs house, elder brother," said Kozhandai.

"Be quiet! Do you know which chain the monkey brought and dropped on this man's tiled roof? They convicted me for supposedly stealing a chain beside the tank. They falsely accused me of taking it. The monkey carried that very chain away and dropped it on his roof. He grandly built an upstairs house, while I ate dirt in prison!"

He had told the monkey story in court as well. The court burst into laughter. Who would believe it? Two years' imprisonment was the verdict.

"Building an upstairs house was enough for me, and so was all the suffering I endured," Mahadevan said in anguish. How could he have said otherwise?

The townspeople abandoned all their former remarks about the beautiful new house and began calling it "the house the thieves entered." It took a long time for that bad name to fade.

“மாடி வீடு” is rendered as “The Two-Storey House,” referring to a house newly built with an upper floor. The story's accusations of black-market activity, municipal encroachment, theft and wrongful conviction are translated as fictional source content and are not independently verified.

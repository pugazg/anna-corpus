முய 57-1953

## English Translation

## Kamatchi

"What is lacking for Kamatchi? A husband as sweet as sugar candy will surely be hers. Men will compete with one another for her beauty," the people of the street would say.

"Kamatchi is a golden statue! A golden statue cannot speak, but our Kamatchi will speak and dance. How fortunate the young man who wins her!" my friends would say.

"I have never seen her with a sullen face. She always appears smiling. I have never once heard her utter a harsh word. She never sits idle. Hers is a heart of gold," my relatives would praise me.

Hearing such words of praise, my parents swelled with happiness.

Then their happiness faded, and they began to sigh. Because we had no money, our relatives would not come forward to arrange a marriage. "Marry into a family of paupers?" they mocked. Six years passed after I came of age. No one showed concern. My youth and beauty became like rain poured upon a desert.

It was while I suffered in this way that I was married to a man named Sundaram from the village of Sandhapettai.

The marriage took place. But neither the neighbours who had described me as beautiful nor the relatives who had praised my virtues, then reviled us as paupers and refused to marry me, attended. They did more than stay away: they began to gossip.

"What is our caste, and what is Sundaram's caste? There is no custom of intermarriage between them and us. Even if they too are Mudaliars, what rank are they? Can our people enter into a marriage alliance with them?" They fired volleys of questions and imposed a caste ban.

That ban obstructed my brother's love. My Rathinam was forced to stand with tear-filled eyes.

## Rathinam

I suffered with tears in my eyes, fearing that my younger sister's life might be ruined and sink into degradation. When Sundaram brought her happiness, I rejoiced. I believed that I too might marry and find joy with the woman I loved.

My happiness was scorched. They had imposed a caste ban. Saying that we had married my sister into a lower caste, they issued a firm command that I must not so much as look at Anjugam, my maternal aunt's daughter whom I loved. I trembled. Anjugam suffered in anguish. We were tormented. What other way was there for those who crossed a caste restriction? This was the law created by this society.

These wretches use law and prohibition to destroy love. Yet when my sister had remained unmarried for six years after attaining womanhood, they mocked us as paupers and abused us without a trace of compassion. Those who would not even turn to look at us then now ask, "Why did you do this?" What kind of custom is this? What kind of law? And what kind of society beats the drum in support of such a law?

Ridicule and mockery arose wherever we went. Scorn and derision came at us from every direction.

There was only one conclusion. To stop the muttering of this foolish society, to close its mouth, and to open its eyes, death alone was the answer.

I reached Sandhapettai and saw Kamatchi.

She broke down and told me of her condition: her husband was a drunkard, a gambler, and a man without responsibility for his family.

I was tormented by love.

My sister was trapped by a scoundrel and being ruined.

Our family had been struck by the caste ban.

All night these things appeared before my eyes in a grotesque procession.

At dawn, I told Sundaram I would take Kamatchi back to our village, and I brought her away.

## Kamatchi

I set out happily with my brother for the village where I was born. Along the way, we spoke to one another about the anguish of our lives. Absorbed in our conversation, I did not notice which direction we were travelling. When we fell silent and I looked around, I realized that it was the road to Pulicat.

"Aren't we going to our village? Why did you turn onto this road?" I asked my brother, wondering whether he too had failed to notice the path, just as I had.

His eyes filled with tears. He gave no answer.

"What is it, brother? Why are you distressed? Aren't you coming to the village? Where are you going?" I asked again.

His eyes remained tearful. He did not reply.

## Rathinam

"Where are you taking me, brother?" my sister asks. How can I tell her that I am taking her to a land of death without caste conflict or social conflict? I had wanted only for her to live. How could I say that I was now leading her toward death?

Yet what else could I tell her? Her relatives reviled her, and her husband wandered without caring for his family. What happiness could remain in my sister's life? And now that the door had been shut upon my love, what happiness remained for me? Is death not better than a life without joy?

You may ask whether dying is not a crime. Think and tell me: whose crime is it? Is it the crime of the relatives who mocked us for being poor and refused to marry my sister? Is it the crime of the society that planted caste division in those relatives' minds? Whose crime brought our lives to this end? When will this crime leave the country? Will it disappear at least after people like us have died?

Somehow, I told my sister what I intended.

I expected her to refuse. She accepted my words gladly.

"I have brought disgrace upon the family. There is no happiness in my life. There is no place for your love. Brother, death is better than living this life," she said.

We reached Pulicat. It was nine at night.

We went to the sacred temple of the Lord who protects and saves all things. With anguish in our hearts, we worshipped him.

"Lord, did you not create everyone? Why, then, did you create some high and others low? Why did you make some poor and others rich? Why did you not create a proper way for everyone to live in happiness?" we cried and asked.

Whether he heard us or not did not concern us. The midnight worship of the Lord who created everyone came to an end. Everyone left the temple. We alone remained hidden inside.

At midnight, I pushed my sister, whom others believed had disgraced our family, into the temple tank, the Lord's sacred tank. I too leapt into it.

Conspiratorial society that speaks of caste, you are happy now, are you not?

And Lord, who created and protects even such degraded people, are you too pleased by the death of us both?

5 July 1953

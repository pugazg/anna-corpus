## English Translation

This is not the story of a human being. It is the story of a god, and of a god's wife too. By a story of gods, I do not mean a legend that we have never seen and have only heard. This is a story that actually happened.

Rise and fall are natural in human life. People say that pleasure and pain alternate like day and night. A rubbish heap rises; a tower falls. Kuchela becomes Kubera, and Kubera becomes Kuchela. They call this natural and say it is the Lord's divine sport. But this story concerns the fate of the gods themselves!

"Kathayi! Come, let us go directly to the Sun God and tell him our grievance," said Sadaiyappar.

"Come, let us go. But where will the Sun God find time to hear our grievance? He has to keep running without rest. He will have no leisure to stop for a moment, ask what has happened and offer us consolation," said Kathayi.

"That is also true. Nevertheless, the mind will find peace only if we tell someone of our distress," said Sadaiyappar. "Then let us go and see," Kathayi agreed, and the two set out.

"Alas! You sinners! What cruelty is this? Stop it! My whole face is bleeding and my back is swollen. Thousands of you have gathered to torment me with stones. Lord! Will you not question them? Must even this happen?" The Sun God was weeping. Tears streamed from his eyes. Only after that water fell below did the thousands who had stoned him put down their stones and sing, "Raghupati Raghava Raja Ram, Patita Pavana Sita Ram," dancing in delight.

Kathayi and Sadaiyappar, who had come to relate their own grievance, were startled to see the Sun God standing in tears, his face, back and body covered in wounds from the stones. Without mentioning their own matter, they first began to inquire about his sorrow.

"Wait a little. Let me see whether those Saturn-stricken fellows have gone; then we shall talk," said the Sun God, looking down. Seeing the crowds depart in groups, full of jubilation, he sighed, "The afflictions have gone," and began to feel the welts on his body.

"Lord! What is this condition? Why are you crying? How did you receive these wounds?" Kathayi asked.

"I cried over my fate, Kathayi. Those sinners stopped beating me only after I wept. What heavy blows! What a beating! An evil time has come upon me," he said in anguish.

"What exactly happened?" asked Sadaiyappar.

"What happened? Why make me recall that torment? People say that even after visiting Kashi, that supremely sacred place, one's karma may not depart. In my case this karma came precisely because I visited Kashi. Yet how could I fail to come? Kashi, Rameswaram, Rangoon, Singapore, London, Paris, Berlin, Baghdad, Moscow, Milan, Ankara, the Azores, New York, Ottawa and New Zealand: what place have I not visited?

"I see forests and hills, and the homes in which lovers caress one another. I cross the Sahara and enter regions of dense fog. I see emperors awaiting my arrival. I make no distinctions and allot no special measure to anyone; I treat all whom I encounter without the least partiality. I have never failed in my duty.

"When I see a poor man carrying a burden until his back bends, I sometimes feel pity that his feet and head will burn. But what can I do? That is his fate, and it is my fate to scorch him. I harden my heart and perform my duty. I have heard thousands of poets speak of women with the gait of swans. Yet it pains me when I see those women walking until their feet blister, or their infants suffering. Still, what can I do? I am not here to show favour or indulgence. I have never abandoned my duty out of fear. And now this is the fate that has befallen me," the grieving Sun God said.

Kathayi shed tears. "Lord! Has even this fate befallen you?" she asked compassionately.

"Yes, Kathayi. Let my story rest. You must be hungry; perhaps you should eat something," said the Sun God.

As a matter of convention, Sadaiyappar replied, "We are not hungry just now. Tell us your story first."

"Of course, why would you be hungry? Legions of devotees offer goats, chickens, cooked rice, milk, fruit and delicacies. You must be sitting there sated with food," said the Sun God. He did not know that Kathayi and Sadaiyappar had wandered for six days without food.

"Can anyone count those who have immersed themselves in the Ganges here in Kashi, risen, folded their hands and worshipped me? Crores have praised and adored my crimson body. 'The visible deity! Lamp of the world! Embodiment of compassion who dispels pitch darkness!' How many have sung such poems? And this is that very Ganges!

"In this sacred place, where they worshipped me in all those ways, those same legions of devotees cursed me and beat me as children gather around a madman and pelt him with stones. You cannot imagine the size of the crowd or the fury with which they struck. Thousands upon thousands scooped up stones and hurled them. 'Sinner! Villain! Vengeful wretch!' they shouted," said the Sun God.

"Why?" asked Sadaiyappar.

"They ask why I keep scorching them. They rage and beat me," said the radiant one.

"But scorching is the sun's duty," said Kathayi.

"Yes. That is both my duty and my fate," said the Sun.

"Then why should the people be angry?" she asked.

"People! You ask about people, Kathayi. Do they need a reason for their anger? For what do they ever seek a reason?" Sadaiyappar said with disgust.

"Yes. What do those creatures know?" Kathayi said sadly. Then she asked, "Why are the people who stoned you like madmen now dancing so joyfully?"

"That is what I intend to ask the Lord. Should I not ask whether such injustice can be allowed? Wait, Kathayi! Those people over there are saying something. Let us listen," the Sun replied angrily. All three listened closely.

"Protector of the helpless! Friend in danger! We, your servants, offer you a thousand crore salutations for coming forward to end our distress. The scorching sun dried every body of water in the town and burned the crops. Our stomachs began to burn. We did no wrong to the Sun God. Whenever we see him, we cry, 'Lord!' and praise him. After bathing in the sacred Ganges, we fold our hands and worship him as the deity visible to our eyes. We offer him milk-pongal.

"He rises smiling, accepts our worship, glares at us furiously all day and, before we go to bed, disappears with a mocking smile. Every day brings the same fate. What sharpness, what ferocity! Is this the reward he gives after accepting our worship? Would we not become angry? There is no rain. People have no food, cattle no grass, and there are no flowers for your worship. There is no happiness in our hearts.

"We pleaded and praised him endlessly, but the Sun showed no compassion. At last we remembered the saying that an unbeaten cow will not obey. We took up stones and released them in volleys. We thrashed him thoroughly. Only then did rain fall. Your compassion is compassion indeed!

"At first we feared that you might be angry because we had tormented the sun. Now we understand that you did not consider our stoning him a wrong. Treasure-house of mercy, we offer our thanks for the rain you sent." Thus the residents of Kashi offered worship at the Vishwanath temple and praised the Lord.

"Did you hear that, Kathayi? The tears I shed became rain for them, but they worship the Lord for it. I receive the stones; he receives the worship. I suffer; he enjoys the reward. Is this too my fate? This is my trouble. If only I could obtain a peaceful life like yours, I would even resign this post," lamented the Sun God.

Kathayi, who had restrained herself until then, could endure no more. Tears filled her eyes. "You will understand only after hearing our story. That is what brought us here, but on arriving we heard of your suffering," she sobbed.

"What misfortune befell you?" the Sun God asked with concern, and Kathayi related her story.

"Perhaps you have forgotten. It has been fifteen years since we took up our post in Manjapattinam. Did we go there on our own? No! How earnestly they pleaded, danced, celebrated and worshipped us! My heart trembles when I remember it.

"Manjapattinam is neither an ancient and renowned city like Kashi nor a centre of modern civilization like Calcutta or Bombay. It is a remote village in Paramakudi taluk of Ramanathapuram district. We had no wish whatever to settle there. Though city preachers repeated every possible praise of village life, we felt no desire to go to Manjapattinam. But the petition its people sent us was deeply moving.

"I do not know why they conceived the desire to invite us. What did that village lack without us? The people laboured and somehow maintained their families. I do not know why affection for us suddenly arose among them.

"Sadaiyappar said that some Mahabharata preacher, after completing recitations for his livelihood, gave advice to those who had paid to hear him. He claimed that building a temple for Kathayi would yield immediate rewards in the Kali age. Believing him, the people must have hoped that if they built a temple and installed Sadaiyappar and me, every kind of blessing would arrive: parched fields would bear dense ears of grain, the river would overflow its banks, vegetables would pile up in gardens, children would be born into their families, and prosperity would reign.

"That puranic preacher would have described it all until their mouths watered. Why should he not lie? The puranas he had studied were overflowing with that commodity. His own younger brother could become the priest, and the Mahabharata discourse could be held in the temple every year.

"The man who agreed with every word by saying, 'Yes, elder brother,' hoped that if a temple and tank were built and chariot festivals conducted, some of the charitable collections might fall to him. Who dislikes licking honey from the vessel?

"The poor of the village agreed that it had to be done. The preacher had lied and frightened them that otherwise Kathayi would pluck out their eyes. They also believed that giving a quarter-rupee for Kathayi's expenses would bring lifelong good fortune. What was there that they did not believe?

"The wealthy thought, 'It is good if these fellows occupy themselves with building a temple.' Once the poor concentrated on temple work, they would have no time to examine their own condition and would cause the rich no trouble. By contributing a little, the wealthy would earn names such as 'Lord of Charity' and 'King Karna,' while retaining other ways of making money. So they too agreed. Many others said it should be built because, even if nothing else came of it, the construction would at least provide work. And they built it!

"You should have seen everything done to bring us to the new temple. The whole village was exhilarated. Every house bore lamps, every street festoons, every corner a flower pavilion, and every junction resounded with drums. Maidens wore flowers in their hair and men new dhotis at their waists. The beat of the udukkai and cries of 'Ongali!' filled the village. Chosen priests came to conduct ablutions and chant spells, many assistants attended them, varied instruments played, and fireworks shook the sky. That day Manjapattinam was truly the city of Indra.

"Words cannot describe the ceremony with which we were welcomed, or the beauty of the temple and tower. A fine image was made for me to inhabit and grant darshan, its sculptor displaying all his skill. The image prepared as Sadaiyappar's abode stood eight feet tall. I received silk garments and a coral necklace; Sadaiyappar too received special adornment.

"That is to say, after the people's worship moved us to enter and reside in those images, they decorated them richly, lit lamps, fell down in worship, offered many kinds of food and carried milk kavadi, fish kavadi and flower kavadi. People from the surrounding villages came to pay homage. This was in 1926.

"In 1927 the people of Manjapattinam retained their former devotion. Worship continued as usual, and they never failed to petition us. The priest sang himself hoarse about our heroic glory. His lies became tiresome even to our ears.

"He would suddenly declare, 'At midnight Kathayi appeared in my dream and said that if three black goats are sacrificed and a black stone installed and worshipped, the village's sugarcane crop will flourish.' The people would reply, 'Why not, priest? If we do not give it to the Mother, whom else will we give it to? Everything is her alms. The gruel we drink and the wealth we possess are her gifts.'

"The priest's family received goat meat and rice. Alamelu received a black sari, and the priest obtained opium, ganja and liquor. What could we do about it?

"For two or three years we lived comfortably in this manner. Early in 1930 our bad time began. None of the things the priest had promised when we settled in Manjapattinam came to pass. Could events occur merely according to the people's prayers? Were we Brahma, able to make everything happen? When matters failed to follow their wishes, the people began behaving with a touch of resentment.

## Part 2

"There was no harvest that year and no rain. What could we do? Were we Indra? The priest offered the discouraged people a stratagem: 'The whole village must cook pongal for Kathayi. If you do, rain will pour down by the potful.' They held the village pongal. The priest's household prospered a little, while the liquor in his stomach overflowed much more abundantly.

"The next day there was good rain. 'Have you now seen Kathayi's greatness?' the people exclaimed in praise. Our position grew a little easier. But the accursed rain would not stop. It rained all day. Rivers and tanks filled; fields and forests flooded. It rained the following day too, with a gale that broke trees. Houses and courtyards became pools, and still the rain continued.

"The people who had rejoiced at its arrival now complained, 'When it is dry, it scorches; when it rains, it pours. What is this, Mother Kathayi?' The rain caused loss to the village and anguish to us.

"The following year there was no rain. No bloom remained on the people's faces and no coolness in our lives. They began questioning the priest: 'Priest! We have never failed to sacrifice a ram to Kathayi, and you never stop beating the udukkai. Yet we see no result. The village declines day by day. Has the Mother's heart not softened?'

"'Do not be impatient, brothers. All this is the Mother's test. Just wait until next year,' the priest would console them. But when no one was present, he would mutter softly, 'Kathayi's plight and ours are both desperate.'

"The priest predicted prosperity the next year, but something else arrived: cholera. At its onset worship increased for a while. When corpses began dropping even in the homes that had performed worship, the worship stopped. Everywhere the priest went he encountered opposition.

"'What wrong have we committed? Such misery has never before visited our village,' people asked. He said the Mother's anger would cool if they bathed her image in tender-coconut water. They did so half-heartedly, but the cholera did not cease. Every neem tree in the village was stripped to bare branches. Many families suffered pitiful deaths.

"In one poor family, a woman had already lost her mother, father and husband. Then her five-year-old daughter contracted cholera too. The woman circled the temple and pleaded endlessly. She gave the child water from the ritual ablution. She wandered and trembled. 'Kathayi! Mother! Open your eyes and look!' she cried. Her child died nonetheless.

"How her heart must have broken! 'May lightning strike Kathayi's temple! My family is gone. I worshipped you day and night, yet I have become a destitute sinner. Now I have lost my precious child too,' she cried.

"The others scolded her: 'Shame! Do not blame Kathayi.' But inwardly they thought, 'Yes, poor woman. She pleaded so much, yet Kathayi showed no compassion. Is her heart made of stone? All our worship has gone to waste.'

"Such doubts began to grow, and fear arose in us. Worship diminished. A few people visited the temple merely for form's sake, then left. The old affection had vanished. Several years passed this way. We lived like an impoverished household, consoling ourselves that whatever little came our way would have to suffice. The priest too grew lean.

"Last year Saturn truly caught us. The young men of Manjapattinam forgot to read tales such as the Mahakali Purana and the Birth of Maheswaran. Did they at least remain quiet? No. They began ridiculing those puranas. The village elders tried to restrain them but could not. 'It is the nature of the times,' they said.

"A young man would begin: 'Grandfather, why do we alone have thousands of gods, while other countries have only one? They live well, but we suffer. Kathayi, Mariayi and Mookayi stand at every corner. We worship them ceaselessly but see no benefit. Our troubles never diminish.'

"The old man would answer craftily, 'Does not everything happen according to what is written on each person's head? If one worships Kathayi and cooks a quarter measure of rice, will what fate has written be erased that very day?'

"'If everything happens according to fate, why do we need Kathayi and Karuppan in between, Grandfather? We may as well sit still and let whatever must happen occur. Why this wasteful expense?' the youth would ask.

"'Go away! What a wicked question! Should we not worship the Lord who created us?' the grandfather would say.

"'Certainly, Grandfather! But if we put seed into the ground, it must produce a crop. If we worship, it must bear fruit. Yet there is no harvest. It has been fifteen years since Kathayi's temple was built. Did famine stop coming? Did cholera disappear? Have our hardships ended?' the youth would ask.

"'Not one of them has gone. Trouble never ceases,' someone would admit.

"The young man would leave thinking he had won the argument. But whenever he met another old man, that man sang the same old refrain: 'Are our troubles so great? Does not everything happen according to destiny?' Another elder would begin, 'Why, such things have happened in every age. Even the mighty Lord Rama...' The two would pass the time recounting how Rama suffered separation from Sita, Shiva begged for alms and Brahma lost a head.

"We reassured ourselves that we could somehow survive as long as these old men retained the power to speak. But the young men did not relent. They spoke without pause: a Self-Respect association, a Tamil gathering, the Kudi Arasu newspaper, and all manner of agitation. Their campaign grew stronger.

"There was no prosperity in 1940, and the birth of 1941 brought no benefit. The anger planted by the young men seized the elders too. What shall I say? The very people who once worshipped us began to revile us.

"'Not worth half a paisa,' said one.

"'A sheer waste,' another went a step further.

"'Useless expense and trouble,' said a third.

"'Kathayi and Mookayi: as those boys say, all these are fabricated stories,' said a middle-aged man.

"'What difference does it make whether that temple exists or disappears?' another asked bitterly.

"'Do you know the compound wall is collapsing?' someone said.

"'Let it collapse. Our village has been falling into ruin ever since this Kathayi temple was built,' replied another.

"An old man blamed the hour rather than the temple: 'It was not built at an auspicious time. All this trouble came from that.'

"'If the time is wrong, Grandfather, why keep that temple and weep? Why not demolish it?' a youth asked.

"'Why not demolish it?' many voices answered.

"What could I say? They took up spades and crowbars and came to the temple. They demolished it with as much fury as the devotion with which they had built it. The wall fell, the tower was broken, and the enclosure was split apart. A single uproar arose: 'Strike! Split! Smash!'

"Some women said, 'It deserves to be demolished. That woman cursed it before she left: "May lightning strike Kathayi's temple." She was a chaste woman, after all.'

"Some feared what misfortune might follow the temple's destruction. We heard that the priest had fled the village.

"The demolition force finally reached the sanctum where we lived. Before I could say, 'Sadaiyappar!' he ordered, 'Leave!' We sprang from the images and stood in the air, invisible to their eyes.

"'Look at those eyes, like a savage owl's! Look at that nose, three cubits long! Look at that hand, like a plough! Look at the moustache! One blow from that waist would split you in half!' The people mocked us as they broke the images.

"The mouths that had once praised me as, 'She who created a thousand eyes for us! Beloved of the skull-bearing Lord! Sister of the dark-hued one! Flower-eyed Mahakali!' reviled me that day. The images were reduced to dust.

"We decided it was dangerous to remain there. These people had summoned us, lived under our protection for fifteen years, then smashed our temple, humiliated us and forced us to flee the village. We resolved to petition the Lord to punish them appropriately. We thought we would first tell you. But here we find you in this condition." Thus Kathayi completed her history.

The Sun said, "Very well. Come, let us go and appeal to the Lord." At once a voice arose from the heavens:

"Sun, Kathayi and Sadaiyappa, listen! You think that you, the people who once worshipped you and later tormented you, and I, to whom you have come expecting justice after I hear this history, are all separate. You suffer under that illusion. We are not separate. Everything is I!

"I am the grieving Sun. I am also the devotees of Kashi who stoned him. I am the people of Manjapattinam who built the temple, and those who demolished it. I am Kathayi, and I am Sadaiyappan. Fools, you complain as though some other being has harmed you! I am not separate from you. Everything is I. Have you forgotten that not even an atom moves without him?"

The Sun went away to scorch the earth. Kathayi and Sadaiyappar waited, listening for the beat of an udukkai that would summon them somewhere. The residents of Kashi immersed themselves in the Ganges, rose and worshipped the Sun. The people of Manjapattinam prayed, "By the Lord's mercy, the Saturn that afflicted us has now departed." A religious advocate pondered what stratagem might induce people to build another temple.

You may ask, "What sort of story is this? Could such events ever occur? Pure nonsense! Kathayi visiting the Sun? The stoned Sun crying out? A temple being demolished? To whom are you telling such tall tales?"

Leave aside whether Kathayi spoke or the Sun wept. The heroes of Kashi who stoned the Sun to make rain fall still exist. This report appeared in *Dinamani*:

### Stones for the Sun!

**The handiwork of Kashi's residents**  
**Kashi, June 28, 1947**

Unable to bear the heat because rain had not fallen, thousands of people became enraged. For the past few days they had therefore been throwing stones at the Sun.

Today, to the delight of those people, dark clouds gathered in the blue sky. Rain fell for half an hour in the morning and half an hour in the evening. Kashi is consequently cooler now, its heat having subsided.

And here is the report about the demolition of Kathayi's temple, published in *Swadesamitran*:

### Images on the Bank of the Vaigai

**Incident at Manjapattinam**  
**Ramanathapuram, June 18, 1947**

In Manjapattinam of Paramakudi taluk, the villagers performed a kumbabhishekam for the Sri Sadaiyappa Swami temple in the month of Vaikasi in the past Pramoduta year, at a cost of approximately 2,500 rupees. The villagers believed that they had been declining from that year onward.

Consequently becoming hostile toward the temple, on Sunday they damaged it, removed the principal image of Sri Sadaiyappa Swami, which stood eight feet high, the image of Sri Kathayi Amman, approximately five feet high, and several other images, and left them on the bank of the Vaigai River. There is now no worship at the temple.

On inquiry in the village, it is said that they all acted unanimously in this matter.

What do you say now? In a country containing so many such Brihaspatis, is the claim that Kathayi spoke the truly astonishing part?

On what day will this folly end? When will such obscene ideas depart from the minds of our people?

Is silence the only answer? Are there no courageous people who will offer another answer? Are there no heroes who will change this dreadful condition, make knowledge shine and inquiry flourish, and turn the people into rationalists?

9 November 1947

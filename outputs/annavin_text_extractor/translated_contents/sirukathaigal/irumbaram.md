## English Translation

"The welcome must be magnificent. That great hero disregarded the dangers of the sea, journeyed to places unseen by human eyes, and sought out fame and wealth for us. We must give him a grand welcome that will gladden his heart."

"I have been thinking the same thing. He is a noble man whose name will enter world history! The townspeople dance with joy merely on hearing his name. Kings grow envious when they hear of his prowess. He has won a glory rarely attained. It is our duty to honour and praise such a great benefactor. Our welcome must be unlike anything ever accorded to anyone in this realm. He is no ordinary man, and his victory is no rainbow that fades away; it is a victory that will endure for all time."

"Indeed! He left his town and all his kith and kin, sailed the surging sea, and endured wind, whirlpools, and every danger that arises upon the ocean. He travelled far and discovered many new territories that had never before met anyone's eye or entered anyone's thought. He fought and defeated the native inhabitants there, raised our country's flag, and made those lands new possessions of our kingdom. What courage, endurance, labour, and power to withstand danger such a feat must have demanded! He is a peerless hero."

"A man of ideals! His one longing is to discover lands hidden from the eyes of others. He is ready even to lose his life for the fulfilment of that ideal."

"We are surrounded by well-fed idlers who, if asked to climb a mountain and bring back a medicinal herb, will lie that they went and found no such herb simply to avoid the labour of climbing."

"And there are plenty of tricksters who, fearing that they may be sent to another mountain, bring back some counterfeit in place of the herb they were asked to find! There is no one in the world like our Columbus. He is a hero of action. I shall welcome him so magnificently that kings will be envious and the victory celebrations of other heroes will be put to shame."

"Say that *we* shall welcome him!"

"Yes, we shall welcome him."

"A garden banquet! A dance recital! Music! Garlands of praise from poets!"

"All that and more. When the welcoming ceremony is held and he is brought into our court, we must spread cloth of gold overhead and have him enter beneath that canopy."

"Do you mean that we should present him with a golden shawl?"

"No, no! We have presented golden shawls to many. The sky forms the roof above the path on which he walks. We shall replace it with cloth of gold: a golden roof! Poor man! For how many days must he have looked up wearily at the sky while lost upon an uncharted sea and contending with the troubles caused by bewildered companions?"

"Yes. He must have thought, 'The sky is my roof and the sea my playground.'"

"Why, he may also have lamented, 'Is the sea to be my grave?' Inbavalli, let him look upward during this welcome and see golden silk. He has seen the cloud-filled heavens; today let him rejoice at a canopy of gold."

"An excellent idea, my lord! The cloth of gold is fitting. The welcome must truly be a celebration."

* * *

The welcome was indeed magnificent. The cloth of gold was spread like the sky itself, and Columbus came in procession beneath it, filled with pride and joy. King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain conducted this splendid reception, brought him into their court, seated him beside them, praised him, and placed the royal seal upon his great renown. The welcome took place in Barcelona on 15 March 1493.

Could Columbus's heart have failed to rejoice? All the suffering he had endured vanished from his mind. He blessed the king and queen wholeheartedly. He believed that their treating his service as so great and honouring him in this fashion revealed their nobility. It was not merely the monarchs but the whole country that welcomed him. His fame spread beyond the palace, beyond Spain, and into many other countries.

The rulers who had once rejected him now grieved over the good fortune that might have been theirs. Columbus had pleaded with them and spoken many words of assurance:

"My king, trust me! Give me ships, supplies, and your support. I shall discover new realms and present them to you. Do not dismiss this as a fabricated tale. Do not imagine that I speak whatever comes to my lips merely to seize what I can. I am giving you the conclusion of my research and describing a plan that has slowly grown and taken shape in my mind over many days. Do not hesitate, and do not doubt my ability."

One such ruler now confessed to himself: "I refused to believe him. I mocked him as some swindler and arrogantly sent him away, saying, 'Rule and enjoy your imaginary kingdom yourself. I already possess a kingdom and the wisdom to know whom to trust and what to do.' Now I am ashamed. That hero has returned crowned with distinction. Ferdinand is said to have honoured him lavishly and spread cloth of gold above him. During that welcome Columbus may even have remembered me with contempt. The new realm he discovered will bring Spain wealth, influence, and commercial profit. How much soldiers' blood would otherwise have been shed to establish a kingdom so far away! Through the grace of one idealistic hero, Spain has gained a new dominion. Who knows what riches may be found there? Ferdinand, enriched by Columbus's victory, will encourage him to discover still more lands for Spain. The Spanish flag flies in a country beyond the Atlantic, where my flag might have flown! What a fool I was to drive Columbus away. Ferdinand was wise enough to use Columbus to plant his flag even in unseen lands."

Many kings thought and grieved in this way.

Before Columbus's eyes were the joyful crowds, the king and queen honouring him with their handclasps, the golden canopy, and the celebration. Yet upon a fine curtain in his mind there appeared for a moment an endless ocean, a great storm, a whale advancing to attack, sailors resisting him, and groups of native inhabitants. There also appeared scenes of his unrolling charts before many people, pointing out straight and curved lines and explaining his design, only for lords and kings to listen carelessly, laugh arrogantly, order him to take away his papers, and point after him crying, "Look at that madman! He says he is going to create a new world!"

The visions vanished in another instant. Before him sat the smiling sovereigns of Spain; above him was the golden canopy; around him stood those who welcomed him; the banquet was under way. Joy surged in Columbus's heart.

"Long live Columbus! Long live Columbus, the mightiest of heroes, who sailed the turbulent sea without fear and discovered a new world!"

The people's roar was the reward for years of hardship.

Even his friends had once thought him a dreamer. "Beyond the Atlantic lie vast lands," Columbus had said. "Because there is no regular route, no one has yet visited them. Merely because no one has gone there, people have concluded that nothing exists there. Their conclusion is wrong. I shall demonstrate that error to the world and discover those new lands."

As he spoke with fervour, important men winked at one another, women curled their lips, and young men clapped and laughed.

Columbus had been born into a very ordinary family. No remarkable incident marked his youth, nor did his early conduct suggest that a plan capable of changing the course of the world was forming within him. His brother Bartholomew made charts useful to mariners. Columbus married the daughter of a prominent Italian naval officer, and through these connections his knowledge of sea routes, the history of distant islands, commerce, and related subjects grew.

Those two connections alone could not have enabled him to perform a deed praised by the world. Yet they awakened in Columbus the desire to discover new countries, and he devoted himself to the necessary research. How many nights must he have tossed without sleep? How many days must he have forgotten himself and struggled inwardly? Before that immense plan took form in his mind, indescribable storms and gentle breezes must have alternated within him.

At last an unshakeable conviction arose in Columbus: by crossing the Atlantic Ocean, new lands could be reached. Many mocked him when he first said so, but he did not lose heart. He repeated it to more and more people. Again and again he pleaded with them to believe him and begged them to test his plan.

"Go away, madman! The world we already have is enough. You need not discover another one!" they jeered.

"What an accomplished cosmic liar! He boldly claims something never reported by the thousands who have sailed the seas. He says wonderful countries lie beyond the Atlantic, lands that no one has ever seen. What tales he spins!"

Columbus did not tire. He continued speaking and pleading, never abandoning the belief that someone would place faith in his words. He approached kings eager to enlarge their domains and increase their wealth. Yet everyone regarded his plan as an empty dream, a swindler's scheme, or a madman's babbling.

He appealed to the Republic of Genoa, spoke to the king of Portugal, and sent word to the British king. Everywhere mockery and contempt were his only answers.

Thinking to approach the Spanish sovereign, he tried repeatedly to obtain an audience. Was that an easy task? Was Columbus a king, an ambassador, a court poet, or a beautiful dancer, that an interview should be readily granted? Only after great difficulty did he meet Ferdinand of Spain and explain his plan.

"Three ships, a few men to accompany me, some provisions, and your blessing: give me these, and I shall present you with a great new kingdom," he said.

Somehow a measure of faith arose in the king. "So be it! If you discover a new realm, I shall make you its governor and grant you and your heirs one-tenth of its revenue. If you return empty-handed, there will be no reward."

Columbus stepped from the world of dreams into the world of reality.

The ships departed, his eyes shining with ardour. They sailed a great distance until nothing but sea lay as far as the eye could reach. All the lands familiar from ordinary voyages and trading journeys had disappeared.

"A vast land lies in the distance. Come, I shall show it to you! My calculations are not false. My plan is no dream. My effort will not be wasted!" he declared passionately.

The companions who saw nothing before them but sea seethed with weariness, disgust, terror, and anger. "He is a madman," they too said.

The world that then existed only in that "madman's" mind is the American continent at which the world now marvels.

He sailed on without fearing anything. Then came the cry: "There is a place! Ground! Land! A territory! A kingdom!" Each man called it something different. They rejoiced and danced. Columbus's gaze seemed to ask, "A madman, am I not?"

He named the place he had found and planted Spain's flag there. Spurred by victory, he continued the voyage. He found other places and new islands and claimed them all for Spain. Far from Spain, the Spanish flag flew. Spain acquired new realms: countries its king had never seen, lands never before marked upon a world map, places beyond imagination. The Spanish flag flew in all of them. They were filled with natural beauty and wealth. There was resistance, and Columbus overcame it.

Then he returned. The radiance of victory danced upon his face. Products of the discovered land were piled in one part of the ship, and he brought back several inhabitants of that country as well.

It was after this astonishing victory that Ferdinand and Isabella gave Columbus the magnificent welcome, seated him as their equal, and honoured him beneath the golden canopy. His fame spread across many lands. Those who had mocked and condemned him as madman and cheat were ashamed. Columbus stood revealed as a victorious hero.

"However much a person is neglected and mocked, victory is certain if he possesses the resolve to complete the task he has undertaken and perseveres without yielding. Those who reviled him will sing his praise; those who mocked him will extol and worship him. If you doubt it, look at Columbus today. The king is delighted to call him his friend. The Spanish realm honours him, and the whole country praises him."

Many spoke in this fashion.

Columbus rejoiced that the sovereign's support had enabled his plan to succeed. A man might possess genius and be an exceptional explorer, he reflected, but for him to succeed and for many to benefit from that success, some compassionate person must extend a helping hand at the right moment. Otherwise his talent becomes a flower blooming unseen in the wilderness or a lute with no one to play it. "My delightful dream came true only because of the aid of a king capable of doing what was fitting for a worthy man."

He did not rest content with the victory already attained. Calling it merely a sign of still greater victories to come, he again obtained the king's command and boarded a ship to discover more new lands. This time he rejoiced to see the royal household itself bless and send him on his way.

On his first voyage he had received the townspeople's mockery, the arrogance of some, and the king's doubt-filled permission. Some had said, "There goes a man who does not know how to make a living." Others retorted, "Does he not? He knows very well: he has told lies, bewitched the king, and obtained money for his journey." Some predicted he would become a pirate; others that the sea would devour him.

When he departed a second time, however, no one dared mock him and no opponent appeared. They praised him as a gifted man certain to return with still more victories. Victory had so completely changed the situation. The ship sailed amid celebration, and Columbus was honoured as its guardian. The king rejoiced to see his realm expanding, while the people delighted in the growth of their country's fame.

But did everyone rejoice? No, no! Though fragrance fills a jasmine garden, the nature of the snake concealed in the bushes does not change. In the same way, although Columbus's victory filled most Spaniards with pride and delight, it stirred anger and envy in a few.

"He actually did what he said he would! What honours and welcome that fellow has received! He once lay forsaken at every door and wandered like a madman; today he sits beside the emperor!" they muttered.

Envy blazed. "His head will swell with pride now. He will no longer respect the people of this realm. He will even manipulate the king!"

While Columbus paced his ship anxiously calculating which island might lie in each direction, the envious calculated whom to approach, what quarrels to kindle, and how to ruin his fame and destroy his influence. Columbus knew the sharks of self-interest, the whales of selfishness, and the whirlpools of conspiracy at sea; he did not know that they existed within his own country too.

He remained happy. He thought everyone applauded his victory. Though immediate fame had come to him, did not the material benefit and lasting renown belong to the country? Why should anyone harbour poisonous thoughts toward a man who had set out upon such a good undertaking? He did not know that such people exist everywhere and in every age. Just as waves exist in the sea, treachery exists in society.

He did not know that victory alone is enough to turn some people into enemies, that villains roll stones down without cause upon anyone entering the pass that leads to fame, or that some cannot bear to see another rise to a station none among themselves has reached. He kept steering his ship onward while the treacherous slowly fed sedition at home.

"Columbus is fortunate! Ferdinand is a man of great character who gave him a hand and raised him up."

"There are men who kick away the ladders by which they climbed. Columbus is now said to believe he has risen so high that he no longer needs a ladder."

"On the ship he sits enthroned as if he were its sovereign."

"The native inhabitants have apparently come to believe that Columbus himself is the king of Spain."

"The fellow is clever! He brought heaps of precious stones from the new territories and did not show even one to the king."

"If he returns victorious this time too, no one will ever be able to restrain him."

"And what of the king? From now on he is only a clay doll."

"They say Columbus himself spoke that way."

"Yes, while drunk!"

"They say he gave some of the jewels he brought to dancing girls. Why speak about him? The king imagines that Spain's fame grows through him, while he uses the king's support merely to raise his own position."

"A selfish man! A gambler! Arrogant! Greedy! Power-mad! Conceited!"

At night in a forest, tigers, wolves, wild dogs, buffaloes, porcupines, and many other creatures emerge and wander in search of prey. In the same way, while Columbus was away, all these poisonous rumours came forth. They spread through the country, corrupted many hearts, and even poisoned the minds of its rulers. The cultivators of venom rejoiced. While Columbus gazed happily at a patch of green far away and wondered whether it might be a new island, the conspirators rejoiced as they calculated where their rumours had spread, whose mind each had corrupted, and how much resistance they had produced against him.

Having discovered several new places and planted the Spanish flag properly in each, Columbus returned home. He found none of the old jubilation among the people. Smiles no longer came naturally. People praised him, but mockery seemed to sound even in their praise, and a kind of hostility appeared in their gaze.

Columbus was troubled. He later learned that selfish men had spoken maliciously and poisoned the atmosphere while he was away. Anger surged within him. "Bah! Are these people human? Should they believe whatever some fellow says for some unknown purpose and diminish the affection they held for me? Is this the full measure of their character? Was it for this that I endured so much?"

Then he reflected again. "Poor people! What can they do? Deceivers fabricated tales out of envy toward me, and the people were fooled into believing them. Their hearts have been corrupted because they do not know the truth."

He resolved to state the truth and demolished every accusation made against him by the agitators. The people recognized their mistake.

"We suspected him needlessly. Listening to someone's words, we reviled Columbus. Only now do we know that not one accusation the deceivers made against him has any foundation."

Columbus became a gem cleansed of grime. Yet the people's conduct and the conduct of the envious men who had produced it inflicted a wound in his heart that could not be healed. He had a heart, after all; he too was human.

Columbus sailed once more from Spain in search of lands. He discovered Trinidad and several other new places, established Spanish rule in the territory called Hispaniola, and suppressed the resistance that arose there.

Columbus might subdue the native inhabitants' resistance through fierce battle, but what could he do about the resistance of the agitators? They were working in Spain while Columbus was in Hispaniola.

The conspirators stirred opposition to Columbus skilfully in Spain. They understood precisely which spring to press.

However much Ferdinand praised Columbus and his ability and declared that his service had brought glory to the kingdom, a thought must nevertheless have crept into the king's mind: "Columbus obtained all this fame only through my support. He discovered new countries, and that is indeed worthy of honour. But who revealed Columbus to the world? Was it not I? Had I not approved and supported his plan, he would have remained babbling like a madman. My kingdom has grown larger, but I would still have been a king; he would have remained a madman. It was I who made the world know him."

Knowing this sensitive point, the schemers worked upon it carefully. Their stratagem succeeded.

Did Ferdinand bring fame to Columbus, or did Columbus bring fame to Ferdinand? Who benefited from whom? What position could Columbus have attained without Ferdinand? What new kingdoms could Ferdinand have obtained without Columbus?

Did Columbus respect Ferdinand or not? Did Ferdinand appreciate Columbus's service and talent or not? Would their relationship endure or break? Was Columbus's victory after victory good or harmful for Ferdinand?

When questions like these are raised, can even the purest-hearted people refrain from saying a word or two for one side or the other? Every such word injured Columbus. While he was far away, the poison spread thoroughly and produced disastrous consequences.

In a single line Queen Isabella revoked the authority she had conferred upon Columbus. His position collapsed. Could royal anger stop there? The queen sent an official named Bobadilla to remove Columbus, assume his office, and exercise his power.

The new official arrived in Hispaniola and dismissed Columbus. Bartholomew was then with his brother. By the queen's command both brothers were put in chains. Shackles upon the hands of the victorious hero! The very hands Isabella had once clasped and honoured were now, by the same queen's order, made to bear an iron burden.

A ship sailed for Spain. On board, Columbus came as a prisoner with his hands chained. What must his state of mind have been? How many scenes must have appeared and vanished before his mind's eye? His heart possessed great strength, and only for that reason could he endure such injustice. A person of weaker resolve would have broken down and died there upon the ship.

Columbus looked at the irons on his hands. The man who had gone as guardian of the ships was being carried back aboard one as a prisoner. He remembered the earlier banquet and pageantry and reflected that such savagery too existed in the country. Poor man! He may not even have had time to weep, for thought after thought must have risen within him like wave after wave.

What a welcome in Barcelona!

What honours in the royal court!

And now, upon the sea and aboard a ship, a prisoner with shackles upon his hands!

The ship's captain could not bear the sight. He told Columbus, "I shall remove those fetters."

"What is this deed you propose to do, my friend? Is not this iron burden the finest proof of the Spanish sovereigns' noble way of showing gratitude? Remove it? No, no! Let this iron burden remain upon my hands until death. I shall not lose this priceless gift. Who else in the realm receives a gift like this iron burden?"

Thus he refused to have it removed.

The ship reached port, and the news spread through the land.

"They have chained Columbus!"

"Shackles upon the hands of the man who mastered the sea!"

"Columbus has been arrested."

"Is this the punishment for the man who spread Spain's fame?"

"Do they humiliate like this the man who made our flag fly in places whose very direction was unknown?"

"What an atrocity! Why this injustice? Shackles for Columbus!"

The people's hearts throbbed. They raised their voices and grew angry. All Spain was aflame with indignation.

"Shackles for Columbus! Columbus made a prisoner! Cruelty! Cruelty!"

The cry arose everywhere and grew stronger. The people opposed the king's mind and the queen's hasty judgement. The king heard their clamour and understood the cause of their fury. He ordered Columbus released, declared him innocent, and promised him rewards, which he gave. The people prevailed. The great hero was freed, and the king wiped away the dust that the envious had smeared upon Columbus.

"Columbus is innocent!" That was the people's verdict, and the king accepted it. Yet Columbus had lost his peace of mind, and neither king nor people understood it.

Did Ferdinand profit through Columbus? Did Columbus profit through Ferdinand? This was the very question the mischief-makers had turned into a dispute. Why did the people allow it? Was that truly the issue?

Columbus and Ferdinand were both symbols of their age. Ferdinand represented an age ready to support fearless explorers. Columbus represented an age ready to accept any danger in order to discover new places. Why did the people not understand this?

The crowned Ferdinand and the ship-borne Columbus were not immortal. But the new lands obtained through their cooperation would endure. Why did the people fail to reflect upon that?

Who profited through whom? Why did the people make this ruinous calculation when another calculation stood clear and undeniable? Because Columbus and Ferdinand worked together, Spain obtained a new realm and the world learned of new territories. Was that not the profit? Why did they forget that calculation and engage in conduct that would destroy the benefit? A ship travels upon the sea. Is the question whether the ship benefits from the sea or the sea benefits from the ship? Yet this is what the people made into a dispute, working to ruin cooperation.

Such thoughts entered Columbus's mind and tormented him. His former enthusiasm was destroyed. He sailed again and discovered still more places, but he was no longer the old Columbus. They had killed the Columbus who once worked with immense joy because at least the king of Spain had accepted and supported his plan.

The golden canopy and the iron burden: in one lifetime he had seen both conditions. He had seen the queen who clasped his hand and the queen who commanded, "Shackle that hand and drag him here." Life held no savour for him. He continued to move about until he was fifty, but only as a shadow of the old Columbus. Poverty overtook him, and misery tormented him. He ceased appearing before others and became a solitary man.

Columbus died in 1506. Until his death he alone did not relinquish that iron burden. Who knows how many nights his tears fell upon those irons? At his request, when he died, that iron burden was buried with him.

## Translator's Notes

“இரும்பாரம்” literally means an iron burden and refers here to Columbus's shackles; the title's bitter contrast with the earlier golden canopy is retained. The Tamil source is preserved verbatim in the bilingual output, including damaged OCR. Clear readings used only in translation include வரவேற்பு for வூரவேற்பு, சாதாரணமானவனல்ல for ஈமான்யனல்ல, நடைபெற்ற for நல டபெறாத/நல டபெற்ற, காட்சி for கூரட்சி, பெர்டினாண்டு for damaged variants, போவாடில்லா for Bobadilla, and தளைகளை for தலைகளை in the captain's offer to remove the fetters. The source presents a dramatic, moralized account of Columbus and contains historical details that may be inaccurate, including dates, offices, motives, treatment of Indigenous peoples, age at death, and the claim that the irons were buried with him. These are translated as the author's claims and are not independently endorsed or verified. Colonial-era descriptions such as காட்டுமிராண்டிகள் are rendered contextually as “native inhabitants” rather than repeated as dehumanizing labels; the coercion and conquest described remain visible in the translation.

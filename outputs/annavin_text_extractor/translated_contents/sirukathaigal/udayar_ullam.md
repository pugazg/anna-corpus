## English Translation

“What anger or pride have you left, man? All that disappeared long ago.

“Once you would say, ‘If a man steps upon my foot, I shall not rest until I step upon his head,’ and you would do it too. Now, if someone steps upon your head, you seize his foot to relieve its pain. That is the extent of your temper.

“Only your moustache remains twisted upward. Your courage broke and folded away long ago.

“It is not only you. There was a time when the whole village screamed at the sight of our ‘goring bull.’ In a single year, four men ran away saying, ‘We cannot handle it, master.’ Such was that bull. And now? It seems not even to need a nose rope.

“Some markings produce that result, but I never expected it to become a trembling coward so quickly.

“A weak fellow like me may naturally be afraid. If only I had your body! But what comes from saying it? If an entire bamboo forest turned into sugarcane, would money overflow from a cash box? It is just as useless for me to think, ‘If I had your body, would any fellow dare wag his tail before me?’”

When people heard the speech of bachelor Ulagappan, no one felt like saying, “Do not provoke trouble or poison the mind.” He spoke as one slides a needle into a banana without leaving a mark.

Whenever he formed a plan, a quarrel over a field boundary or irrigation channel, a verbal fight, slashing, and stabbing would all arise.

No one could know when trouble would begin, whom he would incite against whom, or what reason he would offer. The clash would lead to court.

Then came Ulagappan’s work: arranging a lawyer, supplying the lawyer with “points,” preparing witnesses, arranging to mortgage or sell land. To put it briefly, he created another Ulagappan in the village.

Work? It was an art, sir, an art.

Ulagappan did not master that art without paying an offering. Only after selling six acres of wet ryotwari land and eight acres of dry land could he become an expert.

He spent his time finding exceptional pleasure in quarrels and lawsuits, so the thought of marriage never arose.

Fathers of daughters withdrew in fear. “It would be better to throw her into a ruined well. If we give her to this troublemaker, thunder will fall throughout her life.”

That was why Ulagappan remained a bachelor.

Some experienced elders alone said his bachelorhood was precisely the reason he had become like this.

“Had a marriage tether been tied around his leg at the proper time, he would not have turned out this way. Men who once roamed obeying no one have been subdued by a wife speaking in a low, drawn-out voice: ‘You are the man, are you not? Is this conduct proper? What happiness did I find by marrying you? Are there no ponds in this village?’

“Is he some invincible warrior? He roams like an unruly bull only because he remains unmarried,” they said.

After selling all his land, bachelor Ulagappan made himself “adviser” to Manickam Pillai, who possessed estate rights in the village.

Through “the grace of Vezhamugan,” Manickam Pillai had enlarged the thirty ancestral acres he inherited into one hundred and fifty.

His father, Sadasivam Pillai, had repeatedly pleaded:

“Attend to the land and irrigation and remain with the village. Preserve the name our family has possessed for generations.”

For years Sadasivam complained:

“The fellow who never disobeyed me said he disliked the village and went to town. Would he defy my word by himself? A temptress seized and shook him, his wife. It was her doing.”

After achieving his “successes,” Manickam spoke loudly enough for Sadasivam to hear:

“Only by going to town could I see money with my own eyes and buy more land. Father is completely old-fashioned. Had I listened to him, the thirty acres would have become ten rather than increase.”

Sadasivam had grown old. His hearing and sight were failing.

“My son is saying something, but I cannot understand it,” he muttered.

In truth, the son’s words reached his ears clearly. Unable to answer them, he used poor hearing as his excuse.

The village was called Mupponur, a name meaning a place yielding three harvests, though its pronunciation had been corrupted over time.

Forty stone-measures away, Kadanur had grown rapidly after a cotton mill was established and had become Kadanur Town. There Manickam owned a factory manufacturing machine parts needed by the mill.

In Kadanur Town he was Estate Manickam. When he came to Mupponur, he was Factory Owner Manickam.

Whenever Estate Manickam Pillai visited the village, bachelor Ulagappan provided his recreation.

This was not amusement of the kind involving questions such as: “What relation do you have with her? Can it be done or not? Tell her to come near the mango grove.”

His recreation consisted of gathering information:

Which land was fertile? Whose finances were weakening? Whose debt burden was increasing? Would sugarcane yield well? Who would receive the contract to cut the forest-river canal? What were the characters of the cooperative bank’s directors? Was it true that one of them “took a little”?

“I am going to the village for ten quiet days,” Manickam would say in town. Yet there would be no rest during those ten days. Some garden, field, cow, or cart would be bought or sold.

When Sadasivam Pillai closed his eyes for the last time, he died happy in the belief that one whole square of Mupponur’s wet land belonged to them.

A newly rich man dared disturb that condition.

He gradually bought every parcel Manickam Pillai had declined to purchase, paying a little more each time. He became a man of consequence and then a great man.

“He came from far in the south and did not possess a paisa of ancestral property. Mupponur is a village where our family has held estate rights since my great-grandfather’s time. Yet he has enclosed a hundred acres.

“The fools in our village forgot that greed brings great loss. Tempted because he offered four coins more, they gave place to an outsider,” Estate Manickam complained to the elders who came seeking a contribution for the Mariamman festival.

“Brother, through what business did that man earn so much? He closes his eyes and pays whatever price is asked.”

“You ask what his earnings come from? His money lies in a ladle.”

“An electric company?”

“Neither an electric company nor a cement company. A ladle, a cooking ladle.”

“A hotel keeper?”

“Hotel keeper, indeed. Look at the title. He used to sell packed meals to mill workers, each packet for eight annas. He made a proper profit. Now he is a hotel proprietor.”

“Then say the business grew strong.”

“These days not one fellow can rise and walk without drinking coffee. One coffee costs four annas, ‘special.’ Do you know how much remains as profit on each cup? No less than three annas.

“One quarter coffee husk and three quarters sawdust. Where does he put sugar? Only saccharin. Goat’s milk becomes cow’s milk. Nothing but fraud.”

“Look at his fortune, brother. Money rolls in by the thousands.”

Thus the conversation went.

Estate Manickam’s attempt to demean the new notable produced the opposite result. Many began praising hotel owner Thandavamurthi and yearned to form connections with him.

It was Thandavamurthi’s granary that bachelor Ulagappan urged Akkarai Pakkiriyappan to set on fire.

Pakkiriyappan had worked for several years on a Ceylon tea estate and returned, hence the title “Akkarai,” the man from overseas.

He was father to six children and owned half an acre. His means of living were to stand submissively before the estate-holder, lease a grove, go to market to catch and bring back cattle, and “teach a lesson” to anyone who dared oppose Estate Manickam.

“When Pakkiri draws his blade, he would not fear even if an army came. What if the police catch him? He says he will merely visit his mother-in-law’s house and return,” the village said of his courage.

But Ulagappan mocked that the spinning top had slowed and bent its head.

Manickam thought that once the hotel keeper learned outsiders would not be tolerated, he would sell his land for half its value and leave.

That was why he had instructed Ulagappan, with a strict warning that his own name must never be drawn into the affair. He also promised to handle matters if a case arose.

With his distinctive skill and tact, bachelor Ulagappan put the proposal to Pakkiriyappan.

Pakkiri passed the time making excuses: “It is dangerous work, sir,” and “It is sinful.”

Meanwhile, about five hundred rupees passed from the estate-holder to Ulagappan, and one quarter of it reached Pakkiri. The work alone remained unfinished.

Thandavamurthi’s granary stood full of kichili samba rice. Estate Manickam could no longer endure the situation.

Ulagappan did eventually succeed.

When Pakkiri’s youngest son developed a poisonous fever, Ulagappan joined his palms, clasped Pakkiri’s feet, and lent him money.

“All of it becomes a gift if you finish what I told you,” he said.

Pakkiri went to perform the task, choosing a time of deep twilight.

Kanni, who guarded the granary, awoke. There was a fight and slashing with a sickle. Kanni’s son, a soldier home on leave, seized and rolled Pakkiri over repeatedly.

Thinking only of saving his head, Pakkiri fled the village. The granary suffered no damage. Shame tore at Ulagappan.

Pakkiri wandered from place to place after fleeing. Poverty struck his family.

He had once told his wife:

“Child, if danger ever comes to me, fall at Master Manickam’s feet and ask. He will not abandon you.”

Remembering this, his wife Sornam went to Manickam and wept as she described her hardship.

Manickam became fiercely angry.

“After what your husband did, must I assist you as well? One of our village landlords trusted our village, poured in money to buy land, and provides livelihoods to our people. Yet Pakkiri dared rob his granary. Is that proper?

“What happens to him today will happen to me tomorrow. Kanni protected the granary and preserved our village’s honour.

“Had anything happened and four people in town asked, ‘Estate-holder, does such injustice happen in your village? Is this how you administer it?’ where could I hide my face?

“Go, go. If I help you, people will think badly of me.”

He sent her away categorically.

Ulagappan tried to intervene, but Manickam silenced him with a single look.

* * *

“He saw her only once, but decided in his heart that he must marry only her. He says the girl is very beautiful, modest, educated, and employed,” Govindammal joyfully told her husband Kanni.

“If he consents, what objection have I? These days, life proceeds well only if the person one marries is pleasing to one’s own heart.

“That was the old time, when elders could find some ghost-like woman, tie her to a man, and he would say yes. Look how I agreed in those days when they tied you upon my head,” Kanni teased, mixing affection with nostalgia.

“What is wrong with having married me?” Govindammal asked in fond anger.

“What benefit comes from thinking about it now?” Kanni mocked as he lit a cheroot.

The conversation followed news from their son. Having completed military service and now seeking work as a bus driver, he wrote that a young woman he liked lived in Pattur.

Kanni and Govindammal went to Pattur to see the prospective bride. It was easy to inquire and find the house of Valli, who worked as a teacher.

But the moment they stepped inside, Pakkiri sprang at them, shouting wildly:

“How dare you enter this house, you old rogue? Are you the man thinking of forming a marriage alliance here? It will not happen. As long as one drop of life remains in my body, it will never happen.”

Was the girl their son had chosen none other than the sinner Pakkiri’s daughter?

Had the foolish boy forgotten what kind of conduct Pakkiri possessed and what unending feud he had created with their family? Was a pretty face enough?

If they formed an alliance here, would the whole village not spit upon them? How furious would the master become if he heard?

“Kanni, of all men, did you find only the fellow who came to rob and burn my granary as your relation?” he would rage.

Even Kanni himself felt it was not right.

“Shame, we came hastily without knowing,” he thought.

At that moment, Valli returned from the school. What serenity in her face, kindness in her eyes, and modesty in her smile.

Seeing her, Govindammal thought, “If only we were fortunate enough to receive this golden statue as our daughter-in-law.”

Kanni released a long sigh.

* * *

“Why ask about it, relation? The villagers increasingly refuse to submit to any discipline. We pour gold into buying earth and then must wrestle with these creatures,” said Manickam.

“I too acted hastily and bought rather more land than I should have,” said Thandavamurthi.

“Yes, yes, relation. At the time many people came and incited me: ‘When you, the estate-holder, are here, can some outsider buy our village land?’”

“Whenever any land came up for sale, I first asked whether you had set your eye upon it. Only after suitable people said, ‘No, he has no interest,’ did I pay an advance.”

“Look at the Lord’s wonder. See how he brought the two of us together. Many in the village even said hostility existed between us.”

“Was that not when a rogue came to rob the granary? Do not mention it. Some even came and quarrelled with me, claiming the fellow Pakkiri dared do it only at your instigation.”

“Those dried-headed fellows profit only by separating us. They live by carrying tales at both houses.”

“Do I not know that you drove Pakkiri from the village? The fellow will not show his head here again. If he does, our Kanni will slice it off. An unending feud exists between them.

“The master entrusted the granary to Kanni, and Pakkiri displayed his handiwork there in a way that could ruin Kanni’s name. Even now, Kanni says with undiminished anger that he will cut Pakkiri into pieces if he sees him.”

* * *

The whole village of Mupponur wore a festive appearance for the wedding of Geetha, daughter of Estate Manickam Pillai, and Ravi, the only son of Modern Lodge proprietor Thandavamurthi.

The newly related men looked at the bridal couple’s photograph published in the daily paper. Delighted Thandavamurthi showed it to Manickam.

Then he saw a report upon another page and cried in alarm:

“Have you seen this, relation?”

Estate Manickam looked.

Murder! Murder! Brutal murder!

“Because of an old feud, Pakkiri stabbed to death Kanni, an agricultural labourer of Mupponur. Police are investigating.”

14 January 1966

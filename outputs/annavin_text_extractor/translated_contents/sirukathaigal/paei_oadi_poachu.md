## English Translation

"Elder brother! I had imagined Priest Ponnan to be an ordinary sort of man. Now I know he is no lightweight."

"What have you discovered now?"

"My daughter had been in a faint for some days, remember? The spirit in the mango tree had possessed her and was tormenting her, elder brother."

"Yes, your daughter Sellayi."

"Yes! The spirit fled because of the priest's spell. Now my girl is well again. She laughs and talks, dresses herself and wears flowers in her hair. My son used to listen to city people and tell us that magic and spells were all false. I believed him because he did not live in a remote village like us but in a city where trams run, and must have heard highly educated people say such things.

"Foolish boy! Only now do I understand that one cannot simply call spells false. Sellayi is well. Earlier, did she not lie curled up, refuse to speak properly or wash her face, do no work and answer irritably?"

"That is how it is when a spirit takes hold."

"Yes. That fellow said there were no ghosts or spirits and that all of it was false. Is a spirit from a mango tree something trivial?"

"Indeed! The fellow who hanged himself was very wicked even while alive. Once he became a spirit, need one ask what mischief he would make? Well, she has recovered, so let it go. Next Friday, slaughter a cockerel and make an offering to Sokki Amman. I shall come.

"Now that the spirit has left your daughter, should you not settle the marriage?"

"Yes, elder brother. We must. My son is staying here precisely for that."

"Where have you found a match?"

"Our Velan."

"I had forgotten. You arranged him for your daughter last year, did you not?"

"Yes. The fellow looked at her, showed his teeth and awakened desire in my girl."

"Never mind. He is a good boy."

"Yes. When he leased the field beside ours, I suspected he had not come next door merely to cultivate crops."

"I hear he even paid a high lease."

"Yes, elder brother. Consider the lease finished with this year. The foolish boy leased it, but did he sow brinjal or radish and earn four coins? No. He planted roses."

"Naturally. If Sellayi likes anything, it is flowers."

"See the cleverness of today's young people! Let it be. May the children live happily. I had been terrified after the spirit possessed her, wondering what fate might come."

"No harm will come to you. Go in peace."

This conversation took place in the village of Orur between the farmer Sengodan and Kariyan, an elder of the village.

Fearing what the spirit might do to his daughter Sellayi, Sengodan had left no ritual undone. At last, only through the grace of Priest Ponnan had she survived. After sharing the happy news with village elder Kariyappa, Sengodan set off to find someone else to whom he could tell it. Since morning, relating the news to one person after another had been his only occupation.

He had already told three quarters of the residents of Orur, which was indeed the village's name, and went in search of Ellan, who was drawing water with the irrigation lift.

Sengodan had feared that no man would marry a woman possessed by a spirit. He had even feared for her life. He had only one daughter and one son.

The son, Subbu, had learned his "two letters" and therefore worked in the city, in a newspaper office no less. His work was only tying parcels, but Sengodan proudly called it newspaper-office work. There was no great error in that. Subbu came from a family that had known nothing but the plough for two generations. If he wore a white shirt and khaki trousers and went to an office, was that not a source of pride?

Whenever Subbu returned on leave, he appeared an individual apart while speaking to anyone in the village. Would he discuss farming? He knew nothing of it. Instead he spoke of world affairs to the extent that his hurried reading had taught him. The villagers listened open-mouthed, and Sengodan swelled with pride when the whole village gathered two by two to hear news from his son.

"The sun, you know, the sun..." Subbu would begin.

"Tell us! The Sun God is the deity visible to our eyes. What about him?" Kutti would ask devoutly.

"The sun is neither a god nor a man. It is a ball of fire," Subbu would say.

Kutti would wink at the man beside him.

"Then does that ball roll from morning until evening?" Kuppan would ask mockingly.

Subbu handled all such questions and explained many matters to them.

"Our Subbu tells us all kinds of things!" they praised. Even when some did not understand, they concluded that what he said must be correct: Subbu knew everything. The prestige his office job gave him was more than even ownership of ten *kani* of land could have provided.

The newspaper where he worked was named *Ulagam* and was filled with coloured pictures of every kind. With effort, Subbu cut out all those pictures, pasted them in a large notebook and gave it to Sellayi. She treasured the book her elder brother gave her. Whenever she found an opportunity, she showed it to the village women.

Even from that precious book Sellayi had torn two or three pages. Did the poor girl do it? Sengodan told his son that all of it was the spirit's work. Subbu did not worry about the damaged book; he worried about his sister's health.

On his previous visit, Sellayi had been entirely well, indeed very happy.

"Where did you go, girl?" Sengodan would ask.

"Toward the fields," Sellayi would answer.

"Why?"

"The red calf may have gone that way," mischievous Kuppan would interject.

It was springtime in the season of their hearts: the time when affection arose between Velan and Sellayi. Villagers did not know to call it love.

Velan was the son of drunken Govindan. "This boy has none of his father's nature. If he sees toddy from half a mile away, he vomits," villagers said of him.

The arrival of black hair upon the son's upper lip coincided with the onset of rheumatism in Govindan. Velan therefore took charge of the farming and lived as a respected young man of the village.

"Velu is terribly wicked," Sellayi alone would say.

Was he not wicked? Why should he stare at Sellayi as though swallowing her whenever he passed? Yet he looked only at Sellayi; his eyes did not wander.

"Why, Velu?" some would tease.

"Nothing at all, elder brother," he would reply humbly with a smile.

It was only when he sang:

> Sweet maiden! Queen Alli!  
> Beautiful lady, Queen Alli!  
> May I come near you, Queen Alli?

that Sellayi herself understood the truth that she too loved Velan.

It was the village play, and Velan played Arjuna. "Who else in this place could take a king's role?" drama teacher Kumaresan had said, filling Velan with pride.

That night Velan sang with extraordinary feeling the song taught for Arjuna. The actor dressed as Alli stood beside him, but Velan did not sing toward that man. He looked directly to the side where Sellayi sat. Not even a celebrated actor could have sung as he did that day, for was it not a song dedicated to Sellayi?

The actor playing Alli answered:

> I have discovered your trick, your trick;  
> Man, I know your trick!

Sellayi knew the song, but how could she sing it? She would have been too shy.

At this time Subbu felt proud whenever he saw Sellayi. "My sister is a wild jasmine blooming in the countryside, while women consumed by tuberculosis and dim-eyed women strut fashionably in the city," he thought.

He learned a little of the matter involving Velan.

"Sellayi, there is a tree at the far end of our field. What tree is it?" Subbu asked one day.

"That one, elder brother? I do not know," Sellayi lied, contrary to her habit.

Pretending not to understand, Subbu asked, "You go past it every day. Do you not even know what kind of tree it is?"

"A sticky tree, elder brother," she answered, running inside.

Sengodan laughed until his stomach shook.

"See her cleverness! A sticky tree, she says. Apparently she must not utter the man's name before the marriage pendant reaches her neck. If she says *vela maram*, the name Velan enters it. What ingenuity, my boy!"

Choosing a time when Govindan was sober, Sengodan spoke to him. "We shall finish the marriage in the month of Thai," Govindan agreed.

Subbu saw that Velan was attractive, of fine character and hardworking, and that Sellayi sincerely loved him. He rejoiced that it would be a fitting love marriage.

They could take a photograph and publish it in the newspaper. Wedding blocks appeared for so many worn-out ladies! But how would newspaper people appreciate the noble beauty of a farmer's daughter from Orur and Velan's heroic appearance? It would be merely a picture of villagers. Was theirs a landlord's household, or was one of them a doctor whose photograph deserved publication? Subbu suppressed the desire.

In his mind, however, the sight of his sister and Velan living happily remained imprinted more clearly than a photograph.

In this situation, how could Subbu avoid shock and tears when a letter arrived saying, "Sellayi is unwell. A spirit has possessed her. Do not fear; we are doing what is necessary"?

He had no leave, so he wrote his father a detailed letter:

"There are no ghosts or spirits. It is an illness. Give her proper medicine. Do not ruin Sellayi's health with useless spells and magic. I shall come in ten days."

For a while Sengodan obeyed his educated son and did not summon an exorcist. But Sellayi grew thin, remained faint and lay with dishevelled hair. The "spirit" became so severe that she threatened to fall into a well or pond.

"Sellayi, what is troubling your body?" her mother would ask lovingly.

Sellayi remained silent, terror in her eyes. When her mother pressed her, tears poured from them. She scarcely ate, wore the same dirty sari without washing it, and lay near the cooking place or the back passage.

"Rise, darling."

"No."

"Why, girl?"

"I feel faint."

That was the whole conversation.

Many said, "It is the spirit from the mango tree, nothing else. The signs are clear. Sengoda, why do you stare? Send for our Priest Ponnan and make him perform a spell."

"My son says none of that is needed," Sengodan replied.

One day, in drunken rage, Govindan shouted, "Did you plan to tie a woman possessed by the mango-tree spirit onto my son's head?"

Sengodan saw and thought only of Sellayi. Velan, Govindan and the marriage pavilion disappeared from his mind. "Let that drunken fellow shout whatever he likes," he said.

Still, he considered sending Velan to the city to see Subbu. Velan answered without compassion, "My father's health is uncertain. How can I leave?"

"I had thought him a golden boy. The worthless fellow used to carry a sickle and roam about. Now he says his father is ill. The old man drinks pot after pot and lies there, while this gentleman melts with concern. We want no relationship with that family," Sengodan complained.

"Let it go. Speak softly. She may hear and fall at his feet again," warned Sengodan's wife Sivappi.

Subbu arrived only several days later. Seeing the situation, he could not bear his grief. Whatever he asked, Sellayi gave only two words: "Nothing. Faintness."

"Why, Father, Velan...?" Subbu began.

"He is a very great man, my boy. He cannot leave his father for even half a second. Subbu, a person's nature becomes known only at times like this," Sengodan said, recounting Velan's conduct.

Subbu too grew angry. "Fool! Idiot! Savage creature!" he muttered.

"Why not look at the picture book to pass the time?" he suggested to his sister.

Sengodan brought it and showed Subbu. "She even tore this, my boy. Is all this an illness? It is the work of the mango-tree spirit," he said.

Subbu took the book and turned its pages. Two or three sheets had been torn out: one showing a motor car, the other a handsomely dressed man.

The handsome man's picture lay mutilated. Who was he? Subbu investigated. For some reason, Sellayi must be angry with that man or someone of similar appearance. Why? Why would such a man come to a remote village? Even if he had, what connection could Sellayi have with him?

The more Subbu thought, the greater his confusion. Sellayi stiffened and sobbed but offered no explanation. Velan merely said, "Who knows?" Subbu resolved not to rest until he cut through the knot.

He himself had made the picture book from images cut out of *Ulagam*. The motor car and handsome man must therefore have come from pictures he had selected. Who was the man?

After long thought without success, Subbu went to the office. By searching old issues, he discovered the answer: the man was a film director named Singh.

The issue also reported that the director was filming an exalted story titled *Village Love*. Perhaps he had visited the countryside to photograph village scenes. Could Sellayi have seen him then? The puzzle could not be solved without meeting the director.

How could a famous director be induced to meet a "fellow who tied parcels"? Somehow Subbu had to do it, and tried several approaches.

One day the director himself came to the office to see the editor.

"Sir, I shall screen the rushes of *Village Love*. Will you come?" the director asked.

The editor's heart urged him to support Russia's course, but the newspaper proprietor was an agent for an American company and directed him to publicize America's remarkable efforts to make the atom bomb an ambassador of the path of love. While he lay confused about what to do, the invitation to see a film arrived like an unexpected feast.

For some reason, the editor took Subbu along.

Only after arriving there did Subbu learn the truth.

One scene praised extravagantly by the editor struck him like a blow.

A buffalo ran by, contrary to its usual nature. A girl ran behind it. She was none other than Sellayi, with a garland of roses around her neck.

A little distance away stood a banyan tree. Beneath it Velan lay gazing upward while a fashionably dressed woman held a bunch of grapes directly over his mouth.

As though these two scenes were insufficient, another appeared that made Subbu tremble.

Sellayi bit into an apple and ate a little. At first Subbu thought she must be absorbed in its sweetness, which explained the radiance of her face. But the next stage stunned him.

Sellayi handed the bitten apple to a fashionably dressed man. He happily took a bite and returned it. She bit it again, handed it back, and he ate once more. Subbu's fury knew no bounds.

The scene changed. Velan and Sellayi quarrelled in rage. Velan seized her hair and tormented her. Sellayi bit his hand. He shoved her so that she struck a tree. Thus the fight continued.

The editor declared the film first-rate.

The director said these were not even its important portions but only village scenes. Once completed, the film would bring honour to the entire cinematic world.

"Are the romance and quarrel in this scene not connected with the story?" the editor asked.

"They must be connected," the director replied. "But these scenes are a reward of my own skill. Let me tell you how I created them."

With this preface, the director explained his talent.

"I went to the village of Orur to film rural scenery. The heroine Oyyari and hero Ongaram came with me. Editor, keep this to yourself: Oyyari's true name is Kuppi. I gave a man named Singaram the new name Ongaram.

"The village was beautiful. In the story, the heroine sulks because she is angry with the hero. For this I imagined that she had fallen in love with a villager. The story writer will learn of the matter only when I tell him.

"I found a village man and employed him for a week. Oyyari employed a village woman. Those two have acted magnificently, but they do not know they acted. Everything is a trick shot.

"First I made the man lie beneath a tree and filmed him. Then I placed grapes in Oyyari's hand and filmed her pretending to offer them to someone. Once I joined the two shots, a story arose: a civilized woman falls for a handsome villager, offers him her heart, plays at love with him and feeds him grapes.

"I gave the village girl an apple and filmed her biting it several times. I gave our hero another apple and filmed him doing the same. I joined the shots skilfully. The result was the apple-biting game between the village woman and the civilized man.

"Was that all? I quickly edited those two sequences, showed the woman the scene involving her man, and showed the man the scene involving his woman. The result was that marvellous fight!"

Subbu struck the director across the face. The next instant he clutched the man's feet. The director was frightened, then felt pity when Subbu explained the matter of Sellayi and Velan.

A motor car set out for Orur. They found Velan, and the director explained what had happened.

"Is that so? Alas! I suspected my pure gold of a girl and spoke words that made her tremble," Velan stammered.

The truth was explained to Sellayi too. She grieved that she had suspected Velan without cause.

Velan and Subbu understood that the suspicion ignited between the pair by a director's "work" had gradually become the "spirit." But Sengodan could not understand such a thing.

He pleaded with Priest Ponnan, who performed his customary waving of neem leaves. Meanwhile, because the truth had become clear and suspicion had vanished, Sellayi's anxiety, mental confusion, faintness and longing all disappeared. She became her old laughing self.

Sengodan believed that the power of Priest Ponnan's spell had driven away the mango-tree spirit possessing his daughter, and said so throughout the village.

How could the poor man know that all this turmoil had been the work of the spirit beneath the banyan tree?

31 August 1947

## Translator's Notes

The title's colloquial “போச்சு” is retained in tone as “Ran Away.” The final “spirit beneath the banyan tree” is the film director working at the staged banyan-tree scene, contrasted satirically with the villagers' mango-tree spirit. The source's film production, political and personal claims are translated as fictional narrative and are not independently verified.

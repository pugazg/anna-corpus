## English Translation

Varadhan the carriage driver was dismissed. Kandasamy the accountant was sent home. The gardener's family was broken up: he was required to finish his work by evening and leave the premises.

The reason Santhana Krishna Iyer issued so many dismissal orders was that, contrary to the promise of his name, he had no child. He had driven his first wife back to her mother's house and found himself a younger wife named Mohana. As soon as that beauty entered the bungalow, every employee in the bloom of youth was removed.

Some fools let sturdy young men roam around the house and then clutch their heads if the woman goes astray. If one puts a hand into a snake pit, the snake will emerge hissing and bite. Mohana was a golden creeper embraced by beauty and youth. Wherever she moved, fragrance spread. If burly fellows repeatedly breathed that perfume until desire went to their heads, they would not stop to think that she was the master's wife. Some man would leer. If something happened, the household would have to live with bowed heads forever. Should cats be allowed to roam where one intends to raise a parrot?

Mohana's proprietor turned the house into a pleasure palace. On one side stood a radio; on another, gramophone records released that week. There were full-length mirrors, elegant chairs, sofas, tables, and beautiful pictures adorning the walls. Even the floor covering in the hall had been specially ordered from Kashmir. Amid all this lovely decoration, the woman of luxury moved with a beauty that could disturb the thoughts of sculptors and the imagination of poets.

Iyer's delight knew no bounds. "Mohana!" he would call three times in three different pitches. When she gave him a single gentle smile, he would swell with happiness and recline upon the sofa.

For two years, their life of pleasure continued without interruption. But she moved about radiating the polish of youth and the fragrance of beauty, while time had crushed him and left him in the courtyard of old age. Had Iyer not possessed sixty *veli* of land and fifty thousand rupees in liquid capital, this granddaughter-aged girl could never have become his wife.

Mohana did not think of her husband's age or her own youth. There was Mohana, and there was pleasure. That was all. The woman knew nothing else. She believed this was life itself. Her mind had no waves; thought and sight remained under restraint.

Then the Iyer who had given her such a life developed a fever. Even after the fever appeared, he did not call a good doctor because he suspected that any man who entered might become a danger. Though the fever made it difficult to open his eyes, he struggled to stay awake until Mohana fell asleep. Then he would look once at her beauty, sigh, and close his eyes in distress as the thought tormented him: "The creeper grows in loveliness while the tree bends with age." Sleep would not come.

Thus an ordinary fever became typhoid. Dr Raghuraman entered with his cigarette and smile, bringing logical medicine for Iyer's illness and a syrup of glances that produced an illness in Mohana.

Dr Raghuraman had forgotten many lessons learned in medical college. One lesson alone he had not forgotten: how to be elegant and speak delicately. Mohana had never encountered this before. Until then, she had only seen Iyer's mouth speak; she had never seen eyes speak. Dr Raghuraman's eyes began saying many things.

"Iyer, you must rest for six months. You have grown old, you see," he said.

"You have grown old." Mohana's mind had been given a new task. The realization that the old man was her husband began to plunge her into sorrow.

"He is already resting, isn't he? What does he lack? Are there not servants for everything? What work does he have?" Mohana replied.

Dr Raghuraman laughed. "You are still at the age of play. You understand nothing, madam."

"The age of play! You understand nothing!" Mohana's mind now had still more work. The two phrases, "grown old" and "age of play," danced alternately within it.

The doctor said, "One must undertake activity suited to one's age. Life must correspond to the circulation of the blood. Iyer has strained his body greatly. That is the very cause of the fever. One needs strength to bear the burden of family life."

Mohana understood to some extent. When he was healthy, Iyer shaved his face every day so that grey hair would not spoil it. He walked briskly and dressed carefully, trying to erase the signs of age and spread the scent of youth. Confined to bed, he could not apply this "make-up." Old age became clearly visible to Mohana. Iyer too thought of it and suffered.

Dr Raghuraman had injected Mohana with the words, "You have grown old, you see." The injection began to work.

Someone told Mohana that she should light a sacred lamp at the goddess's temple for forty days to prolong her husband's life. She insisted on doing so. Iyer tried to stop her:

"Why all this, Mohana? These old women always say such things: light a lamp, observe a vow. The temple is not nearby. You must cross three streets. It will be difficult to go and light a lamp for forty days. We can ask a servant to do it."

He could not dissuade her. Carrying a basket of flowers, Mohana began going to worship the goddess. Devotees gathered along all three streets and at the temple for the sight of Mohana. Before the goddess's grace could reach her, the devotees offered her many kinds of gifts: the consecrated offering of their gaze and worship performed with tender eyes.

For forty days, this flavour gave Mohana a new kind of pleasure. Was Mohana a stone image like the goddess? She was a radiant woman.

Some days later, a Siddha physician prescribed a medicinal powder to promote fertility. Mohana refused it, but Iyer compelled her to take it. A hungry heart, eyes filled with fear, and limbs proud with youth: for such a woman, the powder did not aid conception. Instead, it began wasting her body. The lustre of her skin faded. Mohana became skin and bone.

Her suspicion grew strong. When Iyer was away, she took a packet of the powder and went to the doctor, telling him her fear. The doctor held her wrist and examined her pulse. He opened her eyes to see whether the blood circulated properly. There was abundant desire in those eyes, but little blood flow. He stroked her cheek and sighed.

They were in a private room. The doctor understood her illness. The rare medicine required was in his possession. He administered one "dose": he embraced and kissed her.

With parted lips and an astonished gaze, Mohana looked at the doctor and asked, "What outrage is this?" But there was no anger in her face or voice.

"From now on, Mohana, this alone is the medicine that will cure your illness," the doctor said fondly.

Pointing to the powder, he said, "This is mixed with datura. It will kill you slowly. To suppress your youthful feelings, your husband obtained it from some native physician. If you file a case saying he gave you medicine to kill you, he could receive ten years in prison."

She became terrified. "Poison? Did that sinful old man dare to kill me? Doctor, you alone must save me," she pleaded.

"From now on, I will never abandon you," the doctor said tenderly.

The compounder was given leave that day. A board saying "Doctor Out" was hung outside.

Iyer returned home exhausted. Mohana was nowhere to be seen. He searched the garden and asked at neighbouring houses. He could not find her. Fear arose. His attention turned to the doctor.

He hurried there. The moment he placed his foot on the threshold, laughter and amorous conversation from inside struck his ear like a harsh metallic sound. Anger urged him to enter. Shame and fear of what might happen told him, "Go away! You can no longer recover Mohana."

He stood frozen for a while. Then he heard Mohana's voice. Iyer ran home, ashamed, sweating, and bewildered.

She returned much later.

"Where were you all this time?" he asked with authority.

"At the doctor's house," she answered boldly.

He did not have the courage to ask why.

After that, this exchange began taking place every day. She continued to savour her feast of youth. What could the old man do?

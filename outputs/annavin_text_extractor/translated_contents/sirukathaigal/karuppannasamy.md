## English Translation

The bell rang.

The sound of a door opening was heard.

Karuppannasamy ran inside screaming and looked for a place to hide.

A musical laugh was heard.

Karuppannasamy became angry. “I am suffering, and at this time do you mock me as well?” he asked the goddess who appeared before him.

“Karuppanna, why this agitation? Why are you running?” she asked.

“Did the sound of the bell not fall upon your ears?” asked Karuppannasamy.

“It did. Why should hearing it cause fear? It sounds pleasant,” said the goddess.

“You too understand nothing. Some devotees must be coming,” he said fearfully.

“Fool! Why should the arrival of devotees frighten you? They come to worship you, light camphor, perform sixteen forms of homage, and offer food.

“Are you afraid that they will become angry and say, ‘Karuppannasamy, despite accepting so much worship, you do not remove our suffering’?” asked the goddess.

“Enough, Devi, do not torment me. I do not fear that they will rage because I failed to grant boons and remove their deficiencies. Do you think I lack experience administering a temple kingdom?

“Devotees will think that if they do not receive the benefit here, they will receive in the upper world the fruit of worshipping me. I know the doctrine by which they will not blame me for failing to remove their deficiencies here. That is not why I am afraid,” Karuppannasamy said so quickly that he became breathless.

The goddess hurried to the doorway, looked outside, and returned.

“Karuppanna, there are no devotees. A strong wind made the bell ring. Do not fear. But tell me why the arrival of devotees frightens you.”

Learning that no devotees had come, Karuppannasamy regained his courage. He sat upon his pedestal and began explaining to the goddess seated opposite.

“Devi, what do you know of the danger and embarrassment devotees have caused me? Day by day, my disgust for this ‘occupation’ grows.

“A man thinks he can hide his fraud in the smoke of half a measure of camphor and expects me to become his accomplice. Another expects me to assist his greed, and when asked why, says he has offered me a great feast.”

The goddess interrupted.

“What is new in that, Karuppannar? Have we not long seen and grown accustomed to such devotees?”

With a boiling heart, Karuppannasamy replied:

“Devotees no longer stop at that point, Devi. They humiliate me. They have reduced me to a condition in which I must live under police protection.”

“Humiliated you? Who?” the goddess asked in astonishment.

As if mocking her, Karuppannar repeated, “Who?”

“Do you imagine atheists humiliated me, Devi? Not they. They pass their time associating with human beings and attending to human problems.

“The people who humiliated me were devotees. Those very devotees who join their palms, strike their cheeks in reverence, and light camphor.

“Shame! Even thinking of it now makes me feel embarrassed. They humiliated me, and I was rescued with police assistance.”

The goddess could not contain her astonishment.

“Karuppannar, what are you saying? Devotees and police? Your words seem to connect two unrelated things.”

“Listen to what happened, Devi.

“I never asked these devotees to do particular acts for my pleasure or to offer particular foods. They come of their own accord and do whatever enters their minds. I remain quietly enduring everything.

“Seeing my patience and magnanimity, they acquired the courage to do anything they wished to me...”

Grief choked Karuppannar’s throat. The goddess became still more astonished.

“They acquired the courage to do what? Tell me, Karuppannar,” she urged.

“They placed me in a room and locked the door,” Karuppannasamy said furiously.

Even the goddess felt a slight shock.

“Locked you up? Devotees?” she asked in bewilderment.

“Merely hearing it frightens you, Devi. Imagine what my mind endured when they put me in a room and locked it.

“Am I some farmhand who can cry, ‘Master, master, let me out’? I am Karuppannaswamy, before whom they worship and ask for boons. Yet they shoved me into a room and locked the door. What could I do?”

Karuppannasamy became exhausted.

The goddess began to feel genuine sympathy.

“It is indeed a degrading condition. The devotees acted as though they were imprisoning you,” she said sadly.

“Devi, had the shouts they raised then reached your ears, you would know their conduct and nature.

“One crowed: ‘Lock him in and let us see what happens.’

“Another shouted: ‘Bring a large lock.’

“A third asked: ‘Do you want an Aligarh lock?’

“Such shouting, such daring.

“They cried, ‘Lock him in. Let us see who comes and what he can do,’ then placed me inside a large room, locked it, and left.

“Outside they laughed, saying, ‘Now let us see what happens.’ I remained shut inside while the sinners who locked me in laughed.

“Could I question them? Could I say, ‘What injustice is this? Release me, or I shall make blood flow from your noses and mouths and break your limbs’?

“They were devotees, and I was the god they worshipped. Think how my mind must have trembled, Devi.”

“Let that be for a moment, Karuppannar. Why did they lock you up? What had you done?” asked the goddess.

“What had I done? I had listened to and endured all the lies they spoke in my presence. That was my mistake.

“Had I rebuked even one devotee once, saying, ‘Enough, do not lie,’ they would not have acquired such courage that day.”

“Why did it become necessary to place you separately and lock the door?” the goddess asked again.

In a voice blending weariness and disgust, Karuppannar replied:

“You ask why they locked me up, Devi? I was supposedly their god. They locked me in so that some other devotees could not take me away. Such was their ‘devotion’ to me. They could not allow me even to speak with another devotee.”

“What a mad idea,” the goddess mocked.

“When these people eat everything they find, cooked or uncooked, ripe or unripe, develop diarrhoea, and come asking me, ‘Karuppannaswamy, save me,’ is that not madness, Devi? This was another madness of the same kind.

“To tell the truth, it was our mistake to allow such madness to grow.

“Listen to my case. These devotees developed the idea that I was their god and that they must not surrender me even if others claimed a right over me as theirs.

“Conditions arose to match that idea. When devotees multiplied, competing with one another, I too felt proud.

“My ill fortune! My multitudes of devotees divided into two factions and developed an unending hostility. Did I know their quarrel would become a great danger to me? I attended to my own occupation.

“They conducted my customary festival. I was delighted.

“Some fellows had been going about saying temple cars and festivals were useless. I feared slightly that my devotees might be influenced by their speech and fail to hold the festival that year.

“When they came forward to conduct it as usual, I rejoiced that no amount of propaganda had destroyed our influence.

“They hold my festival every year in the month of Vaikasi. Karuppa Udaiyar led the group of devotees conducting it.

“As usual, they took me in procession. I wished to say: ‘You fellows shouting about rationalism and self-respect, look at devotees celebrating my festival!’ But not one of those fellows could be seen. Perhaps they had gone somewhere to hold a conference.

“I came in procession happily. As always, they brought me to the festival hall and enthroned me there, a good arrangement for devotees to come and see me. I sat majestically.

“I told you my multitudes of devotees had become two factions. The festival was conducted by Karuppa Udaiyar’s group. The other was led by Picha Udaiyar, and they too were my devotees.

“The two groups were enemies, though neither had any reason to be hostile towards me.

“Karuppa’s group assembled to carry me from the hall back to the temple.

“‘Do not lift him! Do not take him!’ shouted Picha’s group.

“Karuppa’s devotees replied: ‘Who are you to stop us? We conduct the festival for our Karuppannasamy as we wish. The procession is finished and the ceremonial enthronement is over. We are taking him to the temple. Who are you to prevent it?’

“‘Do not touch him!’ Picha’s group warned.

“‘Lift him! Lift the god!’ Karuppa’s group ordered.

“‘If you take him outside, there will be killing.’

“‘Who are these tigers? Lift the god.’

“‘Do not bring needless trouble.’

“‘We shall carry Karuppannasamy away as we please.’

“‘Karuppannasamy is our god.’

“‘No, he is our god.’

“‘I shall cut off your hand.’

“‘We shall break your leg.’

“Devi, the two factions roared at one another while I sat enthroned in the hall.

“One group tried to carry me back and restore me to the temple. The other blocked them. What could I do? Both sides were my devotees. Which side could I join?

“I thought: ‘Let them fight. I shall go away to the temple. I need the favour of neither group.’ But how could I go there?

“I suffered and stood bewildered, Devi.

“In rural places, when a marriage dispute arises, one crowd shouts, ‘Bring the bride,’ while another cries, ‘Do not take the bride.’ The woman trapped in the quarrel between the two groups laments.

“My condition became like hers. But could I lament? I was a god, and those subjecting me to this cruelty were the devotees who worshipped me. What could I do?

“Karuppa’s group kept saying: ‘It is only right to carry the deity to the temple and install him there.’

“Picha’s group replied: ‘Settle the dispute first, then touch the god. Until the matter is resolved, we shall not permit it.’

“Sinners! If you had a dispute among yourselves, why ruin me for it? Could you not let me return to the temple and then discuss and settle your affair? Was it right to humiliate me in this manner?

“I wanted to ask them, but how could I?

“Meanwhile, the talk around the village became unbearable.

“‘Has the god still not departed?’

“‘No. They supposedly say they will not release the god.’

“‘Why? Who says so?’

“‘Picha’s people.’

“‘Why?’

“‘There is some dispute. They say settle it with Karuppaiah before touching the deity, otherwise they will not release him.’

“‘Is the god still in the hall?’

“‘Yes, poor thing, still in the hall.’

“‘He should have reached the temple by now.’

“‘Yes, if only they released him.’

“‘If they are fighting, what did the poor god do? Must they keep him under guard in the hall?’

“Thus the mothers spoke.

“The children shouted, ‘Hey, the god has been caught in the hall!’

“Devi, the two groups apparently had a dispute over temple administration. For that, they reduced me to this appearance.

“Picha’s group categorically declared that I must not be removed from the hall until the temple dispute was resolved.

“‘Until the accounts are settled, we shall not allow Karuppannasamy to be taken to the temple,’ they said.

“Then, Devi, they left me inside the hall, pulled the door shut, locked it, and went away.

“I remained confined inside. The hall had been locked. Had I not desired a festival, I could have remained peacefully in the temple. Now I had been locked inside the hall.

“‘Poor Karuppannasamy! They have locked him in,’ mothers said with hands upon their chins.

“I was imprisoned inside.

“Karuppa’s devotees, who had placed me in this condition, made great efforts to rescue me. They petitioned the temple board, saying: ‘They have locked the god in the hall. There is no deity in the temple. They must bring the god out and hand him over.’

“Are board members there for this? They exist to inspect whether accounts are correct. They scarcely have enough time to perform even that work properly. Would they run merely because someone locked me up?

“Finally, the Lalgudi Police Sub-Inspector arrived with a police force, broke the lock, and released me.

“Did they leave that good man alone after he came to render this service?

“They surrounded him, shouting: ‘How can you break the lock? Watch what we do. He is our Karuppannasamy. We lock him up, confine him, and do as we wish. Who are you to question us?’

“Was the officer like me, forced to remain with his mouth closed? He told them to behave respectfully and obey the law. They did not listen.

“He called the police and ordered, ‘Give them their worship.’ The police raised their batons, struck them, and drove them away.

“Only then did my mind find some peace, Devi.

“People calling themselves devotees, striking their cheeks before me and lighting camphor, locked me in without considering justice, showing pity, fearing guilt or sin, or asking whether they might reduce a god to such disorder. They humiliated me.

“What could I do? I lay grieving, wondering which virtuous person would release me, how long I must remain confined, and who might be mocking me.

“By good fortune, the man from Lalgudi came. A man of gold. May his children live happily. He brought me out and restored me.

“Devi, these people who call themselves devotees subjected me to such suffering. That is why I became afraid.

“I tell you truly: there is no use trusting devotees any longer. There is no meaning in swelling with pride because they perform worship. We no longer need their favour. I feel we should have no association with them at all.”

Karuppannasamy finished his story. The goddess too was disturbed by hearing it.

“Yes. We must no longer trust these devotees,” she ruled.

“Is it enough for only the two of us to decide, Devi? Is not our company large? We must explain it to everyone and pass a resolution saying, ‘No more devotees,’ so that none of us again falls into their hands and suffers degradation,” said Karuppannasamy.

“Yes, Karuppannar. We must explain the degradation our people suffer through devotees and how tricksters use us as instruments to deceive fools. We must ensure our friends do not encounter such trouble again.

“We must convene a special conference on all this. It can no longer be endured. I shall chair the reception committee, and you conduct the opening ceremony. Whom shall we invite to preside?” the goddess asked enthusiastically.

“Whom shall we invite?” Karuppannasamy too began to ponder.

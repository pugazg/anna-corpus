## English Translation

"Mother! Mother!"

"Why are you taking my life? Go away to school!"

"I am going to school, but..."

"Call your elder sister and ask her to look for an omen. I have work."

"Not an omen, Mother. I want a silk shirt."

"A silk shirt? Of course, child! Your father is Kubera himself. Why one shirt? He will have nine stitched for you!"

"Velayudham, Mother, in the last house, wears one like it."

"If you want a shirt like his, your father must know how to cheat the town as his father does."

"It looks beautiful."

"What does? The shirt, of course. He himself looks like a piece of charcoal. But you, darling, look like a king."

"Go away, Mother! Must a king wear a patched shirt like this? They make fun of me."

"Make fun of you? Who did it, that imp Velayudham? Tell him, 'Yes, my boy! If my father too obtains a widow's property somewhere, I shall wear silk.' That monkey dares mock you!"

"What is widow's property?"

"Darling, you know Velayudham's mother. She lost her husband and then ran away with another man. She possessed property, so that caste-degraded man married her for the money. Their son is this Velu. They have money, but do you know that the caste has expelled them?"

"Being expelled does not seem to have caused them any loss, Mother."

"You do not understand, my king. If a wedding or ceremony occurs in one of our homes, we do not invite them. We must not even drink water in their house."

"Or eat the sweets made there?"

"Why, you curse! Did that fellow give you some sweet?"

"Would he give me any? Mother, I asked repeatedly, but he refused."

"Good that he did not."

"Did I leave it at that? I struck it from his hand. It fell, and I popped it into my mouth."

"Alas! Karma, karma! Why commit such pollution? If you eat something made in that afflicted household, what becomes of our clan and honour? If our relatives hear..."

"I do not know what they would say, Mother. But if they tasted that sweet, they would never give it up."

"Enough. Do not become a glutton. Never do such things again. Look, we are high-caste Saiva Mudaliars. Velayudham's people belong to a lower caste. We must not eat what they touch."

Widow. A woman who ran away. Caste-degraded man. Curse. Karma. Pollution. Affliction. Clan. Honour. High caste. Low caste.

A mother teaches her son all these "lessons" at home, not at school. Later, at school, the son must study a different set of lessons:

Looking for omens is unnecessary and meaningless. Caste distinction must not exist. Widow remarriage is a worthy reform. Everyone belongs to one human clan and must live like brothers.

When home lessons and school lessons meet in this fashion, consider whether a student's mind gains clarity or confusion. He must struggle like a runner racing on slippery ground.

Now let us look inside Velayudham's home.

"Velu! Why do you take two pieces of Mysore pak to school every time?"

"Enough, Sowbhagyam. If not for the child, for whom is Mysore pak?" his father said.

"That is not the point. Even Mysore pak requires a limit. He will develop stomach pain. The doctor has said sweets give him a stomach ache."

"Nothing of the kind. Give him a small measure of milk before bed, and all will be well. What medicine can equal the virtue contained in milk?"

"Velu, your father has joined your side. From now on, take half a dozen each time."

"Go away, Mother! Do I eat both pieces myself?"

"Why? Do you distribute charity instead?"

"No, Mother. The boy in the last house, the red-faced Singaram..."

"What does he want with Mysore pak? Naturally, what else can he do? His father is a great lord, is he not?"

"Who is this Singaram? The son of Mahadeva Mudali, who writes accounts in a grocery shop?"

"Yes, Father. As though that wretched thing lacked only Mysore pak! Velu apparently gives it to him."

"No, Mother! I do not give it. He snatches it."

"Did you hear this injustice? Destitution runs wild. He snatches another boy's sweets!"

"Let it go. He is a little boy," said the father.

"A little boy? Fine justice you speak! If he has a thieving mind while still so small, he will plunder the whole town when grown. You call him poor and say, 'Alas, pity him.' Listen to me: give such people a little room and they climb onto your head. How can they acquire good sense or conduct?

"Velu, why do you associate with that dried-head fellow? Why befriend such people? They wander for want of gruel and become friendly only because they expect this or that from you."

"Singaram is a good boy, Mother."

"What is good about him? What is our standing and worth? Does sitting together at school make everyone equal? Do you know his father's work? He is a clerk in a shop. You must not roam about treating that boy as your equal."

"No, Mother."

"If anyone sees you, they will mock: 'Why does Velayudham roam with that empty-handed fellow Singaram?' Do not associate with him again."

"All right, Mother."

Thus Velayudham learned from his mother at home the nature of wealth and poverty, the inequality between them, and the rule that the rich must not treat the poor as equals because it diminishes their honour.

Then he went to school and studied proverbs and lessons of another kind:

Wealth does not endure. The tears shed by the poor have power. Do not despise a person for being poor.

He learned that the great division between poverty and privilege ruins the world, that the wealthy acquired their riches by cheating the poor of their property, and that the distinction between rich and poor produced great revolutions in history.

The home lesson taught him a furrowed brow, arrogant speech and a haughty gaze. The school lesson taught something else. Velayudham's mind had to grow amid this contradiction. How could such growth be healthy?

At school the lesson itself was the same for everyone. There was no separate lesson for a poor child and another for the rich. Yet one could certainly see a difference in the teacher's method, the weapon he used, the words he applied and the expressions on his face.

Let us enter the classroom for a moment.

"What are the people of Africa called?"

"Negroes, sir," answered Velayudham.

"Sit down, Velu. Singaram, what is the colour of Negroes?"

"Black, sir."

"Correct. Sit. Muniya, what rivers are there in Africa?"

Before Muniyan could answer, someone began crying. Velayudham was weeping. The classroom erupted, and the teacher forgot the rivers of Africa.

"Velu! Why are you crying?"

"Nothing, sir."

"Why cry over nothing? Singaram, did you make mischief?"

"No, sir."

"Donkey! You grow worse every day. Climb onto the bench."

"Why, sir?"

"Climb when I tell you. Worthless, destitute fellow! You ought to go to the shop where your father works and carry sacks. See how he stands like Bhima."

"What wrong did I do, sir?"

"Crocodile tears! Stand on the bench. Velayudham, why did you cry?"

"How would I know? Ask him."

"Sir, Singaram mocked me by calling me a Negro."

"See! I knew it immediately. You are a mischievous tail, Singaram. He would not cry unless you had done something."

"Velayudham is lying, sir."

"Ah, here stands Harishchandra himself! Muniyan, strike him three times on the head. Singaram's head, boy! Yes, enough. Sit down. You will not call Velu a Negro again, will you?"

"Ask whether I called him one even now, sir."

"Say it with your mouth."

"Sir, when you asked what colour a Negro was, he looked at me and laughed. Then all the boys looked at me mockingly."

"See, rascal! You are a troublemaker. Singaram, do you think you alone are beautiful because you are so red? Stout fellow, even a monkey's face is red. Is black skin something to mock? Lord Krishna himself is black."

The class ended. Later the teacher encountered Singaram alone and gave him another lesson:

"Come here. You are a boy capable of studying somewhat intelligently. Why invite useless trouble? You know who Velayudham is. His father is wealthy and influential; he is even a trustee of this school. Should you mock his son as a Negro?

"Even if Velayudham is as black as one, he possesses plenty of money. You look like a king in a stage drama, but what use is that? Your father barely makes a living, and you pay only half fees. You must behave humbly, or you will lose that concession. Then you will have to stand guard at some shop.

"Velayudham's position is different. Whether he learns or not makes no difference. He has property and influence. Must he study and obtain employment merely to live? Your fate is different.

"If you quarrel with that boy, he will stir up his father. His father will leap in here, and before he says 'sesame,' the headmaster will become oil. Your half-fee concession will vanish. Your poor father struggles so hard to educate you. You are poor, and Velayudham is rich. Should you not act carefully around him?

"Go. Do not do such things again. Find a way to survive; do not ripen prematurely."

The poor must bow before the rich. This was the teacher's lesson outside the classroom.

Inside, he had taught another lesson, even that rich men were sinners. Reading elegantly from some book, he explained that people with wealth had eyes yet were blind, mouths yet were mute, and ears yet were deaf. The same teacher taught the practical way to survive outside class.

Which of these two lessons would Singaram accept?

Velayudham and Singaram met on the playing field. Velayudham ate Mysore pak. Singaram had to spit onto the ground the saliva gathering in his mouth rather than swallow it.

"Why do you spit at me?" Velayudham demanded in an authoritative tone.

Singaram spat once more and said, "Must I ask your permission even to spit?"

"What dog can do anything to me?"

"What do I care which donkey it is, even if sacks of money are piled on its back?"

"Who are you calling a donkey?"

"Who is the dog?"

"Empty-handed fellow!"

"Go away, low-caste fellow!"

"You are the son of a sack carrier!"

"And you are the son of a widow!"

"Sir! Sir!" Velayudham screamed.

The physical-training teacher arrived, threatening, "What is it?"

"Sir, he insulted me."

"Who?"

"Singaram, sir."

"What did he call you?"

"A widow's son."

"Who? You?"

That was the entire inquiry. Punishment began at once and rapidly. Singaram's back reddened; his face and eyes reddened too.

That redness never left him. Singaram grew into a young man and did not change. He contested an election against Velayudham and lost, but did not change. He received a prison sentence because of charges fabricated by Velayudham, but did not change.

He was released and returned outside. At a public meeting, Velayudham delivered propaganda for *samadharma*, an egalitarian socialist order.

On hearing him, Singaram's face turned pale with fear: now that socialism had obtained such a preacher, how could that ideal ever rise and flourish?

8 September 1946

## Translator's Notes

The source uses period racial terminology in a classroom lesson and then exposes how colour prejudice, caste hierarchy and wealth influence punishment. The historical term is retained only inside that quoted context. Casteist insults and descriptions are translated as the prejudiced characters' speech, not endorsed language. “சமதர்மம்” is rendered as an egalitarian socialist order in keeping with the story's economic and political argument.

## English Translation

A garden of flowers and an enchanting palace. Electric lights of many kinds shine as brightly as day. Security forces stand guard.

The king sits enthroned in ceremonial splendour. Ministers and chief officials behold his state and drown in an ocean of delight. Women dance on one side. Elsewhere the sound of the veena can be heard. Music sweet as nectar to the ear captures the listeners' hearts.

People greet the king and bow to him. They praise him and offer hymns of adoration. They present gifts and make offerings. The king's face blossoms. Seeing it, joy dances in the eyes of everyone else.

Zari-bordered turbans; costly clothes and ornaments in Western fashion; diamond rings that make the fingers appear to carry lamps. Guests continue arriving for the celebration in many kinds of cars.

A feast, fireworks, arrangements of lights, welcomes and hospitality suited to each person's status, and messages of greeting: everything presents a spectacle that fills the eye.

Chamundeshwari receives complete ceremonial adornment and special worship from the king. He ensures that charity and donations are properly given to the priests. The king is entranced by Chamundi's power, and the people watching are transported with devotion.

At Mysore during the Dasara festival, the Maharaja of Mysore and the others would have immersed themselves in revelry of this kind.

Those assembled in the palace would not have known the torment of poverty or the terror of hunger. The enthroned king would not have known that the town was filled with people whose heads were parched, backs bent, stomachs shrivelled, steps weary, bodies wasted, and hearts broken.

Nor would this have been known to the ministers who retained their posts by crying acclamation before him, or to the other wealthy dignitaries who came to delight in the king's radiant pageantry.

Chamundeshwari could hardly be expected to care as much about protecting the people as she did about protecting the king. How could priests who argued on the king's behalf and obtained the Mother's grace for him undertake the task of pleading for the common people?

That responsibility belongs to the king, not to the Mother or the priest, if he is truly a king who follows the people.

Tippayya was the head of a family. He lived in Lakshmi Agraharam in Chamarajpet. He was thirty-five years old and worked as a peon in the Health Department.

His wife's name was Lingamma. She worked in another person's house, washing vessels, laundering saris, and cleaning the home.

Their elder daughter, also named Lingamma, was ten years old. Devamma was a small child not yet sixteen months old.

Even though husband and wife accepted whatever work they could find, Tippayya could not bear the family's burden. Their income was insufficient to sustain life.

Living under such conditions no longer seemed desirable to Tippayya. One day he took the two girls to a local tank and entered the water with them.

That evening, three bodies floated in the tank. The police conducted an inquiry. The panchayat members ruled that it was suicide.

The mother said, "We could not bear the poverty. There was no means to protect them. My husband killed himself."

What anguish must have torn Tippayya's heart when his ten-year-old daughter cried in alarm, "I am hungry," and when the little child announced her hunger in baby speech?

A child's prattle can be sweeter than the sound of the yazh and flute. Yes, but poor people in Tippayya's condition are an exception. It brought no delight to him. It only multiplied sorrow and increased pain.

Lingamma now had only one source of anguish: the fact that she herself remained alive.

People must compare the scene of the king enthroned in the palace with the condition of the many whose lives disintegrate in mud huts. They will recognize that the former is a spectacle that fills the eye, while the latter turns the eyes into pools of tears.

Must these two scenes continue to exist in this country?

Is it the duty of good people merely to watch forever as bearers of crowns sit enthroned, while most of the people fall prey to the disease of hunger and enduring misery drives them to the point of suicide?

Must the freedom obtained and the liberation won serve only as instruments that give life to the worthless?

The first duty of those seated upon the throne of authority is to reshape the country into one fit for everyone to live in. They must provide a good life to the many that prevents the suicide attempts of people like Tippayya and thereby reduces such killing.

If rulers remain indifferent even after seeing these two scenes, the public has an enduring duty: follow a just method, issue those rulers a permit to attend to some other occupation, and send them away.

This too is revolution. Through public awakening and with the aid of the right to vote, it is possible in this way to establish a new society upon the earth.

Victory to the revolution! Long live society!

17 October 1948

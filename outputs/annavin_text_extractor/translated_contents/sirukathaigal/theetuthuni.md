## English Translation

The summer sun’s fury made it impossible to show one’s head outside. Dust clung to the ground like molten lead. A dry wind from somewhere seemed to bring fire and plaster it across the face. The heat scorched both man and mountain.

In that burning sun, she beat herself alternately upon breast and stomach.

“Alas, elder brother! Have you no eyes, God? Father! Mother!”

Those were the only words that burst from her heart.

Her ten-year-old son returned from school and saw his mother beating herself and crying in disarray. Understanding nothing, he asked, “Why are you crying, Mother?” and began sobbing too.

At the sight of her child, her arms and legs failed. She stared at him like a woman possessed. The next instant she gathered all her love into both arms, clasped him tightly and wailed. What must she have felt upon seeing the boy who looked as though her husband had been peeled and set down anew?

The child did not stop crying.

“What is it, Mother? Will you not tell me?” Then, suddenly remembering something, he asked, “Where is Father?”

Those two words poured oil upon the fire consuming Mariammal.

For one second she was silent. In the next came a terrible cry: “My child... your father!”

Unable to restrain the storm of sorrow rising from her lips, and unable to utter another word, she screamed “Alas!” and collapsed.

Hearing the unexpected cry, people from the street gathered in Mariammal’s house. Some women, standing as though they had trodden upon a stupefying herb, lifted, comforted and revived her. She could not remain silent before so many questioning voices. When she raised her head and saw their running noses and agitation, the furnace within her blazed higher.

She managed one sentence:

“What can I tell you, sister? Him... the Iyer!”

Then, as though seized by a spirit, she flew towards the agraharam with her hair loose. Many from the crowd ran after her.

* * *

Anthonisami came from Kalaiyur village. His father, Arokiasami, had earned respect and influence among all eighteen oppressed castes of the village.

How? He spoke powerfully and possessed some education; he could sign his name without difficulty. The “great weapon” by which he spread his gift of speech through the region was direct truth and perfect honesty. A Dravidian who would give his life to support anyone seeking refuge, he was a tireless labourer. Anthonisami had never needed to worry while his father remained the storehouse that carried the son’s cares.

But nature and the age itself could not tolerate one among the excluded eating a full stomach three times a day. Arokiasami was struck by disease and died within a short time.

While his father lived, Anthonisami had done what he was told, eaten what was set before him and moved through life as a cherished son. Now managing his circumstances became extremely difficult. For a few months he sustained his wife and child somehow with his father’s savings.

How long could a Pallan, Paraiyan, Thotti or Thomban sit and eat? Making a living by deception was a privilege reserved for the Aryan-Dravidian class. He wanted no cruel occupation that lived by sucking another’s blood. What remained?

He entered one of the occupations imposed by the conspirators of old: work that slowly rubbed the body away while requiring a smiling face. He became a farm servant to a Brahmin, and became a slave.

Though Anthonisami lacked the village influence his father had enjoyed, he caused not the smallest blemish to his father’s fame. It only grew. How satisfied and proud he was of that! Apart from sharing his father’s truthfulness, Anthonisami possessed one special quality: even at the cost of life, he would not beg for food.

Perhaps for that reason, within six months of beginning work everyone from labourer to proprietor loved and valued him. If the Iyer himself rejoiced in the man’s honesty, truth, humility and generosity, and in the fact that he did not take even one paisa of idle wages, need one ask how strangers praised him?

From the day Anthonisami pawned himself to the Iyer’s household, the Iyer ceased stepping into his fields. In earlier days the poor Iyer’s ankles had ached as he leaned upon a palm-leaf umbrella, and if he missed a bund his foot did not fail to slip. Now he could “rest.” He stopped even driving his son out of bed. The worry of bathing the milch cow at dawn ended too. From the first release of water until the grain reached the threshing floor, everything became Anthonisami’s responsibility.

The Iyer thought of him as a Ford that required little petrol. With the “steering wheel” of speech, he could turn him in whatever direction he wished.

Whenever guests arrived, the Iyer immediately took them to the fields. Neighbouring lands did not escape his sweeping gaze. Did he point to those fields and use the pavilion of his mouth to secure a bridegroom for his daughter? Only the Iyer knew. One thing was certain: he never told guests that the one and a half veli he displayed was leased land.

When guests came, even Anthonisami’s half-stomach of gruel sometimes acquired dirt. Until they departed, the Iyer made him dance endlessly: “Bring that, carry this, go there, call Vasu, has Subbu arrived?” The strain nearly snapped the man’s marriage thread. At such times he thought even the transplanting season, when he kept his eyes open night and day, was far better.

He was the slave. Had not the Iyer been born upon earth to command and Anthonisami to submit? When would this arrogance and insolence subside? The word falters. In which age?

And why speak only of the Iyer? What master of any caste is ever completely satisfied with a Pallan or Paraiyan’s work, or shows heartfelt affection? If that is false, then the number of such men truly existing equals the number of breaches the Kodamurutti river makes in summer. The Pallan and Paraiyan alone must bow obediently to duty.

What salary, or rather wage, did Subramania Iyer give Anthonisami? Nine kalams of paddy a year and two marakkals of grain every three days.

And his body? Compare Subramania Iyer, who ate at least four times a day until sour belches rose, perhaps that was why he consumed special asafoetida, with Anthonisami, who sometimes stood and scooped gruel from a pot into his mouth, drank only twice a day and ran back to work.

From the day a murderer or daylight robber enters prison until the hour of release, officials weigh him to ensure his body loses no mass. But Anthonisami? His class? From the day a boy old enough to understand takes up the cattle-goad and herding stick, through manhood when they change form into farm work and guarding, has there been anyone even to weigh him? Forgive me, has there been anyone to offer so much as one nominal word of consideration?

* * *

The paddy harvest ended. The earthly life of the Iyer’s mother ended too. Anthonisami remained close to the old woman and skilfully constructed her bier. The corpse mounted the pyre.

The next day, as usual, he rose when the cock crowed, made a full round of the samba fields and completed all his morning duties for Subramania Iyer. At about eleven, he came to the cattle shed, hands folded and body bent, seeking the master’s permission to go drink his morning gruel.

The Iyer had begun holding his nose in ritual observance before dawn. A little before half past ten, he broke it with snacks, his “interval meal,” and sat cross-legged on the veranda before a tray of betel leaves. A thin cloth barely touched his shoulder. Tender betel and areca nut filled his mouth. After his meal he resembled a temple bull chewing the cud.

Nearby, his beloved, no, his wife, extended a folded betel leaf. Her sidelong glance and small smile drew the Iyer towards an incident from another day. He drank deeply from some awkward pleasure even he did not understand.

It was at that charming moment that Anthonisami was blessed with the Iyer’s vision. What great fortune!

The Iyer happened to turn his smiling face to one side.

What a sudden change! Not even a moment had passed before that lovely face darkened. The enchanting smile became a spear striking the heart; the shining eye a piercing arrow. Sesame and horse gram seemed to crackle upon his forehead. The figure standing in the mud shook his entire body.

“Hey, Anthoni!” the Iyer called.

No Brahmin, indeed no non-Brahmin master either, calls an Adi-Dravidian bearing that or any other beautiful name by the whole of it. Why? Look at the final word in Anthonisami’s name: “sami,” lord.

“Sami,” came Anthonisami’s lifeless reply. For some reason his body trembled.

In a slightly lowered voice, the Iyer asked, “What did you do yesterday?”

Anthonisami’s trembling did not stop.

“Sami, when the cock called I went and saw the fields, returned and completed the master’s household work. Only after that came Mother’s funeral.”

The Iyer sneered.

“Anthoni! I did not ask for an account of your work. Look here, the thing you took at the cremation ground yesterday: you have it, do you not?”

Anthonisami looked up. “Sami!” he said, staring blankly and uttering nothing more.

There was silence for a while.

Growing angrier, the Iyer said, “Why do you gape? Do you not understand the language? I am asking about the thing you stole at the cremation ground yesterday.”

“You stole it at the cremation ground.”

Those words became a heated spear and arrow, striking Anthonisami’s lower belly in turn. Life drained from him; every limb seemed separately to contract and turn black.

For the first time in his life, and it would be the last, he heard such words. And from the mouth of Subramania Iyer, the man who squeezed his body handful by handful and consumed it!

“I am asking you! Will pearls fall from your mouth if you answer? Such arrogance!”

“Sami, I do not understand what you are saying.”

“Shut your mouth, dog! Do you hope to escape through pretence? A Paraiyan’s cunning will not work here. If you do not return the sari you took, the skin on your body...”

“Alas, Sami! I did not take the sari.”

“I saw it in your hand with my own two eyes! It appears you will swallow a man alive. So am I the liar?”

“Sami, I did not take it. I swear upon the mother who was burning there. For two years I have lived drinking the water you poured for me. Would I suddenly steal Mother’s sari now?”

“Control yourself, donkey! Will you bring it now or not?”

“Alas, Sami, I never took it.”

“You did not take it at all? See what comes of asking you respectfully. That itself is my mistake. Look here. The sari will come here by itself.”

The next minute Anthonisami’s hands were bound tightly behind him with rope to the odhiya tree in the cattle shed.

By chance Savariyar passed through the agraharam at that time. Seeing Anthonisami tied up, he panicked, ran through the back without anyone noticing, and whispered the incident into Mariammal’s ear.

* * *

Mariammal reached the agraharam with her hair loose and saw her husband bound to the tree as though he too were wood.

Alas, her grief!

“He has not even drunk his morning gruel!”

How much tender love lay in that one sentence. Had she not torn each word from her lips and strung them together?

What a dreadful sight! Mariammal lay at Anthonisami’s feet, biting the earth. Pitiful!

His son cried “Father! Father!” His wife lay breathless. Anthonisami stared fixedly at both, yet not one drop of tears appeared. Only his face had died. Why? Was the sword of humiliation sawing through his heart?

Was Subramania Iyer, watching a sight that would melt stone, human?

He summoned several Iyers to strengthen his case and a few Dravidians favourable to him. Pillai arrived and sat upon the seat the “holy men” showed him. Anthonisami was untied and placed in the dock.

Pillai did not retreat even slightly from the questions or manner employed by the holy men. But his roar, arrogance, mockery and authority possessed their own swagger. As though the suffering already inflicted were insufficient, Pillai questioned Anthonisami while striking him with his “sceptre,” a slim cane.

Through all that torture, the innocent man spoke only one word: “No.”

Poor Iyer! His scheme did not succeed, and he was disappointed. But would Pillai leave without satisfying the holy man? Otherwise, what benefit had he gained from the exalted seat granted him in the agraharam?

Pillai pronounced his stern judgment:

“By thirty nazhigais tomorrow, the next day at noon, the sari must reach the Iyer’s house from wherever you find it. I do not care what you do.”

* * *

Early the next morning, the lord of burning rays shook golden beams from his body. They advanced gently, giving their light to remove the darkness. From the southern end of the street came the wandering song of the curd-seller, “Curd! Curd!” drawing everyone towards the previous day’s rice. The cowherd’s call, “Here, take your straw,” as he laid down the morning fodder seemed to tap us awake too.

“Amma!” called a voice, followed by a knock at Subramania Iyer’s door.

“Who is it?” Amma asked as she came.

Marimuthu, who had taken away the “defiled clothes” the previous day, had removed their ritual impurity and brought them back.

“Here they are, Amma,” he said, setting a small bundle upon the veranda.

She hurriedly untied it. The Iyer came from inside asking, “What is it?” and sat on the veranda. Amma took out each folded garment, and he checked them.

One was his wife’s old sari. Another was her blouse. The third?

It was the defiled cloth Anthonisami had supposedly “stolen.”

Had the Iyer not thundered, “I saw you take it with my own eyes”? Had they not tied to a tree and beaten Anthoni, who laboured until his chest broke?

The very old sari whose theft they blamed upon and punished that poor man for had now been washed and returned by Marimuthu.

The god of justice did not awaken.

12 January 1947

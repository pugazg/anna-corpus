## English Translation

"It is only a sorghum field, isn't it? Three-quarters of an acre belonging to your father."

"Yes, sir. It used to be three acres. We lost it when Mother fell ill."

"And your mother too passed away, did she not?"

"It has been three years since Mother died. Father has lain about like a man possessed ever since."

"All right, all right. Now, against three-quarters of an acre, you ask for a loan of three hundred rupees. How can I give it?"

"I will labour hard, tighten my stomach and mouth, save the money, and repay the debt, sir."

"Look at the fellow who promises to repay! The moment you marry, you will produce children one after another. Your family will grow. How will any money remain?"

"No, sir. They say the good fortune brought by the bride will make our family prosper."

"Has someone read your horoscope? Who, that priest? Fine, go and ruin yourself. They say one must not utter the inauspicious word 'no' when a man asks for a wedding loan. Come after the new moon. I will give it. Bring someone to execute the deed as well."

"Pandari Iyer will come, sir. I will bring him."

The reason Sokkan had resolved to mortgage the sorghum field, borrow money, and marry Sornam was that Pattalam, who had accumulated money, had cast an eye on her.

"Sister said uncle is coming from Madras city."

"Let him come! Does Sornam expect me to beat a drum throughout the village and announce, 'The king is coming! The king is coming!'?"

Such talk with Sornam's younger brother often made Sokkan angry.

The ox accepted the task of enduring that anger along with its work of ploughing. Sokkan spoke dismissively, saying, "Let him come," but inwardly he was afraid. Every time Pattalam visited, he arrived in a different new outfit. When he came the previous year, he wore a gold-plated watch; a ring glittered on his finger; the usual cigarette hung from his mouth; and mischief filled his speech.

"Sokko! From a distance, I couldn't tell whether that was you standing there or the scarecrow in your field!"

"Why are your eyes so clouded? Isn't there an eye hospital in the city? Go and have them examined."

"You make jokes, fellow! You are a man accustomed to cattle. How could your mind be any different?"

"I know your kind. Be quiet! Plenty of men who roam with stray dogs speak this way as though they were kings ruling the nation. Go! Go!"

On some occasions, a quarrel would flare up in this manner. Pattalam would not prolong it. When even the ox trembled at Sokkan's blows, he feared what might become of his own back.

Sometimes Sokkan got an opportunity to accompany Sornam to the marketplace. He felt nothing but joy and pride. Yet were all who watched them decent people? Many kinds of comments fell upon their ears:

"The fellow has sprinkled some love powder over her."

"The girl is like a parrot, and he stands beside her like a brute."

"He must have promised to build her a two-storey house if she marries him."

"Look at the face that is going to build a two-storey house!"

"Look at the girl, walking as if some great prince were accompanying her."

Sornam would suppress her anger.

Only one thought would arise in Sokkan: he must live in a way that made Sornam proud. He must conduct himself so that the townspeople praised him, and Sornam must feel pride when she heard the whole village praise him.

He obtained the loan and sent a village elder to Sornam's father to ask for her hand. Her father yielded not even an inch.

"He has even mortgaged his sorghum field! Who would entrust a daughter to him? My heart will not permit it. Besides, Pattalam seems to have the idea of marrying our girl," the old man said.

The village elder pointed out that although Pattalam had earned some money in the city, people said his conduct was improper and that he was involved with another woman. Sornam's father replied firmly, "Have not so many wild fellows settled down as soon as the marriage tether was tied? Pattalam will be the same."

At precisely this time, Sokkan's father disappeared without telling anyone. Searching for him and pining for Sornam became Sokkan's only occupations. With the money borrowed for the wedding, he bought another small field. A legal dispute sprouted from it. The new field dragged the old field away with it. Sokkan became a hired ploughman.

Elagiri, who had lent the money and taken the field mound through a written deed, employed Sokkan as a general helper in his house when he was not tending Elagiri's land.

Elagiri's only daughter, Kanimozhi, was a young widow. Educated and cultured, she had been brokenhearted by her fate. Yet she resolved to spend the remainder of her life in service. She established a free clinic and, after learning first aid, worked there with its employed physician, Dr Kamala.

At times, Sokkan wondered whether he might tell the lady doctor or his mistress about Sornam and ask for help. Immediately, a feeling of wounded pride intervened and drove the thought away.

Sokkan found employment as an attendant in the clinic and accepted it eagerly. Outside working hours, he enjoyed talking with the doctor. He turned the clinic into a school where he learned about the state of the world.

Dr Kamala's husband served in the Indian Air Force. Whenever the conversation turned to him, Sokkan learned about war, the armed forces, defence, and the duty to protect the country. An aspiration began to bud in his mind: why not join the force that defended the nation?

China, which had spoken affectionately of "Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai" and Panchsheel, attacked India. When that news arrived, the thought budding in Sokkan's mind began to flower. What made him decide to enlist were Dr Kamala's tears and Kanimozhi's smile.

A telegram arrived. On reading it, Dr Kamala struck her face and wailed uncontrollably.

"Sokka! My husband... dead!" she sobbed.

Sokkan was shaken. He did not know how to console the doctor. Tears welled in his own eyes.

He gave his life for the country.

He died a hero's death.

His name will endure.

The whole nation praises that warrior.

The more Sokkan thought in this way, the firmer became his resolve to join the armed forces.

He decided to inform Elagiri, obtain a letter of recommendation from the doctor, and travel to Madras to enlist. He went to Elagiri's house. What he saw and heard there moved his heart.

Elagiri stood shedding tears. Before him, Kanimozhi smiled.

"Father, when grave danger comes to the country, what will become of it if even informed people like us do not rise with courage? I had believed mine was a useless life. Now I have received a good opportunity to serve the country. Do not be distressed. Send me away joyfully. There is no service purer than caring for noble men wounded in battle and struggling for life."

Sokkan heard her speak in a way that melted the hearts of all who listened. After that, could even a trace of hesitation about enlisting arise in him? Sokkan became a soldier.

Many people like Sokkan, Dr Kamala, and Kanimozhi set out to defend the country. Unable to stand before the great awakening they created, the advancing Chinese army withdrew. The war ended. Dr Kamala and Kanimozhi returned home. Sokkan remained in the army.

Time dissolved with fierce speed, like the heart of a debtor. Pakistan entered Kashmir. Sokkan was among the valiant troops who set out to repel the attack.

Through the newspapers, the doctor and Kanimozhi learned that Indian forces were advancing from victory to victory and destroying Pakistani positions. They proudly thought, "Our Sokkan will be there."

They wished to go and serve now, just as they had during the Chinese invasion, and were attending to the arrangements. Then came the news that Sokkan, after showing courage and achieving victory in a battle to strike and destroy enemy targets, had died in the field. Tears rolled down, but heads were held high.

"Our village Sokkan has died in the war! The government has praised him as a great warrior, a man of courage, and a martyr," the villagers said.

Sokkan appeared in a group photograph taken during a clinic function. They enlarged his image, placed it in a public space in the village, garlanded it, and paid homage. The doctor, Kanimozhi, and many others who had come stood there overcome with emotion.

Sornam became a statue when she saw the photograph.

"He was the man I was going to marry. He has passed away. From now on, I am indeed a widow," she thought and sobbed.

The next day, Sornam's younger brother saw Sokkan's photograph in the newspapers and showed it to her. She looked and shed tears.

"Did Father not say that this man was unworthy of me? He was a warrior, a hero who defended the nation, a martyr who gave his very life for the country," she thought, weeping.

On another page of the same newspaper appeared a report that Pattalam had exploited wartime conditions to conduct black-market trade, had been caught, and had received six months' rigorous imprisonment.

Sornam did not see it. Her eyes could see nothing else. Wherever she looked, she saw only Sokkan's form.

## English Translation

Kaathaan was a strong brute. He deserved first prize for going out of his way to buy quarrels. Merely saying, “Kaathaan is coming,” was enough to silence even a crying child. Such was the wickedness of Kaathaan, the thug! In Kaathaan’s case, every provision in the law books remained no more than writing on paper. People were afraid to utter his name even when he was nowhere in sight. How, then, could they dare seek justice to end all his atrocities? How could anyone testify against him?

Whether Kaathaan stole, raped, mercilessly beat others, or committed any number of crimes, people behaved as though they had seen nothing. That was how Kaathaan managed to live like the king of a private realm. The blink of his eye became a command; the words from his mouth became a code of justice; his deeds became righteous verdicts.

People were not afraid to speak of him only in his presence. Even when they met one another and gathered to talk, they would look around before muttering about Kaathaan’s cruelties. The mark of misery in their eyes never failed to reveal the terrifying memory of Kaathaan lodged in their hearts. Whatever their lips might say, their hearts never ceased cursing Kaathaan and wishing for his destruction.

They knew the injustice and had personally suffered its consequences. Yet they had neither the strength to wipe it away, nor knowledge of a means of escape, nor the courage to act. All they could do without shrinking was curse Kaathaan. Every moment they consoled themselves with the thought: “Whatever happens, one day God is surely going to punish Kaathaan!”

* * *

“Look at my money. Look at the stacks of clothes. Look at all those ornamental jewels. They are all yours! In return, everything I ask you to do, everything I expect from you, is only one thing!” These are the coaxing words of a lustful man.

“Your money lies here gathering moss! You possess many silken garments, I know. You have more varieties of jewellery than you need, I can see. Yet none of these gives you pleasure. You yourself admit it aloud! You seek pleasure while forgetting the beauty waiting to give it to you. For the pleasure in your mind, you expect it from one who cannot give it. You seek refuge in her for that purpose!” Thus speaks the woman struggling in the libertine’s grasp.

“This is no time for courtesy. Thinking you a fawn with bewildering eyes, I expressed my desire in gentle words. You rejected it and now preach to me. Do you think I do not know all this? You speak as though you are more learned than I and forget that you are speaking to your master, pouring out everything in your heart. What I need is your body, not your sermon!”

Speaking angrily, the libertine approaches the maiden.

The young woman screams. Her cry for help dissolves into the wind; it does not reach another person’s ears. Her heart and lips speak together: “God is watching your wickedness. One day God is surely going to punish you for this!”

* * *

After Varadappar the moneylender paid for the renovation and consecration of a Ganapati temple, devout Saivites gladly conferred upon him the title “Philanthropist.” The story of how the philanthropist accumulated his money is longer than the story of humankind and filled with anguish. Every coin in his strongbox can tell a different tale; each is a monument to history.

A debt that stood at five hundred when the annual account was written would have grown to fifteen hundred by the time of settlement. The debtor knew nothing of this conjuring trick. Like a caged lion, he would roar, but to no effect. He would not even receive words of sympathy from others; ridicule and condemnation would scorch him instead.

The title “Philanthropist” gave the moneylender a new power beyond that of the money he possessed: the force to withstand and destroy any attack. The deceived and impoverished debtor therefore found consolation in uttering the prophecy, “God is surely going to punish this wicked man!”

It is midnight on a new-moon night. Everyone in the house is sleeping soundly. A thief enters and breaks open the iron safe. The household wakes. The man who amassed the money watches someone seize, before his very eyes, every possession he has accumulated and prepare to flee. He trembles and stares in terror, unable to open his mouth. The shining weapon in the thief’s hand strikes fear into the hoarder of wealth.

His mouth alone is silenced; his heart does not rest. At first it spits out furious words. In the end it consoles itself with the confident hope that God will deliver the proper verdict due to the thief: “God is surely going to punish this rogue of a thief!”

Kaathaan, the libertine, the philanthropist, and the thief: those who suffer pain and loss because of their conduct cannot bear the agony, yet possess a determined desire that cruelty must be ended. Saying, “God will punish those who act wrongly,” they search for a power beyond themselves, a mighty force capable of granting justice, removing cruelty and grievances, providing every protection, and destroying at the root the power of oppressors stronger than they are.

They need such a force, and they believe it may be found. The need exists. The necessity of ending suffering is evident, and the importance of protection is understood. But the supposedly all-powerful entity created by people as a belief, protected and nurtured for ages, has never fulfilled their demands.

All the achievements we see today are the results of efforts made by the finest among humankind, by thinkers. They are not verbal miracles devised and preserved by cunning minds. Remove and destroy these achievements from today’s world, putting them beyond our sight, and the country will become a forest and the people a troop of unreasoning creatures.

A life of well-being, freedom, and rights are essential qualities that every individual must possess. The distinction of democracy lies in securing all these for every person and protecting them in good order from destruction. The responsibility for preserving such self-government from ruin rests upon every individual.

Government safeguards social welfare without destroying individual rights. Every citizen therefore has a duty to protect it. A person’s conception of duty is determined by the rights that person enjoys. This is the fundamental principle underlying democracy.

If those responsible for running a government, whatever its form, forget this foundation, the people subjected to the resulting decay inevitably begin to consider measures to relieve their suffering, whether they desire to do so or not. A person thrown into unstable water naturally flails his arms and legs to survive and reach the shore, whether or not he knows how to swim properly.

When an honest government casts aside the duties it must perform for the people and speaks only of the people’s duty to strengthen the government, those words can only be called the worthless utterances of a polluted mind. No person administering a government whose sole aim is public welfare may forget and pardon his own neglected duty and the service he owes the people, while expecting the people alone to fulfil their duties. It cannot work.

To prevent people from discovering their designs, or from recognising their incompetence, rulers will try to divert public attention to another sphere. Anyone who does not know this elementary lesson has no place in the political world.

What is the condition of the country today? Even staple food is not available at a price the poor can afford. Their income has not risen to that level. Nor is there any arrangement that gives them confidence in the future. At this very time, however, some are intent on making money while disregarding the people’s needs.

Millions have already died because of the greed of the wealthy in this field. That occurred under British rule, but those who made the money were “our own people.” Pandit Nehru once said that black-marketeers should be hanged. The people recovered their lost confidence that day. It also created the impression that men such as the Pandit deserved the people’s trust.

That confidence did not endure. Blood is flowing, not upon gallows but in the middle of the streets. It is not the blood of the vultures of the black market, but the blood of those who ask that their suffering be ended. And all this takes place not under another’s rule, but under self-rule.

“In the country’s present condition, hoarding foodstuffs, preventing them from reaching the general public, and making excessive profits are grievously sinful acts. God will certainly punish them for this.”

This appeared in *Dinamani* on 20 December 1948. The speaker was no religious preceptor, Vedic scholar, or philosopher. It was Patel, the all-powerful “Iron Man,” whose fiery eyes glare at those already withered, who spoke these words as a ruler of the land.

Was he addressing the rulers of the princely states? No, they support him. Was he addressing the Birlas and Bajajs? No, they are the strength behind him. Then whom did he address? Before the divided workers who have lost their livelihood, Patel calls upon the Supreme Being to punish the sinners who hoard commodities.

It appears that even the authority and military power in his hands are afraid to glance in the direction of the hoarders. Like a practised priest, he submits a petition to God.

Is it a workers’ uprising? Here is a prohibitory order. Is it an uprising against us? Here are numerous provisions of the Indian Penal Code. Beware! Faced with all such actions born of an unbearable burden of life, Patel confidently unleashes the army without a moment’s hesitation or the slightest mercy, and scatters the hunger-stricken workers. At such times he forgets God and abandons spirituality.

Patel can punish the worker. To punish the moneybags who turn people into corpses for profit, however, he needs a Supreme Being.

Is Patel a coward without strength in his convictions or authority in his hands? Why does he call upon God to punish black-marketeers? Patel holds the office of governing the country. He is swollen with the belief that he alone possesses the required fitness. He believes he has both the right and duty to punish the unsupported and destitute for the offence of voicing their needs, and demonstrates it in action.

Those harmed by his course will not wait for time to pass while saying, “God will punish Patel.”

The magician, priest, and tyrant used that saying to delude the people; helpless persons without self-confidence accepted it without resistance for a few moments of consolation; and it stood as an obstacle to progress. Patel, now come to rule the land, gives new life to that saying.

Patel’s course, a road filled with stones and thorns, cannot be hidden from the people’s eyes behind the phrase “God will punish.” Those words will no longer serve as a shield against the power of the people.

26 December 1948

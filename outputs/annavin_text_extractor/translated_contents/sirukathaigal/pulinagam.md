## English Translation

The year was 1659. Through his heroic victories, Shivaji had enthroned himself in the hearts of those who longed for freedom in the Maratha land. He had not placed a jewelled crown upon his head, yet the crown lay at his feet. He had no leisure to attend to coronation. He lacked only the title "King of the Marathas"; in substance, that was already his position.

Who was this magician? He made mountains tremble and regions bend the knee. He turned cultivators into warriors. Victory followed him everywhere. He became a new light upon the Indian subcontinent and a storm against the spreading Islamic kingdoms.

Could he be allowed to continue this way? He was praised as hero, champion, saviour, and incarnation. He was creating a new state. His warriors filled the slopes of the Satpura hills. They gathered in caves and devised their plans; afterwards cries arose in palaces. This ordinary peasant sought a place in history. To permit him to grow now was dangerous; his fame would endure into the future. Shivaji's victories had to be stopped.

Such talk arose in many courts scattered across the subcontinent. Astonishment and fear mingled whenever that fearless man was discussed. He could not be dismissed, nor could his growth be treated as ordinary. He drew red gold from the earth and produced glory in a faded Maharashtra. He was praised as stainless. He had no addiction to drink, no courtesans, no life of pomp; he stood as the emblem of valour.

Pure Maratha gold without loss of fineness! Those seated upon thrones thought that allowing this condition to continue would bring them ruin. By 1659 it had become clear that Shivaji was working not merely to remain a jagirdar but to become the ruler of an empire.

Five thousand cavalry! Seven thousand infantry! With war drums sounding, General Afzal Khan led this enormous force forward. He believed Shivaji's victories would vanish like the dust raised by his cavalry. Afzal Khan advanced with anger, determination, and immovable confidence.

Even amid the army's roar, the conversation between him and his ruler before departure continued to fall upon his ears. It marked an important stage not only in his own life but in the life of the state he served.

"Afzal! You are embarking upon the most important enterprise of your life. The fame of Bijapur depends upon your victory," the king had said.

"My king! I understand that the presence of that mountain rat is itself a disgrace to our realm. I depart now. I shall capture Shivaji and return victorious. This is certain," Afzal Khan had replied boldly.

Those words remained fixed in his memory. He looked upon his army and thought that its mere appearance before Shivaji would be enough. The chest-beating warrior would run to kneel before the ruler of Bijapur. Afzal Khan was no ordinary man; he had led armies and won many victories. His confidence was therefore no empty boast.

The forest became his encampment. Horses drank from forest streams. Spies hurried to announce that the violent storm was moving towards the Maratha land. Shivaji's heart did not falter. Yet when he heard that Afzal Khan commanded seven thousand infantry and five thousand cavalry, even Shivaji had to think.

Shivaji knew both courage and strategy. He understood that attempting to defeat so great an army by direct attack would be futile. Afzal Khan stood outside Pratapgarh; Shivaji and his little army were inside. The great storm waited outside. Only a forest stood between danger and Shivaji, and through it ran a single path. If Bijapur's forces entered by that path, Shivaji's army could not even escape.

Were all his victories to be offered as tribute to Afzal Khan? Must Maharashtra kneel and submit to the Sultan of Bijapur? Was there no other path?

In 1946, Sundaresa Iyer teaches Ibrahim the history of India. "What did fearless Shivaji do when he saw Afzal Khan's great army? He said that he wished to meet Afzal Khan alone. The Bijapur commander came. Pretending to embrace him, Shivaji tore him open with the tiger claws concealed in his hand and killed him," says the history teacher.

Without realising it, Ibrahim curses him as a scoundrel. The teacher adds a little orthodox instruction: anything may be done to destroy the wicked. Whatever explanation he offers, can Ibrahim refrain from saying, "This was conspiracy, breach of trust, and treachery. It was not the conduct of a warrior"?

Could a great hero truly act so? Is it martial tradition to invite a man for peace talks, embrace him, and murder him by deceit in the very act of embracing? No. Not only Ibrahim but the outside world as a whole will say that Shivaji killed Afzal Khan unjustly, committed a conspiracy, and placed a stain upon his own fame.

Yet Ibrahim does not know why so great an idealist should have performed this treacherous deed. Nor do the friends who grieve over it in solitude. Why did Shivaji act so? What subtle power led the heroic Shivaji into this deception? The history teacher will not tell that truth either. What loss does he suffer if Ibrahim believes Shivaji committed betrayal? He belongs neither to Ibrahim's people nor to Shivaji's Maratha community. He descends from the earth-gods, the Brahmins.

What was the true cause of Shivaji's course? To discover it, we must again accompany Afzal Khan's army for a while.

The soldiers sharpened swords and spears. They discussed how a blade should be cast under one condition, where a frontal assault should be made, and where an attack should come from the flank. Afzal Khan's army immersed itself in preparations for war. Within his tent, Afzal Khan sat joyfully imagining the approaching victory.

Gopinath Pandit too moved through that forest. He bore no sword. But he was a member of the Brahmin caste, which claimed that Maheswara had granted it the greatness of seeing all other castes beneath its feet. Afzal Khan had arrived with military force to defeat Shivaji, but if Shivaji desired negotiations, Gopinath was sent as the envoy to bring them to completion.

Before Gopinath stood Afzal Khan and his eager army; opposite stood Pratapgarh. Here the Maratha community ruled. On one side were Muslims, and on the other Marathas. Nowhere upon the throne could Aryans be seen. Those Aryans who proudly called that land Bharatavarsha, Bharata Kanda, and Aryavarta were absent from political power. Only the work of carrying messages had fallen to them. Gopinath Pandit's mind's eye could hardly have failed to see these things.

Accompanied by associates and servants, Gopinath entered the city.

"Submit, Shivaji! Otherwise destruction will enter this city. You may possess a fearless heart, but Afzal Khan's army is immense," he warned.

Shivaji received Gopinath Pandit with respect, offered him many courtesies, and arranged accommodation for him and his companions. Was it not proper to honour an envoy of a powerful government? Did heroic Shivaji not understand propriety? He understood not only propriety but some strategy too.

On learning that Gopinath belonged to the Brahmin caste, Shivaji gained hope that he might find a way out of the danger approaching him. That was the inner meaning of the splendid hospitality shown to Gopinath. The Pandit's quarters were arranged at some distance from those given to his companions.

At midnight Shivaji went alone to that secluded place to speak privately with Gopinath Pandit, confident that the envoy could readily be drawn to his side. The Maratha hero's confidence was not misplaced. The Brahmin received Shivaji with a smiling face.

"Great sage! My mind suffers when I think of the danger that has come upon the Maratha land."

"It will naturally cause distress. Nevertheless, great warrior, there is no path but submission."

"It is not submission that troubles me, earth-god! My solemn purpose, fixed in my mind like Bhishma's vow, will be ruined."

"His military power is greater. Resistance is useless and defeat certain. Who would embrace destruction?"

"Venerable one! My resolve is to create a Hindu empire here in Bharatavarsha, in our birthland and sacred land. I labour not for myself but for the sake of the noblest Brahmins. You know my ideal. My purpose is to establish Shivaji as protector of the cow and the Brahmin. Only with this kingdom will former greatness endure and ancient glory reappear. Vedic custom and caste duty will shine in Aryavarta.

"Swami, my mind is confused and I beg you: show me a way to fulfil my resolve, offer a means for Sanatana Dharma to flourish. Shall the youth I have laboured to nurture be destroyed by this army of Bijapur? Venerated one, show me a way!"

"I am to show you the way? Shivaji, I am the envoy of the Sultan of Bijapur!"

"No, Swami. You are the representative of Bharatavarsha, an Aryan elder charged with saving Aryavarta. Whether Afzal Khan sent you or the Sultan of Bijapur sent you, I regard you as a messenger sent by God.

"O Aryan! As one born into the Brahmin caste, you possess the right to prevent danger from reaching Aryavarta. It is also your duty. I command the Aryan army; issue your order! Shall we surrender Aryavarta to foreigners? Shall I submit to Afzal Khan? O Aryan, what does my submission mean? It means that Aryavarta has perished!"

Gopinath Pandit had come to make Shivaji submit. Shivaji poured these polished arguments before him. Gopinath might have replied: "Shivaji, I cannot commit treachery. Breach of trust is a great sin. I eat the salt of the Sultan of Bijapur and will not bring him harm. That would be the deed of a coward, forbidden by dharma."

But did Gopinath say so? No. He possessed no determination that treachery must not be committed. He savoured the words "Aryavarta," "Aryan elder," and "Sanatana" and agreed to betray the Sultan of Bijapur. He came as an envoy and consented to become a traitor.

We do not know how long struggle remained in Gopinath Pandit's mind or whether even a speck of fear arose that a future world might revile him as a traitor. Ecstatic at receiving the devotion of his supplicant, the Pandit disregarded blame and sin and agreed to betray the very state of Bijapur that had sustained him.

Was Gopinath an ordinary man? He was a Pandit, learned in Vedas, Puranas, and epics: texts containing countless methods of subtle deception, destroying a man from beside him, killing through an embrace, and administering poison while speaking tenderly.

He had read in the Mahabharata of Dhritarashtra attempting to crush an enemy in an embrace. He knew how Krishna hid the sun with the discus, made it appear that evening had arrived, and helped Arjuna kill his foe. Was the Mahabharata not enough to destroy an enemy? Both Gopinath and Shivaji knew the Mahabharata and Ramayana that taught an Aryan path in which there was no need to examine dharma and justice. Could their devout recitation of the story in which the worshipful Rama shot Vali from behind a tree, and their delight in hearing it, go to waste? If the "methods" learned from those epics were not used at a moment like this, what was the benefit of reading them?

Did Gopinath advise Shivaji to perform some sacrifice and obtain some weapon with which to defeat Afzal Khan? No. Did he tell him to worship a deity, repeat a mantra, and then go to war? He did not sit as a guru. He became the traitor who would ruin Bijapur.

"Do not fight Afzal Khan. For appearance's sake, send word that you will submit. Say that you have resolved to surrender directly to him. Let Afzal Khan visit this place alone. Send a message that you will seek refuge in him and desire no battle," Gopinath advised. Pandit Gopinath? No, sinner Gopinath!

"Will Afzal Khan come?"

"I shall send him. That is, I shall make him come here alone."

"And after he comes? Your enemy, capable of destroying Maharashtra, your realm and your future, comes alone and without support to meet you. Yet you ask me what happens after he comes, you witless..."

"I understand, Aryan. But Afzal will surely come armed."

"Madman! Afzal Khan will come to grant refuge to Shivaji, who waits in fear to submit. He will come unarmed. Such arrangements will be made."

"Panditji! Your lotus feet..."

It was Gopinath Pandit's conspiracy that created the arrangement for Afzal Khan to meet Shivaji alone. Heroic Shivaji, skilled with the sword, could not have possessed equal skill in casting deceit. Gopinath Pandit of the Vedic caste undertook that service, in accordance with inherited practice.

Shivaji rejoiced that he had won a great diplomatic victory. Gopinath rejoiced that he had transformed Maharashtra into a seat of Aryan service. Afzal Khan reclined expecting the capable envoy to return bearing news of the surrender of the Shivaji who had fought so fiercely.

Gopinath returned after arranging Afzal Khan's murder. Afzal knew armies and warfare. How could he know that human figures carrying sin, conspiracy, and bitterness moved beside him?

"Salaam, Panditji!" Afzal welcomed Gopinath happily. Was it only then? Even in 1946 people welcome Gopinaths with "Come, Swami!"

"Has he consented to surrender?"

"Bahut achha, Panditji! They call him a great brute. How did he agree to submit?"

"Let him be a brute, Commander. Is he blind? Can he fail to see the enormous army we possess?"

"Gopinath, this is your victory."

"In one sense, it is mine."

Only in one sense? In every sense it was Gopinath's victory. Afzal, who had set out for war, must have revelled in the joy of making the great warrior kneel without even fighting. Why would he not? The warrior whose victory drums had sounded in every quarter, whose triumphal flag flew magnificently upon every mountaintop, had agreed to kneel. Could Afzal Khan fail to rejoice?

That joy increased many times when Shivaji's envoy arrived to confirm the surrender. Gopinath Pandit was Afzal Khan's envoy; Shivaji's envoy too was a Brahmin, named Krishnaji Bhaskar.

"Shivaji does not desire war. He agrees to obey Bijapur and submit to the Sultan's authority. Hero of heroes, I beg you graciously to accept our Shivaji's request for surrender," Bhaskar said.

In supreme delight Afzal Khan replied, "We shall enter Pratapgarh in procession with our army. Shivaji may submit there. As the Sultan's representative, we shall grant an audience to Shivaji, who agrees to live under Bijapur's rule."

Bhaskar grew afraid. In a trembling voice he began the speech that would cast the net: "One request! Shivaji wishes to meet you alone to discuss his surrender. He thought of coming here."

Gopinath, leader of the conspiracy, interrupted: "Your peerless hero fears to come before this great army. Brahmin, why conceal it? Are you ashamed?"

Afzal Khan's joy exceeded all bounds. "Gopinath Panditji, is Shivaji such a coward?"

Gopinath had awaited that question. He poured out flattery until Afzal Khan's judgment was destroyed.

"What wonder is there, Commander? Would he not have heard of your victories? Until now he has strutted about boasting of victories over cities without protectors and crowds without courage. Today he learns that Afzal Khan, a resolute hero leading a mighty army and standing like a lion upon the field, has arrived. He has begun to tremble. Does he not know the greatness of Bijapur? He survived only while Bijapur's gaze did not fall upon him. But now?"

The flattery utterly overthrew Afzal.

"Very well. Tell him not to fear. Tomorrow I shall come alone and unarmed to accept Shivaji's surrender," he told Krishnaji Bhaskar, swelling with the thought that he had never seen such a coward.

The meeting occurred according to the conspirators' plan. Gopinath brought Afzal Khan without soldiers and without weapons to meet Shivaji. Pretending affection and a desire for peace, Shivaji embraced him.

"Alas!" Afzal Khan screamed. The tiger claws concealed in Shivaji's hand had entered his lower abdomen.

Afzal Khan, who had come to accept surrender, was treacherously killed. Meanwhile the path by which his army could enter had been blocked. Believing there would be no war and making no preparations, Afzal Khan's soldiers had immersed themselves in happiness. Shivaji's army sprang upon them. There too came massacre.

Gopinath Pandit and Krishnaji Bhaskar induced the warrior Shivaji to practise deceit and commit conspiracy, bringing an indelible stain upon the great hero. They also took Afzal Khan's life.

People know the historical event in which Shivaji unjustly killed Afzal Khan; historians relate it. But they do not speak of Gopinath Pandit and Krishnaji Bhaskar. People imagine history to be merely the story of kings and make no effort to identify men like Gopinath and Bhaskar.

Yet in the history of this subcontinent, the inner meaning and the truth become clear only when we learn about the Gopinaths, rather than merely those who held the sceptre.

It is more fitting to say that Afzal Khan's death came not from the tiger claws but from the Brahmin's smile.

20 January 1946

## Translator's Notes

This is Anna's polemical interpretation of the Shivaji–Afzal Khan episode, translated as presented rather than asserted as an independently verified historical reconstruction. `புலிநகம்` is the concealed weapon commonly rendered “tiger claws” or `wagh nakh`. `பூதேவர்` and the concluding `பூசுரன்` literally elevate Brahmins as “earth-gods”; the latter is translated as “Brahmin” so the final contrast remains intelligible. `ஆரியவர்த்தம்`, `சனாதன தர்மம்`, protection of cow and Brahmin, and the epic examples are deliberately deployed in the source's critique of caste and political manipulation. `பூசுரனின் புன்னகை` (“the Brahmin's smile”) is the final counter-image to the tiger claws.

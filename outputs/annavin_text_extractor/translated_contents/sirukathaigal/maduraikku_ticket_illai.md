## English Translation

Marriage, they say! Marriage! These old people have no other work. From morning till evening, I am the beast of burden! And they say I need a companion for this life! She will sit at home asking for this and that: look at Annam's sari, look at Akilandam's bangles, look at Sornam's luxuries, look at Sokkammal's smile. At night it will feel as though she is pricking me with a needle. I am expected to suffer this torment, and my people at home lecture me about it. In my wretched condition, do I need marriage as well? At least now the trouble comes only in the morning.

"Hey, ass! You fat idler! Look at that face, you crybaby! Why do you whine through your nose when told to work? Do you not take your pay like a fat radish? A shameless creature! Even while living as a destitute beggar, these fellows carry such arrogance in their heads!"

This ritual of abuse takes place in the morning. At night, at least, I can pass the time building castles in my mind. The corpses who gave birth to me now want to destroy even this peace! If I marry, the morning show will continue at night as well. The language may differ, but trouble remains trouble. What does its form matter?

The master holds court with bulging, threatening eyes. The wife will summon tears to her eyes and sing a song of sorrow. When he abuses me, anger may arise. If not one time, then another time I may glare back, mutter, or curse him behind his back. These offer at least a little consolation. But when she lies beside me and pierces my heart with thorns, there will be less anger and more sorrow.

"Poor woman! How much affection she has for me! She shares our joys and sorrows and has the right to ask us. We too have a duty to buy what she needs. I am her husband. All she asks for is a decent sari like those worn by four other women, not a necklace of nine gems."

Thoughts like these would arise, making it impossible to grow angry with her. If I happened to glare, tears would overflow and roll down her cheeks. Then I would have to wipe them myself, calm her sobs, grant the boon that I would buy what she wanted, receive a kiss as advance payment, and lie there sighing. The hard-hearted master's morning cry of "Hey, ass!" would seem bearable even a thousand times over. But at midnight she would coax, "Yes, will you buy it if I ask? If you had any love for me, would you deceive me like this?" To hear this and struggle because there was no way to keep her heart from being hurt would be unbearable torment.

What benefit did my parents gain by marrying? Me! A poor man, a pitiable labourer! Knowing this, they still insist that I too must marry. The hardship I already have is enough. I want no marriage.

Madurai, a mill worker, spoke this way while he remained in the stage of brahmacharya. He resolved to remain in that stage for life. Then his wage of six annas rose to one and a quarter rupees, and the necessity of changing stages arose with it.

What could he do? His parents stubbornly wanted to see three knots tied before breath left their bodies. "If I do not marry, they will have to die without peace of mind. Why should I cause dissatisfaction to those who bore and raised me? Whatever gruel we have, let another person drink it and share hardship or happiness with me. Perhaps when I touch her and tie the marriage pendant, God will open his eyes and our troubles will end."

Madurai thus claimed that he consented to marriage only because of his parents' pressure. There was some truth in what he said. But their sighs were not the whole reason he wished to leave celibacy and enter the householder's stage. Periyanayaki's gentle smile was a reason too. He did not say that aloud. Is it only Madurai? No Madurai will admit openly that he submitted to his Periyanayaki's smile!

"Madurai! I hear there is fragrant marjoram in your garden. Give me a little," said Thirumalai.

"Why? For the god's temple?"

"For the goddess's shrine, not for the god."

"Which goddess's shrine?"

"You fool! For my wife, Sowbagyam. Not for a temple or sacred tank."

"Thirumalai, it seems you will not cross the line drawn by your wife. If she asks for sesame, you look ready to carry home the oil itself."

"Yes, indeed! We are great kings, are we not? Are we going to buy diamond necklaces and armfuls of bangles? Poor woman, for the misfortune of marrying us, should we not do whatever lies within our means? Am I alone in this? Let it happen to you tomorrow. Then you too will be the same."

"Me? I shall never marry!"

"How many people have said that! However confirmed a bachelor he may be, after a few days he will grin at some woman, marry her, and settle down. It happens in every land. What makes you different?"

Thirumalai was Madurai's friend, and they worked at the same place. In the evenings Thirumalai preached this doctrine to Madurai. But on reaching home, Thirumalai became Madurai. That is, whenever Sowbagyam asked for something, he would say, "Has your torment begun already? Madurai knows about this; that is why he says he will never marry. He is clever. He has none of this misery."

"We shall see how long that bachelor's obstinacy lasts," Sowbagyam would reply. "He says marriage will bring him hardship. Is a wife a tiger or a bear? Some men declare that they want no marriage and that marriage is trouble. Why? Only so they may roam as they please. If a man does not marry and maintain a family, will he not spend his earnings as he pleases, ruin his body, and become useless? I ask you: the poor man goes to work in the morning and returns in the evening. When he comes home exhausted, should there not be a wife to ask whether he wants something for his thirst? If he lies ill for four days, should there not be someone beside him to lift and tend him? His parents may die today or tomorrow. Should they not see a grandson or granddaughter before they go? Why does none of this occur to that man? A fine man indeed! He wants to earn and eat everything himself, with no wife, because he would have to feed her too!"

Sowbagyam would pour out this discourse, and the next evening Thirumalai would submit the sacred hymn to Madurai.

Six annas became ten annas and then a rupee. When he earned six annas, Madurai had said, "Thirumalai, your wife seems very talkative." When it became a rupee, he responded calmly to the same discourse: "Everyone says what your wife says."

His wage rose, and at the same time he received Periyanayaki's smile. She had not launched that smile to break his celibacy. She saw him, remembered what her elder sister had said about him, and smiled. It shattered his vow. Periyanayaki was Sowbagyam's younger sister.

Madurai entered the householder's stage. As many powerful reasons as he had once found for observing celibacy, he now found for entering this new stage.

"A house has charm only when a wife of one's own moves about in it. How long did I remain alone, and what did I gain? The moment I enter the house, Periyanayaki looks at me and asks whether I need hot water. All the hardship of the morning flies away on the wind. Somehow I labour; Periyanayaki lovingly serves food, saves a little for me from the curry cooked in the morning, puts it before me at night, and urges, 'A little more, just one handful.' She asks tenderly, 'How hard you work! Should you not eat your fill? Does not everything depend upon strength in the body?' She becomes a doctor and says, 'You are growing thin as a splinter,' and rubs oil on my head when it aches. Ah, that is a joy of its own! Whatever hardships a man has, is it not happiness to have a wife at home?"

Madurai rejoiced in such thoughts. His happiness increased when Periyanayaki became a mother. Even when they had two children, he remained happy.

"Where is he? Watch the little one carefully. It looks like a cold," Madurai would say with the proud responsibility of a father.

They became four children. Happiness gave birth to anxiety.

"Good heavens! Why are these creatures screaming like crows?"

"Can children exist without crying?"

"Children! Grand children indeed! They howl like owls and take my life."

"They were born from this sinner's womb. That is why they suffer like this. Oh! You must not make the children cry so."

Worry took up residence in the householder's stage. The family grew, but the wage did not. Madurai's anger grew. He felt that leaving the stage of celibacy had been a mistake. What use was longing for that former stage after becoming the father of four?

The arrival of smallpox granted Madurai his third stage. Periyanayaki fell victim to the disease. Four children, and parents reduced to skin and sinew! In that condition, he quarrelled with his employer and lost his job. The full burden of household life entered his heart. His appearance changed; his mind changed too.

He entered vanaprastha, the retirement to the forest. That is, he entrusted the children to Thirumalai and his parents to God. Bowing at the feet of labour recruiter Kanakasabai, he obtained work and went to a rubber plantation in Burma.

He laboured in Burma. No family: the forest-dweller's stage! It gave him some satisfaction. He worked and ate what he received. His hand was his pillow; yearning took the place of sleep. He remained in this condition for several years.

The stage of vanaprastha, his withdrawal from responsibility for maintaining the family, did not last long. His eldest boy picked a pocket and received three months' imprisonment. No one knew where the second boy had gone; a conjurer arrived, and the boy ran away with him. The third was a girl; she died after only three days of fever. The fourth boy fell accidentally into the plantation well.

Letters arrived one after another. Madurai's sorrow in the forest-dweller's stage became complete when another letter reported that his parents were begging. At once he left the rubber-estate job too.

He wandered and wandered. He tried asking, "Sir, a little rice." He became intimate with discarded leaf plates. He gained the friendship of some mendicants, donned ochre, smeared himself with sacred ash, and caressed cannabis. He entered the stage of renunciation.

There was no work and no need to ask for wages. There was no family and therefore no responsibility. Eat when something is obtained; worship until something is obtained! In this renunciant stage there was neither the anguish of vanaprastha nor the hardship of household life. Nor was that all: it did not contain even the loneliness of celibacy. Madurai the ascetic found a female ascetic too. Like him, she knew how to cry "Arogara!" She did not have to depend solely upon him to survive, but she needed an "other." Madurai lived without anxiety in the renunciant stage.

The ideal beauties within the Hindu religion are no ordinary things. What sublime principles are dharma, artha, kama, and moksha! What magnificent stages are celibacy, household life, forest retirement, and renunciation! Our religion shines as the noble abode of them all.

People say all sorts of things about industry and prosperity having to grow in the nation. Yet these changes must not destroy this spiritual character. So worried a judge speaking at a Ramakrishna monastery celebration in Madras. The gentleman who was moving from the office of Advocate-General to that of High Court judge spoke about the special greatness of Hinduism.

Mr Austin, who had read the Gospel, replied at once: "These ideals are certainly beautiful, jewels of religion! But when a labourer rises in the morning without even rice water, suffers in a wretched place unfit for human sight, writhes with hunger, and lies weeping without knowing where he will obtain even one meal, what can these divine utterances, sacred teachings, and ideals of the stages of life mean to him? How, sir, can he understand the teachings of those religious philosophies?"

Yes! Not only Mr Austin. Madurai, driven stumbling through all four stages by poverty, distress, and the country's unequal economic order, would not hear the doctrine of four stages proclaimed by our comfortably civilised gentleman without laughing.

"My lords, I know nothing of the grandeur of the four stages. But I and countless people like me have rolled in the deformities of these stages. Holiness is not the cause. It is the poverty festering in society! If your sacred books show a way to remove it, tell us. Great men who know religion, tell us whether your religion contains a path that can give life to those of us who have no means to live. We shall listen!"

That is what Madurai would say. But the place where the judge delivered his instruction about immaculate Hinduism was hardly one that Madurai, distressed because he had no place of his own, could enter. It was a place to which High Court dignitaries and government officers drove in gleaming motorcars to discourse upon the impermanence of the world and the falsity of life. How could Madurai go there?

It was a place for wealthy philosophers, aristocrats versed in doctrine, wise men who knew no poverty, and Vedantists who knew no suffering. Madurai had no "ticket" to go there.

4 March 1945

## Translator's Notes

The title is a pun: `மதுரை` is the mill worker's name, while the wording also reads “No ticket to Madurai.” The story maps his material decline onto the four classical `ஆஸ்ரமம்` stages: `பிரம்மச்சரியம்` (celibate student life), `கிருஹஸ்தாஸ்ரமம்` (householder life), `வானப்பிரஸ்தம்` (forest retirement), and `சன்யாசம்` (renunciation). The mapping is deliberately satirical rather than devotional. `ஆறணா` means six annas under the pre-decimal currency system. `வைசூரி` is smallpox. `தங்காணி` refers to a labour recruiter or overseer who sent workers to plantations. `அழோகரா` is the mendicant cry usually written `அரோகரா`; the source form is reflected conservatively in the Tamil block.

## English Translation

One important reason for the victory of the November Revolution was that many people had already begun to understand the suffering of the poor, creating a favourable climate. Many noblemen, and even some members of the royal family, changed their minds under the influence of that climate. They wished to wipe away the tears of the poor, but tears could not be wiped away with the silk cloth of the aristocracy!

Noblemen whose minds changed in this way incurred the wrath of the Tsarist government and were punished. They were exiled; some went abroad.

One such Russian nobleman, punished and then escaped from prison, reached a foreign land and told his story to friends there. This is its substance.

"Friends! You know very well that I was a nobleman born into an aristocratic family in Russia. You may all wonder how a man such as I came to join the revolutionary movement. Listen, and I shall tell you how my mind changed.

"Born into a noble family, I was raised as a pampered child. My father possessed great influence in the government. Our estate was vast, with servants and retainers in abundance. Life itself was a festival!

"As a boy I practised religion. That is, I went to church as custom required. Our family was deeply committed to orthodox religion.

"It was at the age of twelve that my faith in religion began to erode. Why? I saw how meekly and submissively the priests who came to my mansion behaved before my father. When those priests remained silent before a devotee who spoke contemptuously of them merely because he was a lord, how could I retain faith in their teachings, respect for the places of worship under their authority, or belief in a religion organised in this way? I realised that just as a lord possessed many kinds of servants, the priest too was one of them. With that realisation, the devotion of my childhood went up in smoke.

"I entered the special military college intended for the sons of noblemen. It was a pleasure house, the god of lust's dancing hall, and a place for squandering money. Drink and debauchery were its textbooks. Ostentation, intimidation, bullying, and contempt for ordinary people were the lessons we received at that military college. It trained us more thoroughly to become aristocrats than soldiers. The student who spent the most money, had the most midnight companions, and could drink himself into the deepest stupor enjoyed the greatest esteem, for such a life was considered the mark of his nobility.

"Like the others, I plunged into those pleasures. I danced, sang, ruined women, learned arrogance, and became worthy of the rank of nobleman. During holidays I returned home, but I did not fail to study my lessons. What lessons? Pleasure was our principal subject! For that, there were the beautiful village women and the maidens on our estate. Any woman who became available had no power to say no. I was a nobleman's son; she was a labourer's daughter. She might even become a wife! If the hut would not render even this service to the mansion, what else was it for?

"My family knew of my hunting of maidens. What did that matter? 'A young man will naturally amuse himself. Is that not normal?' they said. What amusement? Maintaining a farm of sexual exploitation! The aristocratic world permitted it.

"I was eighteen when I received a commission in the army. At the same time the Caucasus war broke out, and I took part in it. How many villages I burned! How many people I killed! It was war. What else was there to do?

"When the war ended, I returned home. The whole town was astonished, but not by my valour. It marvelled at the two beautiful Caucasian maidens I had brought back as prisoners of war. I established a separate residence for them, a house of pleasure for my amusement. The town knew; my family knew. Who could stop me? I was a nobleman's son, merely doing what countless noblemen did. If beautiful women were obtained, were they to be allowed to wander away?

"The two Caucasian women served my revelry well. But, poor creatures, they had grown up amid mountain snow. Our climate did not suit them. They wasted away and died within a short time.

"At that time my father held the rank of general in the army and had also taken part in the Caucasus campaign. The Tsar graciously praised our military service and granted us a great estate in the Caucasus.

"Later, when some people launched a revolt in Poland, I went there with a force. 'Seize! Shoot! Cut down! Kill!' I ordered. I won fame. I returned home and sank into pleasure as before.

"Through all these events, it never occurred to me that this life was wrong or that my sexual revelry was an injustice. How could such a thought arise? I saw the same conduct in every mansion in Russia, and it was surrounded by these very nobles that our Tsar ruled.

"A millionaire wished to give a banquet in honour of my military fame. He was not a hereditary lord and therefore regarded our permission to host us as a great distinction. Many noblemen came, along with countless beauties. Drink flowed like a river, music surged, and everything was pleasure. A dancer famous throughout Russia had been brought in to offer an enticing performance. At that time all Russia spoke of her. You would call that dance a vulgar erotic display and condemn it as immoral. Our noblemen delighted not only in watching it but even in hearing others praise its splendour. I too had heard of the dance but had never seen it. That day I did.

"After the banquet, a servant announced, 'My lords! Here comes the fruit. Eat and rejoice!' It followed our custom of serving fruit at the end of a feast. The curtain parted and music sounded. There, gleaming in brilliant light, was a golden platter. Upon it lay the celebrated dancer, entirely naked, with fruit arranged around her!

"We roared with delight. Fruit surrounding a maiden! A golden plate bearing a woman! Midnight, lustful hunger, that woman, and the noblemen gathered that night: the orgy they enacted became the final step in my aristocratic life. Only after climbing to that summit could I recognise the terrible abyss below.

"That recognition did not come to me in the banqueting hall. There, everyone had only one thought and one action: wine and woman, alternating and combined; the woman who gave wine, the woman intoxicated by wine, the woman who herself became intoxicating. There were many such states, but only one animal condition. I call it animal existence today. Then I considered it civilisation and a nobleman's birthright.

"Midnight passed and dawn approached. Until I left the mansion, I did not know that the darkness covering my life was about to depart and good sense was about to dawn. With laughter, embraces, kisses, and cries of pleasure all around, could another thought arise? It did not.

"We had ordered our carriages to be ready at the mansion entrance at four in the morning. Many noblemen took leave of me and departed. I left somewhat leisurely. The millionaire who had entertained me had a beautiful wife!

"I came outside with my host.

"'Vasia! Hey, Vasia!' I called to my coachman. There was no answer. Only the carriage stood there.

"'Hey, Vasia!' I shouted more loudly.

"'These fellows are always like this. They fall asleep sitting in the carriage. Hey, Vasia!' the millionaire too called, in order to help me.

"Vasia gave no reply. We went close to the carriage. The fellow lay there without speech or breath.

"'Ass! He has drunk himself senseless,' said the millionaire.

"'I cannot believe it. He has no habit of drinking,' I said, going closer and shaking Vasia.

"Friends, he lay dead! Heavy snow was falling. He had arrived at four in the morning and had waited for two hours in the cold and snow for me while I revelled inside. Snow fell and fell, tormenting him and freezing him rigid. He became a corpse! I had been rolling in the mire of lust while my poor servant outside had been killed by the snow. He too was a Russian!

"'Even my coachman lies dead!'

"'Alas! So does our stout fellow!'

"'Hey! Alas! This man too is the same.'

"Four or five noblemen cried out in this fashion. The snow had been severe that night. Five coachmen died while their masters were engrossed in feasting and entertainment.

"A lightning-swift blow struck my mind. Had he not waited there for my sake? Had he not died in the snow? Who was responsible for that wrong, if not I? What kind of life was a nobleman's life? While a lord remained inside drunk, a poor man's corpse had fallen outside. Was this right? My mind became confused.

"The millionaire who had given the feast paid no attention even to the corpses. He busied himself finding other men to drive our carriages. 'Do not worry about the bodies. I shall arrange to have them buried,' he said.

"Yes, friends, my carriage departed after another driver was found. Vasia, who had brought the carriage to take me home, lay dead opposite the mansion in which I had spent the night in sexual revelry. The carriage began to run, and my mind began to burn. My head spun. 'What a life! What an injustice! Is this tolerable?' Questions surged within me.

"The carriage stopped in the middle of the road.

"'Someone is lying on the road,' said the driver.

"Another lash struck my mind. I leapt from the carriage. A figure lay in the road, life half gone, crying feebly. I approached and found a woman frozen stiff. The driver and I rubbed oil over her arms and legs to restore warmth. We lifted her, intending to place her in the carriage.

"Friends, only then did I see an infant beside her, a child just born. The icy wind had killed it. This was on a main road in a Russian city! Until that very moment I had been drinking and revelling, and now I returned to find a corpse in my path. There lay my servant's corpse; here lay the dreadful death of a helpless woman's newborn child, while her own life hung by a thread.

"Amid such horror and destruction, what were we doing? Pleasure amid ruin! A corpse on the road and a dance concert at midnight! What horror was this? Should the condition of a country be like this? There a corpse, here a corpse, and she too might become one!

"I was afraid, my mind filled with pain. 'Drive quickly to a doctor's house,' I ordered. A doctor lived only at the fifth mile. We laid her there and gave her medicine. It was there that I saw her properly.

"Friends, she too looked at me. How can I describe that look? She was one of the many women sacrificed to my years of amusement, a labourer from our own estate.

"'Is it you?' I asked.

"'My lord?' she spoke in a feeble voice.

"Lord! A demon responsible for people falling dead upon the earth! That helpless woman called me 'my lord', me, the man who had ruined her.

"Her husband was a soldier. He too had served in the Caucasus war. It brought me fame; it brought him wounds, illness, and a severe fever. She had set out to find a doctor and obtain some medicine. It was night, and her time of childbirth had arrived, but her desire to save her husband's life made her disregard darkness and snow. She heard wolves howling somewhere. What could the helpless woman do? She ran, lost her way, fainted, and fell. Until I lifted her into the carriage, she did not even know that she had become a mother.

"She told her story, and I became a new man. I need not even say 'a new man', friends. Until then I had been a lord; that day I became a human being!

"I resolved that I must never again participate in that life. I must labour for the poor and humble. As long as stone-hearted people could watch them fall dead upon the road, Russia could not live and humanity could not endure. My mind seethed. Many thoughts and countless questions arose within it.

"Most Russians lay immersed in poverty and ignorance. They had to be helped to rise. How?

"Why, they were ignorant and did not know how to make a living!

"What a strange chain this was. Poverty and ignorance: each gave rise to the other, and each became the other's child.

"My head spun when I began to consider these problems. Though I sat in a mansion, only the mud hut appeared before my mind's eye. My gaze refused to settle upon the wine cup. I could no longer be a lord. The real Russia called me to come and serve it.

"I set out. I visited the homes of the poor and spoke with them. I found grievances heaped up in their millions and resolved to offer whatever help I could.

"Noblemen like me mocked my new course and called it madness. Their words did not reach my ears.

"These poor people were the labourers of Russia, and while labouring they also paid enormous taxes. The nobles paid no tax and always enjoyed the Tsar's gracious favour!

"I began helping the poor. I established small schools here and there to dispel their illiteracy.

"'They are to study? If they study, will they continue to work? Will they not begin asking questions? What are you doing, man? You are educating the poor! Is that not a danger to our position? Can one light a match beside an explosives factory?'

"The nobles came, offered this learned counsel, and departed. I paid no heed. I had become a servant of the real Russia.

"It was then that I obtained a position that brought me into close contact with the Tsar. Thinking that I might use the opportunity, I tactfully explained the necessity of transforming the people's condition.

"With a faint smile, he replied, 'My lord, there are only two ways to govern the world. One is for us to rule the people; the other is for the people to rule us. If we act according to the method you propose, the people will begin to rule us. There will be neither mansion nor palace!'

"I had understood the Tsar's mind, and he had understood me well.

"Within a few days I was arrested on the charge that I belonged to the revolutionary movement, tried, sentenced to life, and sent to a Siberian prison.

"It was no prison. For me it was a place of enlightenment! During the two years I spent there, wide reading increased my general knowledge and strengthened my conviction that a new Russia must arise. I truly became a member of the revolutionary movement.

"Disguised as an old woman, I boarded a train. From Vladivostok I travelled aboard a Japanese ship to Nagasaki, later stayed in America, and finally arrived here.

"This is the story of how I, born a lord, became a revolutionary. Nor is it true of me alone. Anyone who directly witnesses the conditions, poverty, and cruelty of the real Russia will feel the heart beat. That beat will kill the lord within. When the lord dies, a human being is born.

"How else can the Tsar's Russia, founded upon beastly justice, beastly desire, and beastly life, describe one who fights for human dignity and speaks for humanity except as a revolutionary? The Tsar accuses me of being a revolutionary. I declare that I became human. This is my story."

Thus he finished his account.

17 November 1946

## Translator's Notes

`சீமான்` and `பிரபு` both denote members of the privileged landed or aristocratic order; they are rendered contextually as “nobleman,” “lord,” or “aristocrat.” The source uses `மாதாகோவில்` for a Christian church and `வைதீக மார்க்கம்` for the family's orthodox religious observance. `காமவேள்` is the god of desire. The title and conclusion deliberately contrast inherited rank with becoming fully human through recognition of suffering. Historical references such as the Caucasus campaign, the Polish revolt, the Tsar, and the November Revolution are translated as presented by the source and are not independently reconstructed.

## English Translation

Everywhere there was tremendous excitement about the unveiling ceremony for the statue of Vezhamugathar, king of Vetripuram.

More people spoke about the sculptor’s artistic skill than about the king’s rare virtues.

To tell the truth, the ceremony did not confer glory upon the king. It became a festival honouring the sculptor’s ability.

The sculptor Chandanathevan had displayed the whole of his artistry in creating that statue.

The king himself was astonished by it.

“It exists today, but what will it become tomorrow?” the religious teacher often says of the human body.

Though the human body is Mahesa’s creation, the divine sculptor’s statue is afflicted by age and disease and finally becomes a handful of earth.

Eyes overflowing with beauty one day lose their light and become empty sockets.

Majesty turns into infirmity.

Shoulders capable of lifting a mountain collapse.

The smile disappears somewhere.

They call man the divine sculptor’s portrait of thought. Yet time strikes that statue, bends and shrinks it, and reduces it to refuse.

But here man becomes a sculptor. He carves stone into a statue and conquers time.

Majesty, charm, and radiance remain exactly as they were.

The divine sculptor’s “leather puppet” shrivels, but the statue shaped by human hands withstands time with youth and beauty undiminished.

The king praised it: “Not merely is it a work that will astonish experts in sculpture; it has been fashioned in a way that makes them proclaim that the art has found a new master.”

Unceasing thought and labour had left the sculptor’s body itself wasted and disordered.

When a mother awaiting childbirth screamed, those ignorant of the matter called it pain. When she heard the infant’s cry, her face blossomed. “A feast!” said the mother as the child wailed.

The sculptor was in that same condition.

His thought had become a statue. His conception had taken form.

There had been many sleepless nights.

The king’s forehead possessed a certain curve, but it appeared only when he was thinking. For many people, thought makes the face lose its lustre. During thought, Vezhamugathar acquired a special beauty.

That must appear in the stone. It had formed in the sculptor’s mind, but how was the required ease to come into his hand? How?

The sculptor had reflected similarly upon many details.

He would strike once, carve away a little, make a slight incision, and remove a tiny fragment. Then he would stand at a distance and look. Milk would boil over and fruit would be carried away by birds while he remained absorbed.

After immense hardship, he gave birth to his treasure of art.

Now the townspeople could see it and his kin could rejoice.

The unveiling would be his victory celebration.

When ministers and court officials said the king’s statue had been beautifully formed, the words tasted sweet to the sculptor for a moment. The next moment, they seemed to be praising only the king, and hunger arose in his mind.

“Your beauty appears exactly as it is, Majesty.”

“Do you not possess a broad chest? That is why the statue shines.”

“Many poets have turned the light in your eyes into verse. This young man has shown their beauty in stone.”

“The subject he received was exquisite, and the method of creation commendable.”

They praised the king and tossed only one or two words of praise towards the sculptor.

“The sculptors will come tomorrow. Only they can offer a true assessment,” he thought. The next moment came another thought: “But they...”

One would say, “It must have taken four days to carve the eyes.”

Another would reply, “Why four days? I carved King Narendra’s eyes in four hours.”

A third would affectionately call him “younger brother” and ask, “Was it evening when you carved the ear ornament?”

Would not each sculptor shape his praise merely to display his own knowledge and skill?

The king was delighted. But was he delighted by the artistic achievement, or by the thought that his stone form had conquered time and future generations would know his greatness?

The queen would certainly rejoice. But would she keep praising his craftsmanship? She would caress the stone image, saying: “My darling, this is a speaking portrait for the townspeople and for me.”

An indescribable sadness enveloped the sculptor.

To lessen his distress and escape the heat, he wandered through flower gardens all evening and sought fountains.

Whatever he saw told him beauty existed everywhere. There were blossom and bud upon the same creeper; there was a rooster courting a hen. What mischief lived in that maiden’s glance.

Everywhere were paintings of beauty, uncarved sculptures. There was no chisel and no sleepless night, yet there were images that gave art its ideas.

“What does my handiwork amount to?” he thought and sank into grief.

Darkness slowly spread. On the very day that should have brought incomparable joy, an indescribable sorrow possessed him.

He lay upon a rock and slept.

In his dream, his mother and father appeared. His mother scolded him for breaking stone and chasing time instead of attending to fields and forests. His father beat him with a small cane.

“A great sculptor indeed! Shame! Son of a pauper, find a way to earn your living,” he threatened.

They had gone to a country beyond his sight, cursing their son as useless.

What had become of them? He did not know.

He had become a sculptor. Would his spreading fame reach wherever they were, or had they attained a state in which no news mattered? Who knew?

* * *

“Poor man, he is exhausted,” said an old man.

“Has he no home? Why is he sleeping upon rough ground?” asked the old man’s granddaughter.

The sculptor awoke.

He noticed the maiden standing with bowed head in shyness, casting her spear-like eyes sideways at him.

“Sir, who are you? Is this your garden? Have I entered without permission? I fell asleep from exhaustion,” he faltered.

The young woman laughed musically.

“Be quiet, child. Who are you, young man? Even tigers roam this way after dusk. Can you sleep here? Come. My palace is nearby,” said the elder, taking him home.

The sculptor wondered why palace cooks who received their wages in abundance could not prepare food so delicious. The chutney, fried vegetables, and stew touched by that maiden’s hand tasted exquisite.

He wished to say all manner of things, but for some reason her glance tied his tongue.

“Grandfather! Oh, grandfather!”

A veiled figure entered calling out.

Grandfather and granddaughter welcomed her. The newcomer became as still as a statue when she saw the sculptor, and he too looked at her in astonishment.

She was the king’s widowed sister.

“Child...” the old man began.

“Hush, do not speak,” warned the royal woman.

“How do you know this man?” she asked.

“We found him in the forest. The young man lost his way,” said the old man.

“The sculptor has arrived precisely where he needed to come,” she said teasingly, then looked at the elder’s granddaughter and smiled.

“Grandfather, I immediately need a ‘poison’ that will make me fall dead when mixed with milk and drunk,” said the royal beauty.

The sculptor was startled.

“Sculptor, you have lost a good opportunity. Had you learned of this four days earlier, you might have turned me into stone too. Tomorrow, or after midnight tonight, I shall no longer exist.”

As she spoke, tears poured from her eyes.

“Young man, what is this?”

“I know nothing of it.”

“Grandfather, he is the expert sculptor who beautifully fashioned my elder brother’s statue.”

“Yes, lady. What follows from that?”

“What follows? Through your artistic ability, everyone will behold that compassion-filled face. My brother’s fame will spread, and yours too.”

“Lady, mockery sounds in your words.”

“Sir, you have grown accustomed to hearing only the sound of the chisel upon stone. My pain has not reached your ears, nor have you seen my tears.

“Do you know whose statue your art has fashioned? My brother’s. Who is my brother? King of the country, a tiger in war, lover of art, respected by the people, and influential across many regions.”

“Yes, lady, yes.”

“But my brother is blind.”

“Lady!”

“Yes, sculptor. His eyes are open, yet he is blind. Not to everyone. He can see your statue and the treasury. He can see the fort, the bastions, the treasure house, and the worship room.

“Sculptor who has lived among stone all your life, he can see me too. But he is blind to the child growing in my womb.”

“What suffering is this, mother?”

“I am a widow, sir. A widow. It is not my fault.

“To prevent his crown from being seized, my brother gave me in marriage to an aged rich man who led the conspirators.

“Yes, it was a magnificent wedding. My eyes became pools while the wealthy drank and danced. Delighted that his position was now secure, my brother strolled with his wife upon a marble terrace in the milk-white moonlight.

“I was trapped with a lust-blinded old man who tottered with a head like milk foam. He said, ‘Ah!’ Even my cry of ‘Alas!’ sounded sweet to him.

“It took me a little time to understand how to live as an old man’s wife. By then my beloved returned from the battlefield.

“Who is he? The warrior who was assigned to assist you.”

“Yes, the man who made the lotus of my heart bloom. He remains merely a warrior because he possesses no throne. I carry the fruit of his love.”

“After you became a widow?”

“Man who knows only the surface, when was I truly married? I became a widow when an old man underwent a wedding and later died.

“You carved eyes overflowing with compassion, which poets and novelists will admire tomorrow. Not one drop of compassion arose in those eyes.

“I fell at my brother’s feet and shed tears. I showed him the treasure growing in my womb and asked permission to remarry.

“I pleaded: ‘If that would disgrace this realm, allow us to run away. It is enough if you do not send your dogs to chase and capture us.’”

“What did the king say, lady? What did he say?”

“The king spoke royal justice. He threw my Kathiravan into prison. After tomorrow, or four days from now, once the festival is over, he is to be beheaded.”

“What injustice. What wickedness.”

“Tomorrow they will praise the beautiful statue and its majesty. Tonight I must become a corpse.”

“Sister, you need not become a corpse. I ask you to live and rejoice when the man who erred seeks atonement. I shall find a way to rescue your beloved and arrange your marriage. You need only change your decision to end your life.”

“Yes, sister, trust his word,” said the granddaughter.

“Child, suicide is a grave sin,” said the elder.

“Not only must you avoid suicide; destroying the child in the womb would be a greater injustice than all the rest,” said the sculptor.

“Make a promise, lady.”

“What should I promise?”

“That you will not kill yourself.”

“Sister, trust him.”

“Very well, Marutham.”

After this conversation, the sculptor hurried back to town.

The king was conversing with friends.

The sound of a chisel rang out. A man was sent to investigate. The sculptor told him he had forgotten a very important detail and would complete it before dawn.

* * *

“I am immeasurably delighted to have received the privilege of unveiling the statue of our sovereign Vezhamugathar.

“The master sculptor Chandanathevan, worthy of being called a king among artists, created this statue of our noble Vezhamugathar, a jewel among kings.

“This image will not only display the perfection of art; it will surely demonstrate the noble manner in which kings must rule and bring comfort to their people.”

After delivering this speech, Swarnalinga Guru, head of the Somesar monastery, drew aside the curtain.

Regional rulers, chief ministers, officials, and sculptors applauded and cried, “Long live King Vezhamugathar!” Then they looked at the statue.

It appeared blind in both eyes. The chisel had cut them into two deep cavities.

Everyone panicked.

“Alas! Who did this? What villainy! Where is the sculptor? What is this appearance?” people shouted in many ways.

The sculptor Chandanathevan stood without agitation.

“Why do you panic? The king is blind. Only after learning that did I make this correction to the statue,” he said fearlessly.

“Has madness seized him?” cried the ministers.

“Seize him and bind him to the post,” ordered the king.

“No, Majesty. Throw me into the prison cell where Kathiravan is held. But first ask whether the correction I made to your statue was wrong, and I shall answer.

“I am an artist, not a hired labourer. Art may accept imagination, but concealing truth is not art.

“Throughout the many days and nights when I carved the statue, I too was blind. That is why I did not know you were blind. Only last night did my eyes open, and I learned that you were blind.”

“Give him ten lashes, until he falls curled upon the ground,” ordered the king.

“Give me a dagger. After I die, they may conduct the lashing ceremony,” said the king’s sister as she entered the assembly.

The courtiers looked alternately at her and the sculptor with suspicion.

“She is my sister, fools. She is my widowed sister, senseless men, a widow. But she is an unfortunate woman struggling upon the threshold of motherhood,” said the sculptor.

The assembly erupted.

The king tried repeatedly to dissolve it but could not. Even the ranks of soldiers gathered to hear the sculptor.

“Yes, I am blind,” the king cried with a stricken heart. “Social restrictions and false honour blinded my eyes. Chandanathevan was entirely right to make me blind.”

He wiped away the tears of his sister, who had fallen at his feet.

“I shall arrange her marriage to Kathiravan today, here, this very moment,” he said.

The ceremonial hall became a wedding hall.

“Where is the fine chisel? Where is my fine chisel?” Chandanathevan cried joyfully.

Taking the small chisel from his assistant, he worked for a few seconds near the eyes of the statue and removed the blind coverings he had newly carved there.

The statue shone without loss of beauty. Its eyes gleamed.

With tears filling his eyes, the king embraced the sculptor.

“My eyes have opened,” he said.

Was this not the true opening ceremony?

9 October 1955

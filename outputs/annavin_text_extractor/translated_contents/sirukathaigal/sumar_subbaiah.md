## English Translation

“What time was it when you set out?”

“It would have been approximately eleven, sir.”

“How far away was the place you visited?”

“It would be about three miles, sir.”

“What time is it now?”

“Approximately one o’clock, I think, sir.”

The master could contain his anger no longer.

“Man, are you a complete blockhead? Whatever I ask, the answer is ‘approximately, approximately.’ Is that an answer? Does a single definite, sharply cut, correct answer ever come out of your mouth? Your head has gone grey, and looking at you arouses pity. Yet your conduct is neither correct, satisfactory, nor of any quality.”

“How old are you?”

“Approximately fifty-two, sir.”

“Must even that be the accursed ‘approximately’? This has become a great affliction. If I asked how many arms and legs you had, you would probably say ‘approximately two.’ I cannot wrestle with you any longer. I truly mean it: you are severely testing my patience.

“Go! Stop weeping out ‘approximately, approximately.’ Your answer must be exact. Do you understand? Go, go! Half my life is gone trying to herd crooked creatures like you.”

Those acquainted with the details knew that landed property, gardens and groves, a cloth shop, and the trusteeship of a temple had all rolled together into one human shape named Velayudham Pillai, a prominent man of the village.

The elderly man who said he endured “sufferings unknown even to a dog” in trying to become worthy of Velayudham’s confidence was Subbaiah. The villagers had conferred upon him the special title “Approximately Subbaiah” because he answered every question with “approximately.”

It was not merely a habit but training. Subbaiah had acquired both habit and training at the place where he worked before joining Velayudham. His former master was named Muthusamy.

Muthusamy once sent Subbaiah to buy a cow. When Subbaiah reported the quantity it yielded at each milking, the master asked four times: “Tell me properly. That much at every milking? Is that right?”

“Yes, sir. It gives exactly that amount at each milking. I am telling you. I know what I am saying,” Subbaiah explained.

Once purchased, the cow proved reluctant to yield even about one measure in an entire day. The master’s anger became unbearable.

“Subba! Come here!” he called threateningly. When Subbaiah approached, the master twisted his ear.

“You named a precise measure as though you had measured it yourself. Go and look at the quality of the cow and the meaning of your speech. You did not even say it would give approximately one measure. You deceived me by speaking as though you had taken the exact measurement.”

From then on, Subbaiah became afraid when speaking to his master and began adding “approximately” to everything. It grew into habit and training. How could that life disappear after he came to work for Velayudham? He continued attaching “approximately” to every statement, thereby increasing his new master’s anger.

“If you know something definitely and correctly, say so. Otherwise say it may approximately be like this. Do you understand?” his former master had taught him.

It took time to learn and master that lesson. Once he became proficient and such speech formed part of his nature, the new master ordered: “Abandon it. State everything definitely and correctly.” The new master, who was also a relative, even paid ten rupees more. Yet Subbaiah’s relationship with the word “approximately” refused to dissolve easily.

Despite everything said that morning, Velayudham called Subbaiah that evening and presented him to a visitor.

“This is the Subbaiah I mentioned. He is related to me as an elder uncle. We all come through the Sholingapuram line,” he told the visitor.

Then he asked Subbaiah, “Your son is handsome, is he not?”

What answer could Subbaiah give but, “He is approximately so, sir”?

The visitor owned ten acres and had come seeking a suitable husband for his daughter. Even then came that accursed “approximately.”

“Go attend to your work,” the master said with visible agitation and sent him away.

The poor man had spoken only out of a good intention: he wanted Subbaiah’s son to obtain a match from a prosperous family. “Approximately” arrived and ruined his plan.

There was another reason why the word had become habitual to Subbaiah. The truth must be told. He cared deeply about speaking only the truth and only after knowing something correctly. Alongside this was the thought: “What do we know? How could we know?”

That sinful cattle broker had quoted a precise milk yield. Without checking whether it was correct or asking him to demonstrate the measure, Subbaiah had babbled to his master like an all-knowing scholar. His master suffered a loss, and he himself suffered hardship. Thinking about it repeatedly, he concluded that nothing should be stated categorically. The result was this “approximately.”

After the visitor left, Velayudham called Subbaiah.

“Alas, you will never prosper. The Sholingapuram man had half agreed to give his daughter to your son. It is ruined. Your ‘approximately’ came and destroyed it.”

Subbaiah felt sad. But how could he say his son was handsome? To his own eyes, the boy certainly looked handsome. Who knew what the master thought? Without knowing that, how could he give a categorical answer? The master had instructed him how to speak, yet now became angry. What could he do?

Muttering thus, he went home and lay down sorrowfully.

Kuppusamy was younger than Subbaiah, yet look how cleverly he conducted himself in the cloth shop. The master saw that too. What use was Subbaiah’s modest nature, however hard he tried? Look at Kuppusamy.

“Young man, how is the dye?”

“Can you come to our shop and ask such a question?”

With an explosive laugh, Kuppusamy would place the sari in a paper wrapper, hand it to the customer, take the pencil from behind his ear, write a bill for 17.30, and attend to the next customer.

“Young man, the dye runs from saris bought at your shop.”

“There is no defect in our shop’s sari, madam. Something is wrong with the place where you had it laundered. Are we responsible for that?”

“We did not send it anywhere. We washed it ourselves at home.”

“What soap did you use?”

“Malli Brand soap.”

“Now you have said it. I was bewildered, because it is such a fast dye. How could it run? Madam, paying a price and buying a sari is not enough. The soap must be of good quality too. Wait. Boy! Kittu! Buy a box of Alli Brand soap and bring it for madam. Give him three rupees, madam. Use this and see whether the dye bleeds.”

The customers left satisfied. Kuppusamy was the agent for Alli Brand soap throughout that village. One stone, two mangoes.

Subbaiah watched all this with his mouth open in astonishment. He could not understand how Kuppusamy had learned so many tricks. He thought he could never acquire that skill. More than that, he thought he did not want such “skill”; it was sinful and disgraceful.

Subbaiah’s nature caused trouble at many stages of his life. Once it even placed him in danger.

Sigappan was the village’s great troublemaker. One day, he created a disturbance in the shop and attacked the master. He also tried to stab him with the scissors there.

A case was tried. The lawyer representing Sigappan was an expert at threatening witnesses and leaving them bewildered. Whenever cases involved assault, stabbing, or arson, he stood foremost in defending the accused. Whether he won or not, crowds gathered at court to watch his fireworks.

Kuppusamy alone would have been enough to testify about Sigappan’s attack. He could handle himself. But on the day of the disturbance, Kuppusamy had gone to Koradiyur for the consecration ceremony of his family deity.

Subbaiah was the principal person present in the shop that day. The master warned his lawyer: “Do not put this man in the witness box. He will pour out nonsense and ruin the case.” Even Subbaiah felt relieved by that arrangement.

But the police report recorded that Subbaiah had been in the shop. Sigappan’s lawyer, Kazhugasalam, sniffed this out and brought Subbaiah into the witness box. Subbaiah trembled.

“Your name is Subbaiah?”

“Yes.”

“How many years have you worked there?”

“Approximately seven or eight, sir.”

“Your master is a good man, is he not?”

“Gold, sir. A man of pure gold.”

“Do not say that loudly. This is a period of gold controls.”

“Do you receive sufficient wages?”

“Approximately...”

“Is it not sinful to betray the household that feeds you?”

“It certainly is sinful, sir.”

“Must one not remember throughout life those who gave one salt?”

“That alone is justice and dharma, sir.”

“Therefore, you would not betray your master.”

“How could my heart permit it?”

“You care about whatever benefits your master.”

“That is true, sir.”

“That is why I say you have come here to give false testimony on his behalf in return for the wages he pays you.”

“Do not say that. I do not lie, sir. I do not know how. I have never had the habit.”

“Your true form will soon be known. You say the disturbance occurred in the shop. What time was it?”

“The time came, sir... It may have been twelve... approximately.”

“Look here, man. This is a court. You must speak the truth. You may not drag out your words or evade the answer. What time was it?”

“Approximately twelve.”

“Is there any calculation called ‘approximately twelve’? Tell us correctly. Had the clock struck twelve? Was there a quarter of an hour left before twelve? Had half an hour passed after it struck?”

“How can I say that? It would have been approximately twelve.”

“Did you look at a clock?”

“No, sir.”

“Can you know the time without even looking at a clock?”

“I felt slightly hungry. From that I say it would have been approximately twelve.”

“Oh! Your clock is inside your stomach. Very well. You did not state the time after looking at a clock?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Therefore, you do not know definitely what time the disturbance occurred.”

“You swear an oath in court and speak of a matter you do not definitely know. Is that proper? Let it pass. An assault occurred, did it not?”

“He came, sir. Sigappan, Sigappan...”

“This is not a religious discourse. Answer the question. Did an assault occur?”

“It did, sir.”

“Who struck whom?”

“Sigappan attacked the master.”

“With both hands or one hand?”

“I did not notice that. I heard slap, slap.”

“Were you not frightened when the blows fell?”

“I was afraid. My whole body trembled. My arms and legs would not move. I closed my eyes tightly.”

“When your eyes were closed, how can you say Sigappan struck your master?”

“I heard the sound.”

“Did you see it with your eyes?”

“They say Sigappan came to stab him with scissors.”

“Yes, sir. The master cried out in alarm, ‘Kathiri! Kathiri!’”

“He cried ‘Kathiri, Kathiri,’ but did he say, ‘Sigappan is coming to stab me with scissors’?”

“He did not say that, sir.”

“Are there boys in that shop who carry and stack bundles?”

“There are three, sir.”

“Is one of them called Kathiri?”

“Yes, sir.”

“I say that what you heard was your master calling his employee Kathiri to strike Sigappan. What do you say?”

“What can I say, sir? I heard my master shout.”

“Your eyes were closed, you did not know the time, and a sound fell upon your ears. You thought that was enough and came to testify?”

“I beat my head and said I would not come, sir.”

The case ended in Sigappan’s favour. Subbaiah lost his job.

At home, his wife said with great distress: “You must understand everything clearly and state it definitely. Unless you do that, who will employ you?”

“It is true, Kannatha. I understand. But it must become a habit. From now on, I shall speak only after learning everything definitely. That alone will be my work hereafter. You will see,” he reassured her.

From that day, he kept thinking of the same thing and began practising that method.

Six months later, he obtained work under a man named Karmegam.

“Subbaiah, go and collect the outstanding amount owed by Kanniyappan in Ponniyamman Temple Street,” Karmegam would say.

Subbaiah would immediately take out a small notebook, look at the clock, and record 10:25. He would step down from the shop, then return.

“How much is the outstanding balance?” he would ask.

The shop accountant would say, “Come here, man! You ask for the exact account as though Kanniyappan will pay the moment you go. Go and ask him to pay what he owes.”

Subbaiah would not leave it.

“No, sir. I must know the account exactly. If he asks how much money is due, I must be able to tell him.”

The accountant would consult the ledger and say it was eighty rupees and sixty paise. Subbaiah would write that in his notebook and set out.

Halfway there, a thought would occur to him: “They said only Ponniyamman Temple Street. I forgot to ask the house number.”

He would question many people and find the house. His first act would be to take out his notebook and record the house number.

He would not immediately go inside. He needed a method of proving later, if necessary, that he had come there and entered Kanniyappan’s house. Only when someone known to him passed would he record that person’s name. Then he would go inside and speak to Kanniyappan.

“Are you from Karmegam’s shop?”

“Yes.”

“Are you the accountant?”

“Yes.”

“Have you come to collect the outstanding balance?”

“Yes.”

“How do I know you are really a man from Karmegam’s shop? Is it written upon your forehead?”

Only a Kuppusamy could have answered that. Subbaiah would return to the shop, resolved that whenever he went to collect a debt in future, he must first obtain a letter of identification from the master.

In this way, he began acting with elaborate advance preparation in every matter. The practice matured and became Subbaiah’s nature. As a result, no task was completed without delay.

“Another man flies like an arrow and finishes the work. You too call yourself a man, yet you are like a tortoise. For everything you must know the details and write them in a notebook. Shame! You will never prosper.”

With these words, Karmegam too dismissed Subbaiah from his job.

## English Translation

It was not arduous labour; I could not call it difficult, for people would laugh if I did. What I held in my hand was neither a crowbar nor a spade, but a pen. There was no bundle or pan of earth on my head; I wore a hat. A coat covered my body. I held an office job. Yet my heart felt like iron caught inside a furnace.

It was not merely ceaseless anxiety and unending trouble. The trouble grew from day to day, and no effort of mine could lessen it.

When I entered the service of Vaiduriyam Chettiar, my father's old school friend, I never imagined that my heart would be wrung in this fashion. He owned Nandeeswarar Mills, chaired the municipal council, patronized the city Congress committee, served as trustee of the Kumaresar temple, was vice-president of the cooperative society and secretary of the Poor Relief Fund.

To this day he has never spoken harshly to me. At times he even calls me "younger brother." When extremely angry, the worst he says is, "You are a thoroughly foolish boy. I do not know how you will ever make your way in life."

When I say that I "worked" for a man who treated me with such respect, I do not use the expression carelessly. His labourers did the actual work. My work was to see whether they did it properly. Others worked; I supervised them.

"The astrologer has declared firmly that the boy does not seem likely to pass his B.A. That is why I have brought him to you. Put him into something or other. Who else do I have?" It took my father fifteen minutes to say this to Vaiduriyam Chettiar because he suffered from an asthmatic cough.

After thinking for a long time, the Chettiar said, "Very well. Let the young man remain with us. When an opportunity appears, we shall find him a place in a bank, the municipality or somewhere. At present there are no vacancies in any of those places."

"Do whatever you can. From now on he is like your own son," my father said, entrusting me to him.

At the time, the Chettiar was constructing a lodging complex named *Mathur Bhoomi*. The municipal council, recognizing that visitors from other towns suffered for want of accommodation, had passed a resolution. If wealthy men came forward to build lodgings and rent them out, the council would sell them vacant municipal land at a low price.

When Vaiduriyam Chettiar implemented this resolution, the newspapers praised his spirit of "public service." Vacant land was bought for a very low price, and construction was undertaken by Parthasarathi Chettiar, Vaiduriyam Chettiar's second son-in-law. Could a father-in-law decline to help his son-in-law? Vaiduriyam Chettiar supplied the plans for the lodging complex and chose the name *Mathur Bhoomi*.

Sengamalam Pillai, leader of the municipal opposition, was a troublesome man. At a council meeting he asked, "The public says that although the vacant municipal land was bought cheaply in Parthasarathi Chettiar's name, it really belongs to the council chairman, Vaiduriyam Chettiar. Is that true?"

"It is true," replied the Chettiar from the chairman's seat.

The opposition leader sprang up furiously. "Is this your spirit of public service?" he demanded.

"Pillai, sit down. Why become agitated without understanding what I said? You asked whether it was true that the public was saying I had bought the municipal land. I said it was true: the public is indeed saying so. What does the public ever refrain from saying? They have even said that Sengamalam Pillai fell at voters' feet and worshipped them! Does something become sacred scripture merely because the public says it?

"The land was sold, and Parthasarathi Chettiar bought it. The record exists. Let mischief-makers talk until their mouths ache; the municipal council need not concern itself. The sale does not become invalid merely because Parthasarathi Chettiar is my son-in-law. Karpura Pillai, who has taken the contract to supply lime to *Mathur Bhoomi*, is your own younger brother."

The council members laughed. Sengamalam Pillai joined their laughter.

Plans called for a total of 130 lodgings at *Mathur Bhoomi*, and construction proceeded rapidly. Vaiduriyam Chettiar made me manager of the building works. My duties were to record when the labourers arrived and left, note how much work they completed, pay wages and keep accounts, and ensure that the work was properly performed.

One may say there was no hardship in this. Cutting, digging, carrying and grinding are the difficult tasks. "All you do is count and record those who arrive and see that they work properly. What hardship is there in that?" one might ask. I too thought this way when I took the job. Only after starting work there did I discover the pain of my life.

The building foreman told me, "The Chettiar struck a magnificent bargain when he bought this vacant land. Even if he sold it immediately, someone would pay three times the price."

The man delivering bricks said that his position as municipal chairman had enabled him to seize the land very cheaply.

When trucks arrived and unloaded iron girders, a driver remarked, "No matter how severe the scarcity, goods continue to reach those who know how to obtain them."

I asked which company had supplied them.

"Company, indeed! All of it was bought at a military auction," he replied.

Everyone who came there spoke of the plunderous profit Vaiduriyam Chettiar would make from *Mathur Bhoomi*. The labourers heard such remarks, said, "What luck!" and returned to their tasks. I could not do the same. My mind filled with thoughts.

He found profit in so many ways and was nevertheless regarded as a great man of the town. A man who amassed money by such means received honours at the temple, a place in the national organization and the municipal chair. "What deluded people!" I thought in sorrow.

Had I been lifting a crowbar, exhaustion might have kept such ideas away. Because I was the man "watching the work," I had leisure to think. That thought was destroying my peace of mind.

"Hey, you hulking fellow! How long will you take to move that sack? Hurry! Do not crawl like a tortoise. He will be here soon."

"Hey, girl! Why are you walking like a swan? Lift that pan and climb quickly. Look above; he is shouting for lime!"

"Foreman, that is enough cement. The Chettiar has ordered strictly that you use no more than half a sack. Do not waste material."

I was the manager, and these were my daily orders. At first I felt happy and even a little proud to intimidate so many people and extract work from them. As the days passed, the task became bitter.

These poor people laboured here while I hounded them without allowing a minute's rest. I knew that their wages were insufficient to sustain their lives. Whole families came to work. They laid their children to sleep under trees, ate sour gruel and laboured until dusk. I squeezed every drop of work from them and, when they went home, paid them according to several grades, from one rupee and six annas down to one and a quarter annas.

Vaiduriyam Chettiar calculated that *Mathur Bhoomi* would bring him 13,000 rupees each month. The workers knew that their labour was creating that permanent monthly income for him. Yet their minds were wholly occupied by work. They had no time to think, "We toil and suffer so greatly while another man takes the plunderous profit."

I did not carry a pan of lime. Instead, I carried the beautiful ideas of freedom, happiness, equality and social justice. College life had given me this burden to bear. Those ideas burrowed into me.

Before my eyes I saw people labouring like cattle. My job was to make certain that they worked hard enough, and I was paid for doing so. Imagine my condition. My heart had not gone numb. Nor had I forgotten the ideas Marx and others had instilled in me. I saw clearly that the labourer suffered while the man who drained him ruled the town. I lacked the power to stop it, yet could not dismiss it by saying, "What is it to me?"

I became like a little mouse caught in a terrifying machine. The machine keeps turning and objects trapped beneath it are ground to powder. The mouse has not fallen below the wheel; it clings to one of the spokes. What can it do? That was my position.

The workers were being crushed in Vaiduriyam Chettiar's machine of exploitation. I understood the cruelty of exploitation, yet had been appointed guard of the machine. What a condition was mine! I drowned in anguish.

One day, without a labourer's knowledge, I tried lifting the sack he carried. The resulting sprain took three days to heal. Yet every day I had to rebuke that man: "You hulking fellow! Lift the sack quickly. Do not crawl like a tortoise."

It had already been painful to say it. After lifting the sack myself, ordering him became as distressing as being forced to touch red-hot iron. What could I do? I was the manager and had to perform my duty.

I placed a pan of lime on my head and tried climbing the ladder. I fell at the fifth rung. Yet every day I had to tell the woman who climbed it, "Do not walk like a swan. Hurry!" How can I describe the pain I felt when I said that?

I grew frightened on hearing the labourers' sighs. Watching sweat pour from their bodies brought tears to my eyes. I understood the suffering of those poor people who laboured merely to feed themselves, but knew no way to change it.

They worked until their chests seemed ready to break. I was the man who extracted the work, though they laboured neither for me nor for themselves, but for Vaiduriyam Chettiar. They worked to increase his wealth; I supervised them to ensure their labour served that end properly. The more this thought arose, the greater my agony became.

A torture was taking place. We sharpened the knife for it. Exploitation proceeded, and we oiled the wheels so that they would turn smoothly. Capitalism thirsted to drink the labourer's blood, and our work was to measure that blood out to the capitalist system, so much to the measure, so much to the smaller measure. We practised the trade of driving sheep to the butcher.

What a degrading occupation, filled with blame and sin! It betrayed the working class. Was this the work I should perform? Better to become a labourer myself. Better to join those who suffered the cruelty than watch it and endure, or become its accomplice.

Such thoughts came in endless forms. I sought peace and tried in every way to relieve the anguish, but could not. The pain in my heart rose like flame.

As I set out each morning, I thought, "How can I go to that field where humanity is destroyed? How can I see it and remain silent?" Yet on arriving, I could not simply let the work take its course. I had to extract labour; that was the duty assigned to me.

The Chettiar came each evening to inspect whether I had done my job properly. I therefore had to keep berating those pitiable people: "Work! Work! Work! Hurry! Hurry!" Every time I did so, it felt as though a sledgehammer struck my heart.

"Younger brother! He is a human being, not a bullock. Have you no eyes? You stand in the shade because the sun is fierce, with shoes on your feet, yet threaten only him with 'Hurry, hurry!' and drive him to work. Is that just?"

"It is not just. It is cruelty, and I know it. But what can I do? I must extract work from them. That is my job."

"Do you hear that sigh? He lifted a burden too heavy to carry. That is why he sighed. Despite this labour, he will not receive enough food to fill his stomach."

"It is true. What can I do?"

"Before long your eyes will grow accustomed to these dreadful sights. Those sighs will become ordinary sounds to your ears. Your heart will lose all feeling. Your thoughts will harden until they extend no further than yourself and your own life. You are still something of a human being now. In a little while, you too will become a machine."

"Alas! What shall I do?"

"True, poor fellow. Your condition is pitiable. You are caught and crushed between two machines: capitalism is the grinding machine, and the workers are human-shaped objects placed inside it. You suffer in the position of making sure that the machine and its material work together properly."

"Yes. I take the parrot and deliver it to the place where the cat waits."

"The comparison is apt. But your heart will not permit it, will it?"

"No. What shall I do? Shall I run away?"

"Where will you run?"

"Anywhere that this horror will not meet my eyes."

"No such place exists. Wherever you go, you will see the same spectacle, except in Russia."

"Then I shall go there."

"If you go, will these sighs and this sweat here cease?"

"No. Can they be stopped?"

"They can."

"How?"

"Read. There are many methods. Read and learn."

This was the battle within my mind, sketched above. To lessen my anguish, I began to read. Reading brought me still greater anguish.

Books spoke of civilization, paths to a good life, the dawn of a new age and countless other matters. I read works about the ideal of happiness. I read puranas and epics distinguishing this world from the next. What benefit did I obtain? I learned that generation after generation the working people had remained in the same condition. My pain increased rather than diminished.

Why had humanity become enslaved? Why was it exploited? How could it be liberated? Was the method by which men like Vaiduriyam Chettiar appointed managers like me to torment workers the only system humanity had discovered after all this time? Was there no other?

Human beings who had once lived in caves developed their knowledge and built upper storeys as though they now lived near the sky. Those who once walked four or five miles invented the aeroplane, capable of travelling four or five hundred miles in an hour. Humanity discovered new methods in countless fields. But for living itself, had it found nothing beyond the ancient method by which the strong tormented the weak? Why?

These thoughts arose as I read. I wondered why scholars had not tried to change the condition of labourers. Ancient scholars had submerged themselves in thoughts of the other world. Those who came later dedicated their talents to art and glitter. They refused to heed the worker's voice. The earlier men's ears admitted only the music of the flute; the later heard only the jingling of anklets. There was no place for the labourer's sigh.

Anger toward Vaiduriyam Chettiar arose in me. Yet when he stood before me with a smiling face, it somehow flew away. Not merely my anger; I am ashamed to confess that even my principles fled.

He would argue directly against my convictions, and without realizing it I would nod my head.

"Younger brother, are all the men working properly?" the Chettiar would ask.

Properly? *Mathur Bhoomi*, rising in the open ground, showed that their very bodies were melting into the work. Yet he asked. I should have answered, "Is there any doubt? These beautiful lodgings arise only because of their immense labour. They work without rest. These building materials are not merely lime and stone, sir, but the workers' blood and flesh."

But when he asked, those words did not emerge. I said, "Not bad."

It was a lie I knew to be a lie, yet I spoke it without shrinking on many occasions. I, who watched their labour and suffered in my heart, nevertheless said only, "Not bad." Why? The very manner in which the Chettiar framed his question somehow placed that answer on my tongue.

"Nowadays workers never do their jobs properly," he would say.

Had his garden, mansion, mill and adjoining properties arisen because people failed to work properly? Yet he asked in that way, and I answered, "Yes." I could not understand why I said it. His question summoned that answer.

Just as a hissing cobra spreads its hood and dances to the sound of a little pipe, some questions of his drew from me answers contrary to my principles and hostile to the truth. He not only made labourers work; somehow he made me speak lies against my will.

My mind had spoken the whole truth when it said, "You are caught between two machines."

## Part 2

The labourers gave him their labour through suffering. I had pawned my very heart. I did it knowingly, though in circumstances I could not escape.

They say that a woman who has slipped and fallen sells herself to survive. They revile her for it and call her a stain and a sore upon society. I had sold my heart itself. I became a human machine that spoke whatever he wanted me to say. Yet people addressed me respectfully as "Manager of the *Mathur Bhoomi* Construction Office."

I resolved somehow to escape this terrible situation. I longed for some pretext. But he continued treating me with his customary affection. Could he not have become angry with me even once? He did not.

How could I say, "I am leaving; I do not wish to remain in this job"? If I did, he would ask, "Why? What hardship do you face?"

"Are the working hours too long?"

"Is the salary insufficient?"

"Has anyone complained about you?"

"Are you going to some other job?"

"Have I ever reprimanded you?"

Those were the questions he would ask. What answers could I give? None of these caused my anguish. On what ground, then, could I say I was resigning?

I remained caught in this agony for several days. At last, one day, I gathered my courage and said with great humility, "I wish to leave the job."

I expected a storm, but was disappointed. With a smile he said, "Yes, poor boy. After studying so much, naturally you dislike herding these fellows. You may begin work at the bank next month."

He spoke decisively and walked away.

The reason he had invented for my dislike of the job struck and felled me. But how could I tell him the truth?

I ought to have said, "Sir, you live as a rich man by exploiting poor workers. I dislike that system. My conscience will not permit me to assist it."

How could I? After I left college and wandered without work, while my father suffered from asthma, the Chettiar gave me refuge upon a single request and paid me sixty rupees a month. Would it be fair to tell him, "You live by exploitation"? Would it not wound him?

Thus the anger that had flared within me changed into sorrow. Anger itself might not have been so harmful; sorrow tortured me.

One day I heard my father speaking to the neighbour. Their conversation gave me a new fear.

"Your son is well, I trust? He works for the Chettiar, does he not?"

"Yes. Only after he obtained that job did I find peace. That blessed man pays him sixty rupees. He has also said that when the time comes, he himself will place the boy in a better post."

"He will. What does it cost him? With a thought he can create any number of jobs."

"You know my son's nature. He keeps to himself. He never lifts his bowed head. He behaves toward his employer with fear, devotion and loyalty."

"Very good. Let all that be. But he must also know how to prosper. You say his salary is sixty. How much does he receive as extra income?"

"Extra income?"

"Tell me plainly. He works for a great millionaire. Is earning a paltry sixty rupees anything remarkable? In places like that, the extra income is what matters."

"Why should I hesitate to tell you? In that way it is never less than a hundred rupees a month."

"Even a lie should sound plausible. Last month the brick-kiln owner alone had a bracelet made from three sovereigns. Altogether your household must have received at least four thousand by now."

"Shiva, Shiva! After every expense, only fifteen hundred remains. With that money I intend to arrange his marriage next month."

"You should. The gifts and cash contributions will exceed even the wedding expenses. Do not fear."

"God exists. Things will follow the path he opens. The boy is innocent and has no bad habits or bad company. Because of him, the family is finally finding a way to raise its head."

"Why does he remain awake so late at night?"

"He loves reading and studies all night. He will not become angry with a single person or speak a hasty word. He has never even opened his mouth to ask for extra income."

"Why should he ask? It is customary. They bring it to the house in the usual way."

"Exactly. Yet those working with him marvel that he has never so much as hinted to anyone that he wants extra income."

Let others' amazement rest. My own astonishment and terror on hearing this conversation were immense.

"Extra income"! What did that expression mean? A bribe!

Yes. Vaiduriyam Chettiar enjoyed the fruits of the labourers' work, and without my knowledge a share had come to me, the intermediary. What qualification did I possess to condemn the conduct of a Chettiar who lived by deceit?

Without my knowledge, my father had been accepting bribes from merchants supplying construction materials. I trembled when I learned the truth. I had assumed that the suppliers' smiles and respectful treatment of me meant nothing. My father's conversation illuminated their meaning.

What cruelty! What disgrace! What had become of my ideal? Whenever the timber merchant, lime seller and others made suggestive remarks, I had failed to realize that they referred to this "extra income." I had been an idealist; my father was a practical man.

They planned to marry me with this extra-income money and called it the path ordained by God. When I learned the truth, shame joined my pain.

I condemned Vaiduriyam Chettiar, yet was I to marry using bribe money? Shame upon such a life! The labourer struggled against hunger. I resolved not to assist in exploiting him, yet would I live on scraps scattered by the machine of exploitation? Was that not a degraded existence?

"I want neither the money nor the job!" I resolved.

That night I used a false key and took the three thousand rupees my father had saved. What was I to do? I intended to return it to each person from whom he had received it.

My father muttered in his sleep: "The boy is innocent. He has no bad habits. Somehow, because of him the family is raising its head." He was remembering his conversation with the neighbour.

At midnight I opened the box with a false key and stole the money, while in his sleep he called me innocent. My head spun.

What was I to do? My ideals called the money a symbol of sin. My father's muttering revealed the trust he placed in me and his longing for the family to rise. If I wished to remain a son, I must not take the money. If I wished to remain an idealist, I must not allow it to be used. I had to choose one before he woke.

On one side stood the sorrowful workers and the capitalism growing fat upon them, with a little figure gathering the scraps it threw. On the other, signs of joy appeared on my careworn father's face as he proudly spoke of me to neighbours. Between those two scenes stood I, ideals filling my mind, utterly bewildered. How can I describe my state?

My father turned toward me. I extinguished the lamp. He coughed. I silently closed the box and hid in a corner.

"Who is there?" he asked, not harshly but fearfully.

"Perhaps the lamp went out. Perhaps it was the wind," he thought, not knowing that I had put it out. He felt beneath his pillow. It was over. In a second or two he would find the matchbox. Then what would become of me?

He would ask, "Is that you? Why? What are you doing?" What could I say? What could I do?

"Kumarasami! Hey, Kumarasami!" My father called me. The matchbox held no match. There was darkness. The absence of light frightened him. He knew money was in the house and called me for help. We were the only two people there. Whom else could he call? But how could I answer, "Here I am, Father"?

"Hey! Kumarasami!" he called more loudly, peering into every part of the room. I stood beside the box.

"Who is there?" he screamed.

I threw his upper cloth toward him. As it reached his face, I leapt forward, covered his mouth, stuffed it with cloth, and tied his hands and feet. He was old; how could his strength match mine? He managed only two broken cries: "Alas! Thief!"

My father lay rolling on the floor, bound hand and foot. I stood beside him with the bundle of money: the son in the role of thief, the father in the state of one robbed.

I looked at the money and at his writhing body. Tears rose without my knowledge. What could I do? Carrying the money, I left the room and entered the hall. My legs tangled beneath me. My head throbbed and my chest ached.

Then came a rapid pounding at the door. I hid again, inside my own house.

"Break it! Split it! Climb onto the tiled roof!" Many voices sounded outside.

"Kumarasami! Hey, Kumarasami!" they called.

"Where has he gone, leaving the old man alone?" It was the neighbour's voice.

They climbed onto the roof. I remained hidden. Lamps spread their light. Within minutes many people would enter. I hid with the bundle of money while my father lay bound and rolled up inside.

I thought I might walk out boldly and ask, "Why? What is it? I fell asleep. Why all this noise?"

They might reply, "What kind of sleep is this? We have been pounding the door so long." They might ask whether I was drunk. They would not stop there. They would call for my father. How could he rise and come? If they found him, what would they think of me?

"Alas!" A scream escaped me despite myself.

"What is it, younger brother?" several men asked. They had bravely jumped inside and opened the street door. I knew nothing of what happened after that.

I slowly opened my eyes. Everything appeared dim, and there was no clarity in my mind. I closed and opened them repeatedly and turned my head from side to side. I saw my father cooling gruel for me. I lacked the courage to look at him and closed my eyes again.

"Ekambara Mudali, how is the boy?" the neighbour asked.

"The fever has not fallen. He opened his eyes only now and closed them again," my father replied.

"It has been two days."

"Yes. They say it is a wicked spirit, and that when it calls a man he rarely survives. Somehow, through the merit of my worship, the boy's life escaped danger." Affection filled my father's voice.

"What a marvel it was! To rise at midnight, bind your legs and stuff cloth into your mouth..." The neighbour recalled the events of that night.

"That was the spirit's mischief. That is why he tied me up and then himself cried 'Kaa-oo!' and threw the whole town into confusion," said my father.

"You were fortunate. What would you have done if the spirit's mischief had killed you?" the neighbour asked.

My father laughed. "All of you were badly frightened, were you not?"

"It churned my very belly! First I heard your voice crying, 'Alas, thief!' At once my stomach gave a sudden stab. I had only just returned from the garden after washing my feet. When you shouted 'Thief,' I ran and woke the man in the house opposite.

"We called, 'Ekambaram! Ekambaram!' but heard no voice. We called Kumarasami, but received no answer. Then fear seized us. We brought a lamp and knocked, but the door did not open. We climbed over from the tiled-roof side and entered. There was complete darkness.

"While we wondered what had happened, Kumarasami screamed 'Alas!' We raised the lamp and approached. Foam and froth poured from his mouth, and your son lay on the floor like a felled tree, with a bundle near his head.

"We carried him into the hall, laid him down and told someone to sprinkle water on his face. Then I searched for you and found you rolled up with your hands and feet tied. Shame! I have never seen anyone possessed by a spirit in this fashion. Find a good exorcist and cure him quickly," the neighbour advised before leaving.

My father came staggering toward me. "Kumarasami, open your eyes. Drink a little gruel," he said.

They believed a spirit had possessed me and that everything that happened that night was its mischief.

I did not wish to open my eyes, but my father would not relent. As I drank the gruel, I wondered whether he too believed, like the townspeople, that possession caused my conduct, or whether he suspected something else.

Wiping the sweat from my forehead with his upper cloth, he said, "Foolish boy! Why did you have to do such a thing? The money is yours. If you had asked, I would have given it to you. Instead, in the middle of the night you made me suffer, disturbed the whole town and brought trouble upon yourself."

I woke fully and stared in terror. The townspeople believed I was possessed. My father had concluded that I was a thief. I no longer understood who I was.

A thief? Anyone had the right to call a man a thief who opened a box at midnight with a false key and bound his waking father.

The townspeople said it was certainly a spirit's mischief because I had tied up my father, stood half-naked crying "Ah! Ooh!" in the middle of the night, and fainted like a rootless palmyra falling to the ground.

Both verdicts were wrong. Yet who would believe me? I did not know what proof I could offer.

Those three thousand rupees of "extra income" were not property that rightfully belonged to me. They belonged to others and resembled stolen goods. I did not want them, but they reached me and reduced me to this condition. In that sense, it was indeed a haunting.

Looked at another way, both names fitted me: thief and possessed man. But neither was the whole truth.

I was a tormented idealist. I was a little bird within a cage, beating its wings against the iron bars in an attempt to escape, until the unmoving bars broke its wings.

How many others are there like me?

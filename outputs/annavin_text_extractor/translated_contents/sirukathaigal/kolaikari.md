## English Translation

“Murderess!” the law accuses her. From the wisest to those lacking wisdom, everyone speaks with one voice: “Murderess.”

She killed children. Not one or two, but three.

Were they children born to someone else? No. They were her own, three children formed and protected within her precious womb.

She was the mother. Her three children were killed, and they were killed by her own hand. Cruelty, everyone declares unanimously.

The mother who raised those three children without letting a fly approach, an ant bite or cold strike; who understood their needs and nursed them at the proper times; who gave medicine when their digestion failed and sought a doctor when illness came: that mother killed them. She became a murderess.

This is no amusing invention, but a painful true occurrence. It happened at Tenkasi.

Did she punish children who had committed some wrong? No. She was not angry with them. Her attachment to them, her tenderness and the love she bore them made her a murderess.

“How did the sinner find the heart to do it?” ask those who imagine compassion belongs to them alone. That is their certificate of virtue.

“She killed the children she bore. Was she a mother, or a monstrous demon?” some ask.

“Hanging is too ordinary for her. She must be tortured. Only that punishment suits this wicked woman!” pronounce those who imagine the responsibility for justice belongs to them.

“Yes, I am a murderess. How can I deny it? Only moments before, these forms spoke. Those little eyes shed drops of water. The soft sound ‘hunger’ rose from those tiny mouths. Their arms and legs moved like green leaves creeping through a gentle wind.

“Was I not the woman who ended all that in an instant? Am I not the sinner who turned speaking dolls into corpses? How, then, could I be unworthy of the title murderess?”

Thus even the accused woman thinks.

“I made corpses of the three children I bore. Yes, call me murderess, cruel woman, demon, or any other name. I am guilty, and punishment must be given.

“But... I made corpses of my children because of my love for them. The same love that drove me to protect them from flies and ants drove this act. The same attachment that inspired my joy when they were born caused it.

“Will the world understand? The people of this world cannot know the nature of the motherhood hidden within my heart. Knowing a mother’s responsibility and duty, I forced myself to seek permanent peace for my children.

“I am a mother, not the demon others think and call me. Let people ignorant of the truth concealed in my act, its purity and the motherhood established through it say whatever they please. I do not care.”

So spoke her heart.

The voice of condemnation that today reviles her as “murderess” belonged to the same society that withheld wisdom and compassion when her unemployed husband knelt before everyone he saw and begged for work.

It did not think: “Poor man! He is father to three children and must protect them. Let us save him and his family by giving him work.” Instead it said, “Let him go wherever he pleases, decay and perish. Why should we care?”

Only when it saw three small corpses before its eyes and learned that the foolish woman who made them corpses was their own mother did society claim a right to condemn her and call her murderess.

He described the cruelty of unemployment. “Go away, brute!” society snarled and fell upon him.

He displayed his belly, withered and dried, to plead his case. “Useless fellow, seek another place!” society snapped.

He begged for work, saying he had a wife. “As though a wife were some great matter!” society sneered.

He explained his responsibility for three young children wasting from hunger and sought work. Did society show compassion even then? No. But its tongue lengthens readily to lecture about justice.

The children’s pitiful voices and his wife’s eyes overflowing because she could not bear them broke the husband’s heart. Society’s refusal of compassion did more than that: it made him abandon his duty. Home turned bitter as neem; wife became poison; children became symbols that increased his pain.

How could their mother leave the children? The man she married abandoned her. The marriage thread he tied remained around her neck, and three children born from the pleasure he gave remained too. The responsibility to protect them, and the despair born from asking how it could possibly be done, entered her heart in the form of agony.

She climbed from house to house, showed the living dolls, wept and cried aloud. With whatever she obtained, she kept the children alive for some time and remained half-alive herself.

But during that period, day after day and minute after minute, the words society spoke and the abuse it hurled whenever she begged burned her heart like fire. Still she endured. Was she not a mother? She endured for her children.

Day by day she lost confidence in her occupation. How long could she preserve herself and her children through faith in something as uncertain as receiving food that day?

The terrible sight of the children reduced to skin and bone; the cruelty of their losing even the strength to say “I am hungry” and merely moving their lips: these pained her more than the faces with which others struck her when she begged.

The children’s appearance had once nourished motherhood; their baby speech and toddling steps had increased her love. Hunger transformed those same actions. She found that continuing to live while watching them exceeded a mother’s strength.

She resolved to end the suffering. Foolish woman, she decided she needed no one’s mercy or indulgence for her conclusion, and firmly believed the right belonged to her.

She imagined her decision would give her victory in the struggle of life. In a few seconds she would cast away the burden she could no longer bear.

She took the being she had carried ten months and formed within her womb, the one she had protected through dietary care and raised with praise, the one she had struggled to preserve while enduring countless degrading words. Gathering courage, she threw it into the well.

She closed her eyes tightly. Everything that had occurred in her life from the time she was born a girl until the moment she stood beside the well as mother of three appeared and vanished upon the screen of her mind.

She opened her eyes and granted “release” to another child. The third went into the water too, embracing death. Then she jumped into the well.

Society chased her as far as the well and still refused to leave her alone. Her pitiful decision suddenly gave it a compassion and responsibility it had never possessed. The society that refused her food and life lacked the courage to lose her.

Children are said to catch a small dragonfly and tear off each leg. When pain makes it beat the remaining legs, they imagine it does so from happiness. In just that fashion society pulled her from the well and prevented her death.

But all three children died.

Pachammal committed murder. That was her name. She attempted suicide as well. She was thrown into prison and charged under sections 302 and 393 of the Indian Penal Code. She did not deny the offences; she admitted them. The judge found her guilty and, as prescribed by law, sentenced her to rigorous imprisonment for life.

The Sessions Judge who delivered sentence noted that dreadful poverty had made her forget motherhood to such a degree that she threw her own children into a well and killed them. He also wrote that Pachammal was wholly deserving of government mercy.

An appeal was made to the High Court to reduce the sentence. The judges dismissed it, saying they lacked authority, but observed:

“This is a case capable of breaking the heart. The accused deserves great mercy. Terrible poverty made her entirely unable to protect her three children. Rather than watch them slowly die of hunger and then reach the same end herself after some days, she decided it was better to kill them at one stroke and commit suicide.”

The judges recommended that the government commute each sentence to one year’s simple imprisonment.

Terrible poverty drove the husband away from wife and children. The same poverty gave the mother enough desperate courage to kill three children with her own hands and attempt suicide.

The judges, guardians of the law, exposed this truth and the condition prevailing in a country whose course was so strange and painful. They declared that Pachammal was not alone and that many like her existed throughout the land. They explained so ordinary people could understand her condition, the thought it produced, what followed, and the inseparable connection among them.

Fully conscious of their responsibility, these responsible judges pointed out that poverty made Pachammal a murderess. Their work ended when they imposed punishment. But who must accept responsibility for changing the poverty that can turn good people into murderers and thieves? They did not say. Even so, they indirectly accuse the government.

If the children had slowly died from hunger and the mother had reached the same end after a few days, how many would have come forward to condemn the country’s rulers?

Had this occurred under another party’s government, the tongues and pens of these rulers and the newspapers supporting them would already have grown long. Wave upon wave of condemnation would have risen. From platforms shaking the sky, they would have spoken of the subtleties and duties of democracy. They would have demonstrated, demanding that those in power resign.

Instead this event ends with judges merely noting that she deserves mercy.

Poverty exists in the country, and as a result many Pachammals can arise, the judges state. They are protectors of law, not men intending to embarrass the government. No one would dare say they plot to overthrow it and occupy its place.

When certain creatures seated in the legislature as representatives of the public failed to understand their duty and expose the country’s real condition, judges showed the government how degraded that condition was.

Pachammal submitted a petition asking the Madras government for mercy. A question seeking information about it arose in the legislature. Minister Madhava Menon responded with great indifference:

“It seems her husband abandoned her. There was poverty. She killed three children. She attempted suicide.”

He stopped after this careless summary. Neither the questioner nor the other members appeared to find it pitiful or alarming. They did not consider that a citizen suffering poverty and becoming a murderess under this government stained the rulers themselves.

The minister speaks indifferently, and the members, watching for the end of the month, show they do not care. Still, do not forget: this is “people’s government.”

Had Pachammal sat as judge, would she not have placed in the dock the society that turned her into a murderess; the government that failed to care for her; the society that mocked her as “murderess” only after she offended; and a law powerful only to punish, not to teach rulers their duty to protect her?

Would she not have asked: “From where did a government incapable of enabling me to protect my children, a government without responsibility to provide all people a livelihood, acquire the right merely to punish them?”

Would she not have ruled that the name “people’s government” did not suit this government?

If murder and theft are to be prevented in the land, drive poverty out of it. Only then will people live with human character. Establish the good government required for that.

That is her command to the rulers. No, her plea.

The details of this case were published in *The Mail* on 19 December 1948.

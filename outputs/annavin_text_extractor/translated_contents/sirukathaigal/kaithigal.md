## English Translation

"There he is, the thieving fellow," said Number 84.

"Shame! Why do you abuse that gentleman in such a way?" asked Number 63.

"Why, man! It seems you once received a leaf of food when he held a temple feast. That must be why you place this thief on a pedestal and dance around him," Number 84 explained with a scowl.

"Go away! One can tell merely by looking at him that he is a good man."

"You must have seen him when he came to the temple. If you want to know him, you should not look at him beside the gateway tower. He comes there wearing a coat of polish. If you want to see his true form..."

Before Number 84 could finish, the warder arrived. The two men and the many other numbered prisoners with them adjusted their caps and set out for work. I have not yet said where: in prison.

Number 84 wore the black cap, meaning he had been imprisoned many times. Number 63 was a first offender: both prison and the occupation that brought one there were new things he had learned in haste.

When the magistrate asked, "How many times does this make?" Number 84, otherwise known as Kuttiyappan, displayed a faint smile and even ventured a joke: "Why ask me, master? Surely the gentleman who passes the sentences knows the count."

He was that seasoned a man, a true jailbird. He would enter a crowd, brush against someone and demand, "Why are you falling over me like a bullock?" By then the passerby's purse would have vanished. He knew many ingenious variations of the craft.

Even policemen, while he was in the lock-up, asked him to describe his methods for their amusement and laughed. To him, every policeman, whether one who beat him or one who did not, was affectionately "Naina." "What is it, Naina?" he would say.

This practitioner of theft, a black-cap prisoner, spoke contemptuously to another prisoner convicted in a different theft case about a man who served outside as a municipal councillor and temple trustee.

"If you want to see his true form, do not look beside the temple tower. Do you know where you should look...?" That was all he managed to tell Number 63 before the warder interrupted.

At home, Dharmalingam Pillai, president of the People's Relief Fund, was in torment. A letter had arrived announcing that a senior government officer would inspect the fund the next day.

Dharmalingam had lent out a ruby necklace pledged to the fund so that it could be worn at a wedding. It had not yet returned. How could he avoid anxiety? He had sent a man to fetch it, but the fellow had either become a tortoise or met some other fate, for he had not returned. Pillai trembled.

The officer's peon had already come with instructions: "Sir says he will arrive around ten tomorrow morning. Hot water must be ready, and these particular dishes must be cooked."

Dharmalingam Pillai's stomach churned. He had merely lent the ornament, but how could he say that to a government officer? What if he were charged with misusing public property? At the very thought, Chidambaram, Tirupati, Srirangam and every other sacred place came dancing onto the tip of his tongue. He wrung his hands and sighed. Only tears were still absent.

His wife Karpagavalli laughed at his condition.

"What a spectacle! Why are you panicking like this? Will he fail to bring the necklace back? If you tell the officer, will he cut off your head? We did not lend it to paupers. This ruby necklace went to a household that owns a diamond necklace. Why stand there in such distress?" Karpagam asked.

"You curse! What do you know? You cannot tell an oil press from a Shiva lingam. What do you understand of my suffering?" Pillai replied.

"That is precisely what I ask. Why suffer? What if we lent the jewel to Vaitheeswara Mudaliar's household? Will he cheat you? Is he the kind of great man who denies receiving what he borrowed?" she argued.

Dharmalingam Pillai repeatedly struck his head. What did Karpagam know of the relief fund's rules? She was a creature of the wilderness.

"Karpagam, why take my life? It is someone else's jewel, pledged to the bank. We must not lend it out," he explained as though teaching a small child.

Back in the prison, the conversation turned to the same man.

"Ask this elder brother if you wish. He knows Vaitheeswar's worth," said Number 84.

"Whose worth are you judges discussing at midnight?" another prisoner asked.

"Vaitheeswara Mudaliar's," replied Number 84.

"Do you mean the business of the whole theft?" asked the prisoner.

"You too know about him?" Number 63 asked.

"You ask whether I know? That blessed man is the very reason I came here. Listen, younger brother.

"When I was young, I did not study properly. I went back and forth to school but would not behave at home. My father and mother quarrelled without rest. Their quarrels became the school that taught me language.

"No job suited me. I passed my time visiting the houses opposite and next door, playing cards and telling stories. A goldsmith lived opposite and made jewellery. I enjoyed sitting there and watching his craft. By watching repeatedly, I gradually learned something of it.

"'Why do you need this trade?' he would ask.

"'Merely for amusement,' I would answer. At the time it truly was amusement.

"When no job came and the family fell into hardship, I thought, 'Why should I not take up this very craft?' A moment later I abandoned the thought. We belonged to the Mudaliar caste. What would our caste people think if I practised such a trade?

"It was then that Vaitheeswara Mudaliar suddenly began showing me affection. Even the owner of our village temple addressed him as 'uncle.' I did not understand his reason then. I understand it now.

"The previous trustee twisted his moustache and said, 'Will Vaithi manage the trusteeship? I shall see to that.' He had hired muscle under him. Vaitheeswara Mudaliar became frightened, enlisted me as support and secured the position.

"He did not stop there. By talking with me, Vaithi learned that I knew how to make jewellery. At once he formed a plan. He brought important gem-set ornaments from the temple. In a private room of his own house, I removed the stones and set white imitations in their place. Vaithi obtained the originals and I received payment for my work.

"First we finished off diamonds, emeralds and rubies in this fashion. Then we began attending to the gold ornaments.

"Are you astonished, younger brother? Do you wonder how Vaithi committed so many wrongs yet remains outside and commands respect? There are many like him. Do not imagine that thieves exist only inside prison. The man who fails is in jail; the man who succeeds lives in comfort. Listen to the rest of the story.

"Thus the trustee lived by stripping the temple's jewels, and I too made it my livelihood and lived rather comfortably. Once money began moving through my hands, my expenses increased. Drinking increased too, and as it did, I had to seek other jewels.

"Vaithi announced that in future a deity had appeared and threatened him against such deeds. That was not the truth. Something had occurred in government circles involving support for the temple trustee. Whatever the reason, I had to seek another field.

"For a while I succeeded there too. I hired a man to assist me. He was a goldsmith, and the shop was registered in his name. All of it was Vaithi's idea.

"While conducting this 'business,' we were caught in a case. A fine ruby necklace came for repair. We removed four real rubies and pressed good imitation stones into their places. At the worst possible time, its owners needed to pledge the necklace, and they happened to take it to Vaithi himself.

"While speaking with him, they mentioned that I had repaired it. The sinner immediately laughed and said, 'Oh! Has the jewel passed through that place? Then I cannot lend money on it.' They grew suspicious, took it elsewhere, and the truth emerged.

"That is why I am here. When the case trapped me, I pleaded with Vaithi, but he gave no help. The man who worked with me has completed his sentence and gone out. He does not know what I intend. I shall expose the temple-jewel affair and seek another occupation.

"I sent a warning that when my former assistant was released, he ought to finish Vaithi off. Vaitheeswar is that kind of thief. He robbed temple property and stole the entire lot. Do not think he is honourable merely because he remains outside.

"He was the one who trained me in theft. He profited from it and became a great man; I am in prison. Younger brother, when one begins to speak of property, jewels, money and cash, one understands the secret of how many thieves remain outside prison," the prisoner concluded.

"Did you hear that, Number 63? Without knowing the facts, you saw Vaitheeswar's disguise and called him a great man," said Number 84.

Then he offered his own story.

"This Vaitheeswar often came to Mayavaram. That is where I caught hold of him. At the time I habitually visited the hotel area because goods could be obtained there conveniently.

"One day I entered his room while he had gone downstairs to bathe. Inside I found a box packed full of opium. He had filled the box with opium, placed a tray over it as a lid and arranged bottles of perfume on the tray. He pretended to trade in attar.

"I saw it and did not leave. I decided to test him and sat on the cot. When the man returned, the door was closed but not locked. He panicked, opened it and entered. On seeing me, he gave a cry.

"'Hush! Do not make a noise. Close the door and sit down. I have come to buy attar,' I said softly. He was terrified.

"'You truly are a clever gentleman! Opium beneath the attar and attar above the opium. What a splendid arrangement!' I mocked. He bared his teeth in a grin.

"Then we bargained. I obtained two hundred rupees, and he and I became friends. Within two or three days all the opium had been sold. He had agents of every kind.

"When he prepared to leave for his village, he feared I might accompany him. I wanted to learn his full address so I could threaten him from time to time. He therefore planned to depart without my knowledge.

"At the hotel he was known as Arumugam Pillai, perfume merchant, and had given me the same name. But I saw the initial 'V' marked on his clothes. That made me suspicious. I resolved not to let him escape and to follow him.

"He boarded the train. In the second-class carriage he gave me oranges and apples, but would not let me accompany him. The train departed and I took my leave. He must have rejoiced that he had got rid of me.

"Could he deceive me? I had sent one of our young fellows ahead to gather information. Two days later the boy returned and reported: 'His name is Vaitheeswara Mudaliar. He has a reputation as a very good man in his town. Everyone treats him with respect. He is the temple trustee.'

"I even considered handing the man disguised as Attar Arumuga Mudaliar over to the police, but there was no profit in it for me. I decided instead to squeeze him for as much as I could and waited a month or two.

"The man never appeared in Mayavaram again. He seemed to have written to his agents and arranged another location. Finally I went directly to his village to see him.

"I found him at the temple and bowed to him. He began asking, 'Who are you? From which village?'

"I reminded him of the name Attar Arumuga Mudaliar.

"'Who is this fellow speaking as though I were someone else?' he complained loudly. Temple servants surrounded me and even beat me. I shouted and told them that this man traded in opium, but they pushed me away.

"Meanwhile the police made inquiries about me. The Mayavaram police reported that I had previously been imprisoned. So they charged me with attempting to snatch a purse from the lap of Trustee Vaitheeswara Mudaliar near the temple-tower entrance. They sentenced me too.

"And you speak of such a Vaitheeswar as a great and honourable man merely because he is not here in prison with us!" Number 84 said.

Number 63 gave a long sigh.

"So this is how matters stand. I thought only the poor, destitute and ruined stole. I too committed theft and came to prison, but hard times gave me that thought. Whenever I saw Vaitheeswara Mudaliar, I used to lament: 'Ah, what a great man! This is how one ought to live, while I have become a thief.' After hearing you, it appears that this gentleman is an extraordinary fraud," he said wearily.

"It seems I shall not finish before dawn, sir. These days I cannot work as I once did. Look at the spinach curry they serve in prison: it sucks the blood from your body. See for yourself how I have wasted away to sticks," Kannachari complained as he continued prising the stones from the ruby necklace.

Vaitheeswara Mudaliar kept him talking and working.

"Only greens? Do they not even serve a proper measure of rice?" he asked.

12 January 1947

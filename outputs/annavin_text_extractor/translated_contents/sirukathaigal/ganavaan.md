## English Translation

“I heard that ancient sculptures, symbols, manuscripts, and similar things could be found here,” said a newcomer.

Before showing his wares, Pandari, the antique dealer, offered a sweet smile.

“The number of people who seek ancient sculpture has declined. Only literary scholars and those who know history can understand its value. Ordinary people desire only the glittering, shining goods of the present age.”

“True, sir. But there are also men who show counterfeits as ancient sculpture and symbols and cheat people, are there not? I myself was once deceived in Calcutta. There was a horsewhip of rare workmanship, unlike anything in current use. Seeing me study it closely, the shopkeeper spoke very skilfully and said it had belonged to Raja Desingu. He showed many proofs, and I was overwhelmed.

“If that whip truly belonged to Desingu, what a rare discovery it would be and how useful for historical research! The more I thought so, the stronger my longing grew. The dealer completely bewitched me. He claimed that Nasruddin, who had directly participated in Desingu's battle, carried it away; that it remained in his family for years; that Nasruddin's grandson sold it to Abdul Sattar of Allahabad; that it travelled from Sattar to Nagpur; and that, after great difficulty, the dealer finally acquired it. He showed records to support the story. I paid five hundred rupees.

“Later a great researcher proved to me that Desingu never used a horsewhip. I grieved over what a fool I had been. What use? The money was gone.”

“That is how it often happens,” the dealer agreed. He showed certificates of commendation from famous researchers including Arunagiri, Mr Archibald, Professor Ibrahim, and Ustad Wali Khan.

“Buy any object you like only after proper research. I shall exert no pressure,” he promised.

The visitor continued examining many things.

Pandari, who had been writing accounts, said after a while, “I was determined not to show you anything of my own accord. Yet my heart will not permit me to conceal this from so good a man. Do not ask its price. Merely seeing and enjoying it will suffice.”

He opened a casket and took out an anklet.

It was not gold, nor even silver. Gold and silver could be obtained; such an anklet could not. This was the warrior's anklet worn by Kattabomman!

“Let the cruel Company men take my life if they wish,” Kattabomman had declared heroically, “but I shall not let those base men seize the warrior's anklet my people gave me.” He threw it into Vettakulam pond. It lay there for many years. The pond dried and became an earthen mound.

Thirty years ago, Sevur Santhiyappan bought the place to build a mansion. During excavation, a labourer found the anklet and traded it at a toddy shop. I happened to marry a woman from the shopkeeper's family, and thus it came to me.

Sattichami told me about Kattabomman's anklet. His guru was a great saintly poet who carried earth for a pittance. He composed a splendid *akaval* called “I Shall Speak to the Anklet.” Here is that poem.”

Pandari handed over a palm-leaf manuscript. The visitor read it with great attention and enjoyed it.

The dealer replaced the anklet in its casket. As the visitor looked at other things, he noticed an old violin, took it up, and turned it this way and that.

Pandari laughed. “That is no rare ancient object. It is an ordinary violin available everywhere today. Yet that instrument is my guru.”

“What do you mean by calling a violin your guru?”

“Leave that aside. Tell me what price it would fetch.”

The visitor laughed. “It is a very ordinary old violin. One might give fifteen or twenty rupees.”

“Excellent! You have stated it exactly. How did you know I paid three hundred?”

“Who said three hundred? I said fifteen or twenty.”

“Oh! I thought you meant fifteen multiplied by twenty. You would at least pay fifteen; I would not give one paisa above ten. Yet I paid three hundred. It turned me into such a fool. Listen to the story of this violin and my stupidity.

“One evening, a beggar girl about ten years old stood opposite my shop dragging this affliction of an instrument and singing. No one gave her a coin. After waiting a long time, she entered the shop.”

“Did she enter and sell you the violin?”

“Would I buy it for three hundred? Listen. The girl's face melted me. Tears stood in her eyes. Saying, 'Sir, charitable lord!' she fell at my feet. As usual I shouted, 'You donkey! Release my feet. Get away! Go outside!' But she would not leave.

“'Sir, give me five rupees. Not alms: lend it to me. My mother is dangerously ill and needs medicine. I sang in the market and received only two quarter-annas. At the cinema shed they threw only two chocolates. Here, as you can see, I received no coin. I must buy Mother's medicine before dark. Otherwise, sir, I fear even to imagine it. I shall repay the five rupees little by little, every day. Tomorrow is market day; I may obtain one or two rupees there. I sing well and dance too. Sir, five rupees!'

“I laughed. How could one not laugh when a beggar girl asked for a five-rupee loan?”

“And a ten-year-old child!”

“Yes. I threatened, 'Go away, donkey!' She showed this violin.

“'Even if you will not trust me, keep this violin and give me the money. Look, it cost thirty rupees when we bought it. It is necessary for my livelihood; this alone earns our gruel. To save my mother, I give it as though plucking out and giving my own eye. Return it when I repay the five rupees.'”

“The child spoke reasonably.”

“Reasonable or not, when I considered her love for her mother, I felt I ought to give not merely five but ten. I lent her five.”

“And the violin?”

“Though I said it was unnecessary, she left it as security.”

“But you said you paid three hundred.”

“You must hear the entire story.

“Four, five, even six days passed without the girl returning. I decided that the five rupees were lost. On the seventh day, a man who looked every inch a lord and scholar entered. Like you, he examined many things. When he saw the violin, he started. His hand genuinely trembled when he took it; I saw it myself.

“In a sorrowful voice he said, 'Sir, I could not even dream that I would find this violin. I have searched city after city until exhausted. At last God opened his eyes, and I have found it here.' Tears filled his eyes. I was astonished.

“He began playing. It sounded sweet. He seemed unable to see the shop, its goods, or me.

“'Sir, what rarity does this possess?' I asked.

“He smiled. 'Usually shopkeepers explain the value of their own goods. Yet you ask me.' I felt a little ashamed.

“'Never mind. Do you know Gauhar Jaan?'

“'I have heard of her, a famous Hindustani singer.'

“'Famous, indeed! You fool, she was the one great singer. Even a murderer would become gentle on hearing Gauhar Jaan. A cobra would raise its hood and dance to that sweetness, music, and wealth of expression. Could another acquire it even through penance? The whole land celebrated Gauhar Jaan saris, bangles, and fashions.

“'This violin was used in such a Gauhar Jaan concert. It belonged to my father. He was not a professional musician, but a major merchant in Veerapuri. His “love” for Gauhar turned him into a violinist, then a wanderer and a madman. He left the family, roamed everywhere, and died penniless.

“'With my uncle's help I studied, advanced, conducted business, and became wealthy. After money accumulated, I spent perhaps two thousand merely travelling in search of this violin. At last I find the instrument that rested in my father's hands, that Gauhar Jaan praised, that gave delight upon a thousand stages. Ask whatever price you wish. I am ready to pay.'”

“A man entirely unknown to you?”

“Yes. But had you seen him embrace the violin to his chest and press it to his eyes, you would understand.”

“What happened next?”

“I saw he was ready to pay even if I brazenly demanded a thousand. But how could I sell it? What if the girl returned the very next day with five rupees and asked for it? A beggar would not quietly leave; a crowd would gather in the street. What would happen to my standing and the dignity of my shop?

“The man began at a hundred and rose to a thousand. I told him, 'Sir, it is not mine. It belongs to another. It would be unjust to sell without asking. Return next Friday; by then I shall speak to the owner and arrange it. You consent to a thousand, do you not?'

“'It is the only property my father leaves me. Not merely a thousand; I shall pay more. Obtain it for me somehow. I shall never forget your help. My mind will find peace only if you promise.'

“I agreed, and he departed promising to return the next Friday.

“The beggar girl arrived the following day with five rupees.

“'Sir, through your merit my mother survived. I also earned well at the market. Here is the money; return the violin.'

“In an age when even lords break their promises, I was astonished that a mere beggar girl kept her word.

“'You are a good child; God will bless you. Fortune is about to strike. I have arranged to sell this violin for one hundred rupees. I shall give you the money, and you and your mother can begin a small trade.'

“I thought she would rejoice and fall at my feet. Instead she cried, 'No, sir, anything but that. This violin feeds us. It remains in my hand all day and beside my head all night. My heart will not consent to lose it, and Mother will never agree.'

“What could I do? The other man was willing to pay a thousand. I pressed again. She brought her mother. I spoke affectionately and finally made them agree to three hundred. To be safe, I wrote a sale deed and asked for a signature. Stumbling over the letters, the mother signed 'Thillai.' They went away with the money, but in sorrow.

“I rejoiced over the seven-hundred-rupee profit.

“Tuesday ran, Wednesday moved, Thursday dragged, and Friday came. I polished the violin and waited, also considering whether to let the man leave at one thousand or extract another ten or twenty.

“Greed! Absorbed in it, I did not even attend to the shop. At ten I told myself he would come in another hour. At two I asked why he had not arrived. Three, four, six, eight: closing time came, but the man did not.

“The next Friday, then another. Four Fridays passed. He never came. In greed for seven hundred, I tried to deceive a beggar and make a huge profit, and received the broad religious mark of a dupe upon my forehead. I searched for the girl but could not find her.

“I worship this violin every day as the guru who taught me the noble lesson that great greed brings great loss.”

The visitor laughed. “Madness! The clever beggar girl and the cheat disguised as a wealthy lord must now be feasting together upon the money they stripped from you.”

“What can I do, sir? I know no deceit. Whatever anyone says, I believe it true until I discover it false.”

“Yes. In the same way you have been deceived about Kattabomman's anklet. This belonged neither to Kattabomman nor is it a warrior's anklet. It is a hand-rattle used by Mariamman temple priests. Someone has foisted it upon you. You even show a palm-leaf copy. However much you paid, you were cheated.”

“This is how many become fools, sir. Some remain eternally vigilant,” said the dealer, abandoning hope of business with him.

“Still, let me share the loss you suffered on the violin. Name some price and give it to me.”

Pandari was stunned.

“Why incur a needless loss? I was a fool and should suffer its consequence.”

“True, yet this violin seems suitable to me. I shall give fifty rupees.”

“I paid three hundred...” the merchant began his trade.

“Do not say 'paid'; say 'was fooled into paying.' I can give only fifty. If you wish, I shall also be foolish enough to believe your Kattabomman tale: one hundred for the violin and anklet together. Do you agree?”

Strong suspicion entered the dealer. Could the violin truly have been used in a Gauhar Jaan concert? No, impossible. This man believed that the anklet belonged to Kattabomman; he believed the other tale in the same way. The dealer himself had bought that hand-rattle from horse-cart Kuppusami for fourteen annas.

He sold both objects for one hundred rupees. The gentleman took them and left. “Madman!” the dealer mocked inwardly.

In the neighbouring town, Thillai and her daughter were selling hot idlis and dosas.

Curly-moustached Sundaram boasted to his friend: “Do you know? I spun a tale about Gauhar Jaan's violin, and the dealer believed it. Then I sent our little girl; Thillai went too. We struck him for three hundred.”

“Madman!” he said of the dealer.

“Madman!” said the gentleman too as he broke open the anklet and rejoiced over the diamonds hidden inside.

His mind travelled to an incident two years earlier.

A procession of Muthumari set out grandly from Pondicherry. Hindu leaders planned it to be more splendid than the Mariamman festival itself, and it became a feast for the eyes. Wealthy Hindus had obtained special permission from the Governor for Muthumari to leave Pondicherry and hold a forest picnic in the iluppai grove on the Indian border. Colonial rulers knew that they could obstruct anything else or impose other prohibitions, but interference in religion was dangerous; permission was granted.

Priests praised Muthumari and danced with hand-rattles. Devotees worshipped along the route as the procession neared the border.

Customs officers received alarming intelligence: hardened smugglers intended to exploit the festival to carry valuable diamonds illegally. What could they do? Stop the Mariamman procession and search everyone, even the goddess's image? An uninformed crowd might rage, causing a bloodbath. Yet if they did nothing, the criminals would escape.

“If we search at the festival, people will rage, throw stones, and riot. We will then have to raise rifles. The next day's papers will carry headlines such as 'Trouble at Pondicherry Border' and 'Mariamman Festival Obstructed.' We shall invite needless blame,” said one officer.

“It would be a great sin too, sir. They say this Muthumari is immensely powerful and that sinners die coughing blood from nose and mouth,” another said fearfully.

“Who believes such fabricated tales in this age?” said a third, summoning courage.

The senior officer declared that a search would be improper without firm evidence. One apparently bold subordinate volunteered to enter the procession in disguise, gather intelligence, and signal four plain-clothes officers when he found the culprit.

Disguised as a priest, he sang splendidly and danced with a hand-rattle. Another priest matched him with equal skill. The others greatly enjoyed their contest.

The disguised officer sang:

> Where is the diamond you bought and hid?
> Speak aloud, Mother, and I shall give you a fitting offering!
> Otherwise I shall encircle and drag you away,
> I have told you, Mother Mariamman!

His rival ignored the hint and danced as if possessed.

That rival priest was the present “gentleman,” and the diamonds lay inside his hand-rattle.

The officer's suspicion instead settled upon a man dancing with a decorated pot. The man himself sang:

> Diamond, a diamond,
> a fine diamond;
> buy and keep it,
> it yields profit by the crore!

The song strengthened the officer's suspicion. He stopped his priestly role and shouted. Officers surrounded the procession and began searching. The people cursed them.

Only then did the rival priest hand his rattle to another priest beside him. Without asking why, the man accepted it. The first asked for it back and then handed it over again. Thus the rattle-changing game continued.

When the second man saw a priest being searched, he trembled because a small packet of opium lay in his waistcloth. He slipped away. The rival pursued him because the rattle was now in his hands.

The disguised smuggler was caught with gold the size of a lemon at his waist, but no one possessed diamonds. He tried to assault the officer and escape and received one year of rigorous imprisonment. In prison he felt shame and anguish. After so much effort to carry the diamonds, and even adopting a priest's guise, they had passed into another man's hands.

“Brother! Are you here?” someone later called in prison.

“You wretch, is it you?”

“Is this the reward for priestly work?”

“I truly thought you were a priest. They say you carried gold and opium.”

“Leave that. The rattle changed hands and reached you. What did you do with it? Is it safe at home?”

“It helped me once in hardship. I sold it to Pandari's antique shop.”

The first man fainted. On recovering, he learned every detail about Pandari's shop. It was he who later went there disguised as a gentleman.

Pandari had hoped to gain a little profit by showing him the rattle and spinning the tale that it was Kattabomman's anklet. The thief maintained his gentlemanly role, bought it, broke it open, and found exquisite diamonds. His suffering had not been wasted. He rejoiced that his flawless acting had left Pandari without suspicion.

He went to the shop of Tripuradas, a receiver of stolen property, and showed the diamonds.

Saying, “Like this, see,” the Seth secretly summoned police, handed over the thief, and produced a different packet rather than the one he had received.

“This thief tried to foist imitation stones upon me as diamonds,” he declared.

The thief disguised as a gentleman received three years' rigorous imprisonment.

Seth Tripuradas was praised as an outstanding philanthropist and gentleman. The District Collector inaugurated the Seth Tripuradas Charity Hall. Every gentleman attending the celebration praised Sethji.

The newspaper *Bharat Seva* published a portrait of the benevolent Seth Tripuradas. Its editor wrote: “Sethji embodies the true definition of the word gentleman.”

## Translator's Notes

“கனவான்” denotes a gentleman, man of standing, or wealthy respectable person. The story repeatedly applies and withdraws that social mask: the diamond smuggler poses as one, while the receiver of stolen goods is publicly certified as its “true definition.” “கழல்” is a warrior's anklet in Pandari's fabricated Kattabomman provenance, but the object is actually a priest's கைச்சிலம்பு, a handheld jingling rattle. Gauhar Jaan was a celebrated historical singer; the violin provenance and family story are fictional deception within the narrative. The Muthumari procession, customs operation, smuggling, opium, stolen-property trade, and charitable honours are translated as fictional source claims. Damaged names and cultural terms are logged separately.

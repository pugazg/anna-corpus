## English Translation

Tamil title: பல்லிளிக்கும் பரப்பிரம்மங்கள்

"He who steals food steals life."

One may say so, for it is that grave an offence. It is condemnable that, after seizing six sacks of paddy, the culprit tries to escape punishment with excuses such as, "He struck at my stomach," "He killed the poor," and "He hoarded paddy during the famine."

This is one of the disastrous results caused by irresponsible people speaking of doctrines.

To dismiss the matter by saying, "It was only six sacks of paddy," would ultimately split apart the foundations of society.

We therefore rejoice that this criminal has received severe punishment.

Having concluded his editorial in this manner, the editor of *Bharathavarsham*, Parabrahmam, happily wandered through fantasies of all the praise it might bring him.

Anthonisamy and his companions had stolen six sacks of paddy from a mirasudar's granary. They were caught, and the paddy returned to the granary. Anthonisamy received three years in prison; his companions received six months.

Some political parties, under the name of novel ideas, were introducing moral corruption into the country. Parabrahmam, editor of *Bharathavarsham*, worked according to a plan to suppress their arrogance.

Anthonisamy did not deny attempting to steal the sacks.

He was a labourer on the mirasudar's farm, an impulsive fellow. He often forgot that he was performing a servant's work and spoke with his head held a little high.

His courage blazed still brighter because of his cousin Mary Sithara. Mary was a teacher in Madras. Whenever she came to the village, she told Anthonisamy about the direction in which the world was moving and the awakening of the poor. William, a fellow teacher, brought these interesting matters to Mary along with the flavour of romance. Without revealing him, Mary poured everything he said into Anthonisamy's ears.

Having received this instruction from his cousin, Anthonisamy began speaking to his master in an unaccustomed fashion:

"You do not give us anything for nothing. You pay us because we work."

"There must be mutual respect."

"Are we cattle, never to open our mouths and speak?"

These sentences proved that Anthonisamy had been "spoiled." The manager was ordered to set a trap for him.

The editor of *Bharathavarsham* knew none of this. Even if he had, he would not have cared.

"While we die of hunger, mountains of goods useless to us lie heaped in the granary, and they order us to guard them from theft! What courage these men possess!"

"If you remain dumb insects, why should they not grow bold?"

"Only when you open your mouths and demand justice will they be struck dumb."

"Will they remain dumb if we forcibly carry away the goods they have locked up?"

"Will they? They will take our lives."

"It is because they know that fear exists that they climb onto our heads and ride us."

William, whom Mary brought to the village, had gone away after scattering seeds like these.

William habitually gambled away money. Since he was Mary's future husband, Anthonisamy even gave him twenty rupees. Like a tutor who took a fee before teaching a lesson, William left these ideas in return.

Poverty was severe in the village, and a poisonous fever also marched through it. Anthonisamy's compassionate heart suffered greatly. He asked the mirasudar for paddy and money as a loan. The man said nothing to him, but asked the manager, "Why is that fellow casting such thieving looks?"

William turned Anthonisamy into an actual thief.

It was according to William's advice that Anthonisamy went to steal.

William did not argue openly that theft was no crime. He knew Anthonisamy's honesty would revolt.

"Those who make the stomachs of the poor burn must be taught a lesson."

"Teach them a lesson? We?"

"Yes. Why not? They must learn that the poor will not remain silent when struck in the stomach."

"How?"

"Does this require a conjuring trick? There is paddy in the granary, but he refuses when asked. The poor suffer. If he will not give it, we take it ourselves."

"Indeed! Your brain has turned upside down."

"Mine?"

"Are you asking us to steal?"

"Did he call it stealing, fool? Why do you think of it that way? You asked him to give it and he refused. I say take it. That is all."

The "teaching" that began in this way turned Anthonisamy into a thief and sent him to prison.

I met him there.

He had changed somewhat, not because of the cruelty of prison, for he had suffered far greater pain outside it. Once inside, Anthonisamy understood that William had taught him neither a doctrine nor an ideal, but had ruined him; that he was a scoundrel and an unjust man. Anthonisamy had not learnt this from reading journals. Where would he have found such an opportunity? Mary gave him this knowledge too.

Pretending to perform penance for Mary's love, William took money from her, lost it in gambling, made her a debtor in several places, and then tried to turn her into merchandise.

Her pure heart trembled. She saw his injustice, grew furious, condemned him, and cursed him. William woke up to the danger and went away in search of other prey. From the other side of the prison bars, Mary mixed her tears with this news and gave Anthonisamy fresh understanding.

When I met him, he had understood that a principle was one thing and a crooked path another. I do not know how he will change when he comes out, for inside prison there are so many Williams bearing names such as Veerasamy, Madasamy, Suppan, and Vinayagan.

Does my saying that I met him in prison make you suspicious of me? I did not go there for stealing anything. I went for participating in the righteous struggle against the varnasrama education scheme.

I came out. Many other events rolled past.

Once again I had an opportunity to see *Bharathavarsham*.

Its editor had written a moving editorial:

The prominent mirasudar Peyandi Pillai, charged with secretly sending sacks of paddy to Ceylon, has been fined five thousand rupees and sentenced to six months' imprisonment. Those who commit offences must be punished; there can be no doubt about it. Yet when we examine certain truths in this case, our hearts cannot but melt.

Exporting goods abroad is not in itself a crime. Government permission, however, must be obtained; there are controls. Why do controls exist? Because of the food shortage. Whose responsibility is it to end that shortage? The government's. The government failed in that duty. The true culprit is therefore the government!

There is endless evidence that the distinguished man now punished has led an honourable and dignified life.

When the thousand-pillared hall fell into decay, it was this virtuous man who personally took the lead in repairing it. He is also a nationalist and a Congressman. Whether such a person would commit a wrongful act is a matter requiring investigation.

In general, courts and governments come forward only to punish offenders. They never examine the circumstances that compelled an offence.

No condemnation of this practice can be too strong.

The government's inefficiency causes a food crisis; controls are imposed to conceal that inefficiency. Shame! Shame!

The lawyer appearing for Peyandi Pillai in the present case has explained this in detail.

By issuing ever-new rules and orders, the government turns many acts that are not inherently crimes into crimes. It is only in that manner that Peyandi Pillai became a "criminal." We have no doubt that he is a man of virtue. Some may say we write this way because he is a patron of our newspaper. Patriots will pay no attention to their irresponsible talk.

Furthermore, Peyandi Pillai heard that our Bharatiya brethren in Ceylon were suffering famine conditions. His heart throbbed, and he resolved that food must be made available to them. Such a moral impulse can arise only in a person of the highest character.

The government would not permit food to be sent to Ceylon. People there suffered, while Peyandi Pillai's heart endured agony here. Therefore he sent food to Ceylon, prepared to let others revile him as a black-marketeer and profiteer and let the government punish him. Hundreds of thousands obtained food and joy overflowed. It is certain that all those poor families will now shed tears on hearing that this donor to charity has been punished.

He was accused of making an exorbitant profit.

Government counsel said that the paddy was sold for twice its ordinary price. The eminent Peyandi Pillai's explanation, that this was the act of an agent in his employ, deserves attention.

It is truly distressing when charitable donors encounter such hardship.

That was the *Bharathavarsham* editorial. What emotion! What an array of explanations! What arguments! All because the offender was eminent and wealthy.

Only when discussing Peyandi Pillai's case does *Bharathavarsham* ask whether punishing the offence is enough and whether one must not investigate why it occurred. In poor Anthonisamy's case, its verdict was simply one sharp cut and two clean pieces.

A young man, tormented by poverty and mocked by everyone, is given a million-pound banknote by a wealthy gentleman, under one condition: he must not change the note for a month.

One million pounds!

Shopkeepers, hotelkeepers, entertainment proprietors, and mansion dwellers learn that he possesses the note. They do not know that he is forbidden to change it.

The moment they see the million-pound note, he receives everywhere the welcome and royal honours of a king.

He enters a clothing shop. Seeing his simple, dirty clothes and rustic appearance, the manager treats him with contempt.

"You want clothes? Very well, go over there," he says, sending him to a corner containing cheap garments.

The attendant there too shows him the very lowest-grade clothes. The young man says that he needs change. "For how much?" asks the manager. The young man displays the million-pound note.

That is enough. The manager nearly faints.

"Ah! I erred without knowing who you were. Forgive me," he cries.

He folds his hands in homage. He dances and runs about. He offers the most expensive clothes in the shop.

Money? What of it? "I can collect it whenever you please," he says, melting with deference. "It is enough that your sacred feet have touched this place. Your wish is my good fortune."

It is the same everywhere.

The hotelkeeper bows before him. The entertainment proprietor welcomes him with reverence. He receives first place at banquets in mansions. Sweet-spoken women compete with one another to dance with him. Officials wait upon his smile.

All of it is the work of the million-pound note!

He has not changed it or paid them money. It is enough that they know he possesses it. That fact brings every luxury to him.

The magic performed by money! Its power! Its intoxicating glory!

This is a beautiful picture created by the scholar Mark Twain. I have seen an English film titled *The Million Pound Note* that presents it with great charm.

For many people, baring their teeth in a grin and crying ceremonial praises the moment they discover that a person is wealthy has become second nature.

The editor of *Bharathavarsham* is such a man. That is why, once he knew Peyandi Pillai was rich, he showed such affection.

He bares his teeth through his editorial, as do many like him. Seated in positions that give them the opportunity to perform intellectual work, they spit fire in condemnation of the Anthonisamys.

Chekhov, the great Russian thinker, mocked and condemned this habit of grinning and offering servile praise the moment wealth becomes visible.

Today our red-flag champions here declare that discussing economic problems alone is the mark of those who understand communist philosophy.

Chekhov is a writer and artist honoured in communist Russia. Through his stories and writings, he sowed seeds for the communist revolution.

Chekhov's story-picture *The Chameleon*, condemning servility before the rich, became a storehouse of explosive material for the bomb Lenin prepared.

To depict this tendency to bare one's teeth before wealth, Chekhov shows us a dog and a police officer.

A little puppy!

A man provokes it, and it scratches or bites him. Blood oozes from his finger.

He chases and catches the dog, intending to kill it. A small crowd gathers. The dog is terrified. The man displays the blood flowing from his finger. Many support him, saying the dog must be killed.

Seeing the crowd, a police officer arrives. The bitten man shows his finger and explains what happened.

The officer becomes fiercely angry with the dog.

"A vicious dog! It must be shot. Poor man, did it bite you? Do not be afraid; we can kill it. Who owns this dog? What fool let it roam the street like this? He must not be spared but punished according to law. Look at the dog! Ugly, obviously a low-bred cur. Letting such a dog loose is an enormous offence. Its owner must not escape."

The officer speaks with heroic fury. The bitten man thanks him, and the crowd marvels at and praises the officer.

The dog looks around in terror.

The officer summons a constable. "Hey! Catch that vicious dog and drag it here," he orders.

"Whose dog is this?" he asks again, for a case must be filed and the owner punished.

Someone in the crowd says softly, "Sir, it belongs to General Zhigalov."

That is enough. The officer changes at once.

"Whose? Whose did you say? General Zhigalov's dog? Ah! Constable, put this woollen coat over it. Poor thing, it is shivering in the cold wind. What a fine, beautiful dog. They say this dog bit you? This is a tiny pup, and you are a hulking fellow. How could it bite you? You must have scraped your finger on a nail somewhere. Scoundrel! Move away!"

The officer's support now belongs to the dog. Why? It is the general's dog.

"Sir, this fellow put the end of his cigarette in the dog's mouth. That is why it bit him, the fool," says one man.

The bitten man denies it, but the officer has become the dog's advocate.

Then the constable says, "It is not the general's, sir. He has no dog like this. Would one of his dogs look like this?"

"Are you certain?" asks the officer.

The constable assures him that he is. Immediately the officer joins the man's side again.

"This is not the general's dog. It is a low-bred animal; one can tell merely by looking at its face. It belongs to some nobody. Its owner must not be spared," the officer declares, and the bitten man rejoices.

The constable then drawls, "It is not the general's... but perhaps it might be his after all."

Someone in the crowd says, "It is certainly from the general's house."

The officer changes once more.

He becomes the dog's protector again.

The wind is blowing; the dog needs a covering. It is a fine dog, a pedigree animal. This fellow must have burnt it with his cigarette, and that is why it bit him. What rowdy conduct! The bitten man wonders why the officer shows the dog an affection he does not show him. It is only a dog, an ugly one, and it has bitten a man whose finger is still bleeding. Yet it is not an ordinary man's dog; it belongs to the general.

How could the officer take the man's side? He must take the side of the mansion's dog.

The general's cook happens to pass that way. Some suggest asking him.

"This one? Would the general raise a dog like this?" the cook asks.

At once the officer returns to the man's side.

"Yes, yes! It is a street cur. Would the general even touch such a thing? It must be destroyed. Poor man, his finger is bleeding," he says.

But the cook has not finished.

"It is not the general's. His elder brother is visiting the mansion. This dog belongs to him."

The officer changes again.

The general's elder brother is a nobleman. A nobleman's dog is superior to a human being in many respects. Naturally the officer must show sympathy and support to the nobleman's dog.

He calls the puppy affectionately and fondles it. "A little mischievous, that is all. It bit this fellow in play. A fine pedigree dog; would a nobleman raise anything else? Take it away carefully," he says tenderly, handing it to the cook.

The dog that defeated a human being marches away in triumph.

The man stands there bewildered, displaying the blood that still oozes. Others mock him.

"Wait! I shall teach you a proper lesson," the officer laughs, and goes away to attend to other business.

What a great lesson Chekhov constructed in this short story!

The editor of *Bharathavarsham* belongs to this very breed.

The officer Chekhov portrayed no longer exists in Russia. People like the editor of *Bharathavarsham* abound here.

These are men who beat the drum on behalf of the wealthy. They themselves know honesty, justice, and fairness. They know how to speak sympathetically. They know how to argue very well. They know even better how to offer advice.

But when does all that ability reveal itself?

Not when a Peyandi Pillai must be condemned. They require an Anthonisamy!

So long as such grinning Parabrahmans enter positions meant for spreading knowledge and exercise domination there, how can the poor obtain liberation, and how can the nation attain a healthy condition?

14 January 1955

## Translator's Notes

The title is intentionally satirical. “பல்லிளிக்கும்” means baring the teeth in a servile grin, while “பரப்பிரம்மங்கள்” invokes the lofty “Supreme Brahman”; “The Grinning Parabrahmans” preserves that collision. The story uses period political, caste, class, and disability-related language in quoted or critical contexts.

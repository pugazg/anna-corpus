## English Translation

## 1. The Job Is Gone

He had written, "Visalatchi's wedding." I set it in type as "Visalatchi's argument"! It was the hour for sleep, and there was a little sorrow too. Thus one mistake followed another.

He had written "Swami Kavadiyanandar" quite clearly. I printed it wrongly as "Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar" - turning the holy man into a lustful fellow of the public rest house. In the same way, *kāsumālai* (a necklace of gold coins) became *kāmālai* (jaundice); Mani Arasu became Pini Arasu (the king of illness); Visalam became Visaram (sorrow); *Vedam* became *bēdam* (difference or discrimination); and *moi* (wedding gifts) became *poi* (lies).

So many errors arose from my carelessness! And all this happened in no more than a short item. Our editor's handwriting was neat as rows of pearls - indeed, it looked like print itself. Never mind the subject matter; was that important? He had no need to write page after page. His ideal was that brevity was the essence of wit. The pages of the newspaper owed their beauty not to the mercy of his pen but to the grace of the office scissors.

"Thambi! This is a prominent wedding. Print it attractively and send fifty copies to this address. Do you understand?" he said, handing over the sheet. He told the subeditor that he would return the day after next and hurried out. A delegation of devotees, who had struggled because they could not obtain enough camphor for the ritual waving of lights, had set out that day to meet Collector Brown. The Collector had gone to open a village hospital, so the delegation left for that village. Our editor was its leader, and even his name suited the task: Anumantha Rao. At home, however, there were occasions when he became the Ramayana's hero Hanuman, for his wife was named Seethalakshmi!

After saying so much, I have forgotten to name the newspaper. See what a forgetful man I am. Our paper was called *Vyasar*.

The editor of *Vyasar*, Anumantha Rao, took leave of his wife Seethalakshmi and entrusted the office to the subeditor Garudazhvar Mudaliar. Garudazhvar immersed himself in that week's guide to the horses running at the Guindy races. When, in my haste, I composed the editor's report full of errors, he merely asked, "Thambi, have you done it correctly?" Do not imagine from everyone calling me "Thambi, Thambi" that I was a young boy. I was forty years old, and my name was Thambi Mudaliar. He glanced at it once for form's sake and said, "All right, run it."

Would the printing press refuse to accept such nonsense? It carried all those distortions of meaning, gathered strength, and rolled rapidly through the press. At daybreak the papers were briskly wrapped into parcels. On the second night the office boy hammered at my door. I opened it and started. The editor had summoned me urgently, and I ran.

"Thambi, you have dropped a stone upon my head!" cried Editor Anumantha Rao. Garudazhvar was nowhere to be seen. The editor ordered me to bring the sheet he had given the paper. I brought it. He told me to read, and I read:

### A Prominent Wedding

Could the universe exist without a grocery shop? It could not! Though our town has many grocery shops, can any equal Pandari Vilas? It cannot! The wedding of Sowbhagyavathi Visalatchi Ammal, eldest daughter of Mukunda Raja Mudaliar, the uncrowned king of that establishment, took place with splendour this morning. The bridegroom, Mani Arasu Mudaliar, presented a brilliant sight wearing radiant diamond ear-studs. Srimathi Visalam, wearing a new necklace of gold coins, was a sight for the eyes. Knowing the Vedic devotion of Sri Mudaliar, eminent Brahmins recited the Vedas in the morning. This is no new custom; it was the tradition in the days of the ancient kings.

After the tying of the marriage pendant and all other customary rites had been happily completed, distinguished gentlemen from the town and elsewhere entered their wedding gifts. A fine music concert took place in the evening. One point, however, must be mentioned. The music was not in strict accordance with the classical treatises; it was apparently what some call Tamil music. A procession followed, and the whole town spoke of it. Crowning everything was Swami Kavadiyanandar's visit to the wedding both morning and evening. Seeing him converse intimately with our editor Anumantha Rao, many remarked that only the learned delight in the learned. It deserves mention that our editor, who has no taste for flattery, behaved as though he had not heard the praise.

That was what the editor had written. In print, Visalam became Visaram, the wedding became an argument, the gold necklace became jaundice, Mani Arasu became the king of illness, the Vedas became discrimination, the gifts became lies, and Swami Kavadiyanandar became Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar. They were all printing errors. But Anumantha Rao's cry - "Thambi, you have ruined my very life!" - made me understand how terrible my mistakes were.

He declared loudly, "Your account is settled. I no longer want you at work. Get out."

I went out. What else could I do? A compositor who published so many mistakes in one brief item could hardly remain employed in a press. I was miserable. Sleep would not come, and I suffered without knowing where to go.

The moment she heard I had lost my job, Velatha - my wife had been given the name of our family goddess - flew into a rage beyond description. "If by mistake there is one stone in your rice at one meal, how angry you become! You have worked at a press for all these years and still make so many errors. Who will employ you?" she said. I call it "said" merely for courtesy. The noise she raised made it seem as though the goddess Velathal herself had arrived.

I could not bear the pain. To the devil with family and all this nagging! I left the house without a word. As I walked, I reflected that there were many women like Velatha in the town and that I would have to beg from them. I feared they would sneer, "A great strapping fellow, and you have come to beg?" I need not become a real ascetic, I decided; I would merely stay away from home for a day and let Velatha cry, wondering what had happened.

After wandering everywhere, at about ten at night I lay down in a wayside rest house at the edge of the town. I was half asleep when I heard someone say, "Namasivayam." Swami Kavadiyanandar had entered the rest house. I did not rise from my corner or make a sound. Luckily he did not see me. I trembled at what might happen if he did. What if he asked, "You rascal, was it you who printed my name as Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar?" and cast some spell upon me? I was a man with wife and children. I curled up silently.

"What is it, Swami?" another voice replied. When the Swami said "Namasivayam," I had thought he was uttering the name of God. Only then did I learn that it was the name of a man.

The two talked of various things and then began discussing the wedding. My heart jumped. They were surely going to abuse me. As if to confirm it, Namasivayam asked, "Swami, who was it that wrote all that nonsense in the paper?"

The Swami laughed. "The writer was not at fault. The compositor he employs seems to be blind. He made all those mistakes while setting the type."

"You laugh, Swami. If the fellow who did it were before me..." Namasivayam growled.

Velatha's anger seemed preferable. Even if one's wife struck or scolded one, there was at least the consolation that she was one's own wife. This stranger from the road was grinding his teeth at me. What could I do in a place like that? I lay still.

The Swami began to defend me. "Even in his mistakes there is a great deal of truth, Namasivayam. To tell the truth, I performed the service of carrying the kavadi, built this rest house, and earned the name Swami Kavadiyanandar. The compositor's hand slipped and he printed Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar. Apart from that one, almost everything else is true."

I was shaken, and Namasivayam must have been too. I dared not speak; he had no such fear.

"You are joking, Swami! That fool printed 'Visalatchi's argument' instead of 'Visalatchi's wedding,' and you defend him!"

So I was a fool. What could I do? My wife had said the same in another language: "Your intelligence is the tender shoot of a pounding pestle." I grew angry. Yet here was this man emphatically calling me a fool. It was my bad time.

"Namasivayam," said the Swami, "printing 'argument' instead of 'wedding' was appropriate. Visalatchi did not desire this marriage at all. She argued with her mother about it. Thus what he printed was true; it merely emerged through the slip of his hand."

"Why did Visalatchi dislike it?" asked Namasivayam.

"What is the bridegroom's name?" the Swami asked.

"Mani Arasu."

"Did the fellow not print it as Pini Arasu - the king of illness?"

"Do you praise that genius for it?"

"His hand slipped and changed Mani Arasu to Pini Arasu. Yet it would not have been wrong even had he done it knowingly. The bridegroom really is ill. He has jaundice."

I swelled with delight.

"And what if the name of a woman being married to a sick man is Visalam? The compositor wrongly printed Visaram - sorrow - and that is precisely fitting," said the Swami.

Namasivayam fell silent, while my heart beat wildly. I wanted to rise and fall at the Swami's feet, but I restrained myself and remained lying down.

"When one thinks about it, not one thing the compositor did was wrong. The bridegroom wore a *kāsumālai*, and the fellow printed *kāmālai*. Since the man has jaundice, the spelling was wrong but the truth was not. He printed *bēdam*, discrimination, instead of *Vedam*. The Vedas did indeed arise to create caste, clan, and other divisions. Moreover, what those Brahmins recited that day was not proper Veda at all; there was a great deal of *bēdam* in it. Much of the *moi*, the wedding gifts entered during the half-trained fellows' chanting, was *poi*, falsehood. For empty prestige, they claimed that various people from distant towns had sent gifts of fifty or a hundred rupees. Thus printing *poi* instead of *moi* was also right."

Namasivayam's anger left me and turned upon Mukunda Raja Mudaliar.

"Did he give his daughter in marriage to a sick man? Poor girl! Did he force that jaundiced fellow upon her even after she refused?" he railed. "We permit such wrongs, bless them, say it is his wish, and call it each person's fate. That one man bent two lives to suit his desire. We cannot correct it and must not even condemn it. The poor compositor merely let his hand slip while setting the type, and I called him fool and blind. Looking at the matter properly, it seems we ought to put a golden ornament upon his hand!"

I was overjoyed. He did not need to give me a golden ornament; his words themselves were like diamonds and cooled my heart. After discussing the Tevaram, Tiruvachakam, and other sacred matters, Namasivayam took his leave.

I wondered whether I should now rise, fall at the Swami's feet, and worship him. But fear remained. The Swami had justified every one of my errors, but had I not called the Swami himself Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar?

Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar too could not sleep. He lay singing. At about midnight someone approached.

"Why, the singing is splendid!" said a voice.

I started. A woman's voice! It was Pachai, a woman I knew, who swept and cleaned the grocery shop. She was coming to the rest house at midnight. So this really was Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar, the lustful man of the rest house! What error had I made even there? What happened next is hardly fit to tell. Was he a holy man, Kavadiyanandar? He was finding bliss in the rest house. Had he been singing a Tevaram? It was a dance tune. Did Pachai ask him for sacred ash? "Give it here! The money! You have been putting me off for seven days," she demanded.

The rogue poured clinking rupees from his sacred-ash pouch and gave Pachai five.

"Put the balance here, Swami," I said, stepping beside them.

The mendicant howled, and she stared like an owl. I did not let him speak.

"You posed as a man of great integrity and told Namasivayam all my other mistakes were right. Should I have printed your name as Swami Kavadiyanandar? Are you not Kāmi Chāvadiyānandar?"

Once I had caught the fellow red-handed, what was there to fear? With fear gone, my wits began to work more sharply.

"Did I say my hand slipped and I changed the letters? I made no mistake. I knew the whole truth when I set it. Hand over the money respectfully," I said.

You may be angry that I was committing robbery. But compared with the plunder this womanising rogue had amassed in the guise of a holy man, what corner did my act occupy? He gave me the money. Did I stop there?

"The owner of our press is your friend. You must somehow speak to him and get me taken back into the job. Understand?"

"All right," he said tearfully.

In the darkness of midnight I walked home without a trace of fear. Those two afflictions followed at some distance. I reached the house and hammered boldly on the door.

"Velatha! Vel! Vela! Hey, Velatha!" I called in several styles and pitches. Why should I be afraid? I had eight rupees - the money the fraud had tied up and surrendered.

"What is this authority that wakes the whole street?" Velatha chanted as she opened the door. She saw command in eyes that usually displayed fear, but could not suppress her anger.

"Have you no concern that your job is gone? You roam the town all night!"

"You donkey! Don't say the job is gone. Say the bad time we had is gone! Tomorrow I must go back to work," I said, pouring out the eight rupees.

What a look she gave me! How can I describe it? Again and again she asked why, how, and what had happened. She even used the old endearments from long ago - "my king," "my darling." At last I told her the whole story. In her delight - I am almost ashamed to say it - she imagined herself a maiden and me a new bridegroom, cried, "Ah, my king!" and even gave me a kiss.

*17 February 1949*

## 2. Write What I Tell You!

"Hey, Mandu! Have you eaten? All right, sit down for a while. My hand aches a little. I shall dictate; write it neatly. Do you understand?"

"Very well, uncle. A letter?"

"What does it matter to you what it is? Write what I tell you!"

"Ah! I will bring the letter paper."

"Let me see how clever you are. Which letter paper are you bringing?"

"They call you Mandu - blockhead - and rightly so! We are now writing to Diwan Bahadur Theerthagiri Mudaliar. That paper will not do. Go and bring the letter paper headed 'Raghavachariar, President, Sri Rama Bhakta Jana Sabha.'"

"The one with Hanuman's picture?"

"Why, yes - the journalist's letter paper."

"That one, Mandu! That one!"

Uncle and nephew habitually spoke to each other in this playful way. Not everywhere, of course; this was the custom in Raghavachariar's house.

At times Raghavachariar would ask even his daughter Rukku, "Why, girl, our Rukku looks splendid today! If her husband saw her, would he not faint?" At other times he would tease her, "Why are you standing there rubbing your chin? Has Sundaram bitten it?" Sundaram was Rukku's husband. He too would joke with his father-in-law: "I do not understand the game Father-in-law is playing."

"Mandu" was another nephew, a relation by courtesy rather than one confirmed by marriage, for Raghavachariar had no other daughter to give him. He had summoned Varadan with a promise to find him a job. It was Varadan who bore the title Mandu.

Mandu returned with the letter paper and sat down. The Iyer began dictating.

"To the distinguished Diwan Bahadur Theerthagiri Mudaliar..."

Mandu swiftly wrote it and repeated, "...Mudaliar."

"Hey! Did you put 'Sri Rama Jayam' at the top?" asked Raghavachariar.

"No. You did not tell me."

"Go on! Must even that be dictated?"

Mandu added it and asked him to continue.

"May every blessing befall Mudaliar and all his cherished wishes be fulfilled," Raghavachariar dictated. While Mandu wrote, he remarked, "Do you know what that man's wishes are? He wants to cut off the town's marriage pendant, make the whole country tremble at the name Diwan Bahadur Mudaliar, and buy the Governor's bungalow. Things of that sort."

Mandu stopped writing. "Why, uncle, is he so greedy? Then why bless such a man with the fulfilment of every wish?"

"Will all his desires come true merely because I say so? He gains nothing from it, and what do we lose by writing the words? Send them, and the fellow will grin from ear to ear. What else? Write!"

Mandu wrote, and Raghavachariar resumed.

"However much hardship I suffer, it is never my habit to tell others..."

At this Mandu did not write. He laughed.

"Uncle, what a stroke! You spend all your time telling everyone from the woman sweeping the doorstep to every visitor and passer-by about your troubles. Yet you ask me to write that you never tell others!"

He was Mandu, after all, and questioned his uncle. Did he know that his uncle's special methods had borne fruit as diamond earrings in his aunt's ears, a chain around Rukku's neck, and a wristwatch on the elder son-in-law's arm? How could the uncle explain every method to him? Raghavachariar glared, and Mandu wrote.

"You know that my son Panchu is studying in the B.A. class," he dictated.

Mandu silently wrote it down.

"Why do you not question that? You ought to ask how I can call him Panchu when he signs his name R. P. Nathan," Raghavachariar mocked.

The son's name was Panchanathan. His father shortened it affectionately to Panchu; the fashionable young man shortened it to R. P. Nathan in his signature. Mandu decided the matter was not very important.

"Go on, uncle."

"Only Sri Ramachandra himself knows the difficulties I endure in meeting Panchu's college expenses," said Raghavachariar.

Mandu grunted assent as he wrote.

"This month especially, the anniversary of the Rama Bhakta Jana Sabha has involved enormous expenditure."

Mandu wrote. He knew exactly where in the house lay the grapes, almonds, cashews, dhoti, silver plate, and numerous other articles obtained under cover of that anniversary. What could he do with his knowledge? He had to write what Raghavachariar dictated.

"Therefore, please show me great kindness and give the boy fifty rupees."

"Fifty?" Mandu asked once. He doubted whether Panchanathan needed money at all. The young man had even sent his father by money order the twenty rupees he earned acting in a radio play. In their joy they had prepared sweet rice as an offering to God. Why then did Panchu need fifty rupees?

"Yes, fifty, fool! Would one ask a man of such importance for a mere five or ten? Are we the people who drive his household cart?" Raghavachariar explained. "At this moment these fifty rupees are equal to five hundred to me."

Mandu did not understand that either. Raghavachariar's elder brother was the local priest who caused donors to be told that whatever a household gave, however little it might be, should be regarded as a great amount. Was Raghavachariar not a priest of the modern method?

"Your charitable thought, your compassionate nature..." Raghavachariar continued.

Mandu wrote it and waited, but his uncle paused in thought.

"Why, uncle? I have written 'compassionate nature' too."

"Wait! The more flattery, the better. It will especially cool that fellow's head with pleasure. 'Charitable thought' and 'compassionate nature' are not enough. We must add something more. You tell me what."

"Shall we put 'devotion to Brahmins'?" Mandu suggested.

"Idiot! We must not use that phrase. This is a time when the whole town mocks devotion to Brahmins. Even this man helps us only when it suits him. When he talks to others, he routinely abuses our entire community as 'these Brahmin fellows.' Once he reviled us right in front of me," Raghavachariar said, explaining the Diwan Bahadur's character.

"In front of you? Did you remain quiet, uncle?"

"Instead of remaining quiet, should I pick a quarrel with him? Which matters more, getting the business done or displaying courage? I listened without speaking. Then he himself wondered whether I might be angry, looked at me, and smilingly asked, 'Why, Swami, are you angry that he speaks ill of our community?' I replied, 'Not at all, Mudaliar! You spoke only according to the ways of the world.' The fellow was enormously pleased. 'Your case is different, Swami. I did not criticise Brahmins with your character and heart; I meant only the others,' he said to pacify me. That day he gave ten rupees more than usual towards the coronation ceremony at the Ramayana recital by our Narasimhachariar. Such a man is that Diwan Bahadur."

After a little further thought he added the phrase, "blessed with divine grace." He completed the sentence: "There is no need to say more to one such as you, endowed with charitable thought, compassion, and divine grace."

The letter ended. Mandu gave a weary yawn.

"While your hand is moving, write another letter to Panchu," said Raghavachariar.

Mandu sat down again.

"Use the journalist's letter paper. 'Blessings to dear Panchanathan. Today, by this same post, I have written to the Diwan asking him to give you fifty rupees urgently. He will give it. Collect it, keep five or ten rupees if you need money for expenses, and with the balance immediately buy and send your sister the six-yard orange Bengal silk sari said to be sold in China Bazaar. The District Collector is coming here next Thursday, apparently to raise funds for the army. Our Rukku, Rajam from the house opposite, and Shanbagam, wife of the deaf gentleman in the corner house, are all to perform a Bengal dance. Hence the sari is urgent.

"'Theerthagiri Mudaliar is somewhat hot-tempered and will snap at you. Ignore it and wait a little; he will give you the money. Speak to him tactfully. He loves flattery and is a great libertine, but he will not refuse us money. He is the patron of our Rama Bhakta Jana Sabha. I have made him believe that the Sabha's support was the very reason he received the title Diwan Bahadur. If an opportunity arises, you too say so. Somehow get the money. Do not behave like a simpleton.'"

This was a father's letter to his son, and Mandu wrote it without difficulty or interruption, for he had now understood Raghavachariar's method.

"Good heavens, I forgot! One more line must be added to Panchu's letter," said Raghavachariar.

"Tell me. There is room."

"When you go to Theerthagiri's house, wrap a few dried loose flowers and some turmeric powder in a paper packet, take it with you, and say your father sent it from the village."

With that instruction he completed the letter.

Raghavachariar's letters began their journey. Thinking of the Bengal silk sari, Rukku began practising her steps. They themselves looked like a dance of a new kind.

The letters arrived, but the one intended for Mudaliar reached Panchu, while Panchu received the envelope's other intended contents in reverse: the private letter meant for the son went to Mudaliar. How could the Bengal silk now be obtained?

Diwan Bahadur Theerthagiri Mudaliar abused Raghavachariar as an unworthy, ungrateful, deceitful man and threatened to kick Panchu. Only after Panchu wrote his father a detailed account - enclosing the letter addressed to himself that had arrived in Mudaliar's envelope, and explaining how Mandu had put the two letters into the wrong covers - did Raghavachariar understand the disaster Mandu had caused. He sank into grief.

"I only wrote what you told me," Mandu muttered.

Raghavachariar struck his own head. "You are not Mandu. I am Mandu - for involving you in this affair. I must blame myself!"

He thought deeply for a little while. Then he called, "Hey, Mandu, come here! Even if I abandon you, I have no other recourse. Go and bring the journalist's letter paper."

In a voice heavy with sorrow Raghavachariar dictated, and Mandu silently wrote:

### Rama Bhakta Jana Sabha

"Members complained to the president, Sriman Raghavachariar, that Diwan Bahadur Theerthagiri Mudaliar, until now patron of this town's Sri Rama Bhakta Jana Sabha, had been acting contrary to religious observance. The president examined the matter and found grounds for the complaint. He has therefore removed the said Theerthagiri Mudaliar from the office of patron. The chief among the many accusations against him is that he has been conducting an improper relationship with a Brahmin widow."

"To whom shall I send it, uncle? Tell me clearly," Mandu asked fearfully.

"Make ten copies and send them to every newspaper - *Mithran*, *Hindu Vikatan*, *Mani*, and the rest," Raghavachariar ordered.

Mandu carried out the command. Then, still frightened, he asked, "Uncle, do not be angry. Tell me just this: who is that Brahmin widow?"

"Our Kamali," Raghavachariar replied.

Kamali was his wife's cousin.

*24 February 1946*

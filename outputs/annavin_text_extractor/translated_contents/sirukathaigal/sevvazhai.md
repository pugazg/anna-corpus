## English Translation

Sengodan raised that red banana sapling like his own cherished child. Even if he returned after dark, he ignored the exhaustion of his labour in the fields and first went to the backyard. Only after seeing the red banana plant and checking whether it had received enough water would he speak to his four children.

He tended it with such love. As the sapling grew, his delight grew with it.

When he watered the plant and loosened the stony soil, his eyes displayed such joy that even Kariyan, his eldest son, felt surprised and a little jealous that his father showed it greater love and concern than he showed him.

“The red banana fruit will be round and stout, and very tasty. Merely looking at it will satisfy hunger,” Sengodan would boast to Kuppi.

All four children delighted in his words. More than that, they repeated the same proud claims to the children in neighbouring and opposite huts.

What else could children from a farm labourer’s household talk about? Could they discuss the new motorcar their father had bought, their mother’s diamond earrings, or the radio their elder brother had brought home?

The red banana sapling was their motorcar, radio, diamond necklace, and everything else.

The eldest boy, Kariyan, would say, “When the red banana puts out a bunch, one hand of fruit belongs to me.”

“Will you not give me even one? I gave you a mango, remember. I gave you roasted groundnuts too, remember,” Ellappan from the opposite hut would say.

Kariyan’s sister Kamakshi would blink mischievously and say, “If you get one hand, I get two, understand? One by asking mother and one by asking father.”

The third boy, Muthu, would say, “Do not calculate your shares and then be deceived. Who knows what someone may do before the fruit ripens?”

He did not say this merely in jest. He had already resolved somehow to eat more fruit than all the others.

The red banana grew as Sengodan’s cherished child.

The labour in the field was heavy, and the farm manager’s bullying was greater still. Sengodan endured it all. The moment he saw the red banana, he forgot everything.

When the children cried, he consoled them by showing them the plant. When a child behaved badly, he threatened the child by invoking the red banana.

Sengodan imagined how lovingly the children would eat its fruit. The children at the landlord’s house could eat apples and grapes. How could Kariyan and Muthu obtain such expensive fruit?

The desire to give them red bananas and fill them with delight made Sengodan tend the sapling so carefully.

However hard the labourer Sengodan worked, how could he accumulate the money needed to buy fruit and treats for his children? The paddy received as wages barely filled half their stomachs. Kuppi’s daily struggle helped a little with the family’s hunger.

Such was their livelihood. The largest share of the produce went to the farm.

This one red banana plant alone was labour from which he could receive the entire benefit as his own. The landlord could not intervene to claim a share in it, could he?

After spending his working hours labouring for the landlord, Sengodan worked upon the red banana during the time left for exhausted sleep. He nurtured it like his own eyes. Its entire benefit would belong to his family.

Sengodan rejoiced that in this one thing at least, he could personally receive the proper fruit of his labour.

These were not clearly formulated thoughts in his mind. Like a veil of smoke, the idea appeared and disappeared. Yet it was the reason he felt both delight and pride whenever he saw the red banana.

The sapling grew without deceit, and Sengodan’s joy grew too.

For his children, the place where the red banana stood had now become their playground. As women love flowers and bees love honey, the children came to love the plant.

“Will it put out a bunch in another month, father?” Kariyan would ask Sengodan eagerly.

“It will take two months, my boy,” Sengodan would reply.

The red banana put out its bunch. A new spring entered Sengodan’s very step. He stood erect and looked upon the bunch with pride.

Farm owner Paranthama Mudaliar would not have looked with such pride even upon the diamond necklace adorning the golden body of his daughter-in-law Muthuvijaya.

To Sengodan’s eyes, the red banana bunch appeared more valuable than Muthuvijaya’s diamond necklace.

As the bunch matured, the children’s eagerness, quarrels, disputes over shares, and appeals to father or mother all increased.

“When will it ripen?” asked one child.

“How many more days will it remain upon the tree?” asked another.

Sengodan intended to judge the proper stage, cut the bunch, ripen it carefully, and give it to the children.

It was the fruit of his labour. They would receive the whole benefit. There was no intermediary to seize three quarters of it.

His labour was his own. The landowner took most of the red paddy because the field belonged to him, even though the labour belonged to Sengodan. But the red banana was different. Both labour and ownership belonged to Sengodan.

He decided to cut the bunch in two days. The children jumped with joy.

The “news” flew to the children in other homes. In return for promises of fruit, many children gave Kariyan advances of flattened rice, groundnuts, tubers, tender vegetables, and all manner of things.

“We laboured and are about to receive the benefit. Nothing equals the happiness this gives. How delightful it would be if all our labour similarly yielded its full benefit to us.

“The work spent upon the red banana is not even one hundredth of the work spent upon the landlord’s field. But because the labour is ours and the field is his property, he enjoys the greater share of its benefit.

“Here, we worked and raised this red banana in our own backyard, so the benefit comes to us. How happy we would be if we possessed a little field of our own upon which to labour and live.

“Will such a time come? Will a time ever come when people say the land belongs to the labourer and the man who does not work must not remain a landlord?”

Sengodan began vaguely to entertain even such thoughts.

The red banana stirred these doctrines in his mind. The children’s mouths, meanwhile, began to water.

As Sengodan rejoiced over the bunch, farm owner Paranthamar was arranging an elaborate birthday celebration for his daughter-in-law Muthuvijaya. He had instructed the Iyer to conduct ritual bathing and worship at the Ambika temple. He called the accountant and told him to prepare a list.

While noting many provisions, how could the need for fruit fail to arise?

“Two hands of bananas,” said the landlord.

“Why bananas, sir? There is no good fruit in the shop, only unripe green varieties,” drawled Sundaram, the accountant.

“All right, buy two hands of those. Where else is there any good fruit?”

Before the landlord could finish, Sundaram said, “There is a fine bunch of red bananas in our Sengodan’s backyard. We can bring that.”

“Very well,” said the landlord.

Sengodan’s red banana bunch. His joyful dream. The fruit of his labour. The children’s delight.

Sundaram had prepared its death warrant.

For so many days, Sengodan’s entire family had watched and rejoiced over that bunch. Sundaram became its killer.

Danger came to the bunch that had given them happiness, pride, and hope.

When Sundaram spoke with Sengodan in the street, the children did not imagine the subject could be the red banana.

Sengodan’s head spun, his tongue faltered, and words surged upward only to become trapped in his throat.

Sundaram gave the reason: a birthday worship ceremony for the landlord’s daughter-in-law.

What could Sengodan do? What could he say?

How could he tell of the desire that had grown in his heart with the banana plant, the desire that made his children’s mouths water, and the eagerness with which they had counted the days, saying “today” and “tomorrow”?

The person asking was farm owner Paranthamar.

Sengodan imagined the whole village saying: “What a petty man you are! The landlord opened his mouth, considered you a person of consequence, and sent to ask for the banana bunch. Yet you refused. You live eating his salt. Can you be so ungrateful? It is merely one banana bunch. Is that anything remarkable to a man of his status?”

In his mind’s eye, he saw his children asking through tears:

“Father, do not awaken desire and then deceive us. I watered it. I guarded it so the cattle would not trample it. You said the red banana would be delicious and round as an egg.

“Father, little sister’s very life is in that fruit. Her mouth waters merely looking up at the tree. You promised to give it to us, and now you deceive us.

“Did we ask you to go to the shop and spend money on grapes or oranges? Did we not raise it ourselves in our own backyard?”

He saw his wife asking angrily, “Is it right to make the children cry?”

But before him stood the farm accountant.

Sengodan went to fetch the sickle.

“Father is going to cut the red banana bunch!” the children cried joyfully and danced.

Sparks rose in Sengodan’s eyes. He cut the bunch and brought it inside. He put down the sickle.

“Set the bunch down, father. Let us touch it,” the children said.

Sengodan stroked Kariyan’s back.

“My boy, the master wants this bunch. I am taking it to him. Do not cry. In another month, the sapling beside it will become a tree and put out a bunch. I shall certainly give that one to you,” he said.

He left the house before the children’s cries could split his heart.

That day, Sengodan’s hut resembled a place where a corpse had fallen.

Only very late at night did Sengodan find the courage to return home. He looked at the children, who had fallen asleep exhausted by crying. Tears poured from his eyes. He wiped them away, lay down, and tossed as a thousand thoughts filled his mind.

What benefit came from raising the red banana like a beloved child?

To the landlord, it was nothing remarkable. He could buy a thousand bunches merely by willing it. But to Sengodan?

How hard he had worked merely to see that one bunch. How many nights he had dreamed pleasant dreams about it. How many thousands of times had he awakened desire in the children? How much labour and care. Everything was destroyed in a moment.

Four days later, beautiful Muthuvijaya walked with the gait of a swan to the Ambika temple, carrying one hand of red bananas upon a silver plate.

Even after four days of consolation, the children’s grief had not subsided.

Kariyan stubbornly demanded one fruit. Kuppi took an old quarter-anna coin and sent him to buy one.

Kariyan flew to the shop.

A hand of red bananas hung there beautifully. The accountant had already taken four hands from the landlord’s house and sold them to the shopkeeper.

Kariyan stood before them longingly.

“One banana costs an anna, boy. Can you buy a red banana for a quarter anna? Go away,” the shopkeeper drove him off.

How could poor Kariyan understand the wonder by which the red banana from his own backyard was now enthroned in a shop?

For so many days, the boy had watered it in the expectation of receiving fruit. The fruit existed, but in a place beyond Kariyan’s reach.

He returned home sadly, chewing roasted groundnuts he had bought instead.

Sengodan emerged from the backyard carrying a piece of the banana trunk.

“Why, father? Is that too for the landlord’s house?” asked Kariyan.

“No, my boy. Our grandmother Parvathi has died. It is to tie upon her funeral bier,” said Sengodan.

A piece of the red banana trunk adorned the bier. Cries of grief surrounded it.

Kariyan and the other children walked behind.

Proudly pointing to the bier, Kariyan said, “That is the red banana from our house.”

“We gave the red banana bunch from our backyard to the landlord’s house and cut the tree to tie upon the bier,” Kariyan said.

Poor boy, he was only a child. What did he know of Sengodan’s red banana being an utterly ordinary event in the world of labourers?

1 May 1949

## English Translation

“I, who should have been enthroned in a temple, have ended up upon a rubbish heap. I committed no offence. A sculptor’s mistake reduced me to this state.

“No human being would have time to hear my sorrowful story. At least you listen. I tell it to you because your fate resembles mine.

“You will not say, ‘What indignity! A stone tells a story, and we must listen to it?’ After all, you are a broken shard of mirror.

“Do not become angry and ask, ‘Not content with breaking me into pieces, do you now tell me to listen to your story? What audacity!’ I am telling the truth. There is no fault in me. I never entertained the slightest thought of harming you.

“I lay at the edge of the street, grieving over my own fate. That rough man picked me up and threw me. It was he who shattered you.

“What injustice! I sincerely regret that this happened to you because of me. I too know sorrow. I have endured so many troubles.

“An effort was once made in this town to build a hundred-pillared hall. As the sculptor carved the pillars with his chisel, I was filled with boundless delight.

“I imagined that I would be enthroned in the temple, that thousands of people would admire my beauty, and that even newspapers would publish my photograph. Foreigners would behold me in astonishment, take photographs, and show them to all their friends. My fame would be discussed in many countries.

“That delight enabled me to endure even the pain inflicted by the chisel.

“But I did not receive that fortune. I had committed no fault. I bore the sharpness of the sculptor’s chisel. I do not know what troubled his mind, but a defect occurred in his work. He lifted me, cast me down, and sought another stone for the pillar ornament.

“I, who should have gone to a holy place, became a street wanderer. I suffered for a long time, growing worn, exhausted, and deformed.

“I grieved that my cherished hope had not been realised and that I had become useless. While the other stones that had been with me occupied exalted positions, I reached this condition.

“I would ask myself: ‘What wrong did I do to deserve this fate?’ I longed to tell someone, but who would come forward to listen to a stone?

“I suppressed my sorrow and waited eagerly for an opportunity. Even if I could not obtain a place enthroned in a temple, might I not find some other worthy position that people could see and praise?

“After a very long time, an opportunity came. It was a good one. They were supposedly implementing some Five-Year Plan in the country, and through it I received my chance.

“The town’s great men, educated men, and men in government laid a new road. Ordinarily, roads are laid by labourers earning eight annas or one rupee. Those were not the kind of people who touched and lifted me.

“The man who picked me up first removed the diamond rings from his fingers and put them away safely as a precaution. They even took photographs.

“I found a place in the new road. A minister himself came to open it. There was a procession of new motorcars, a band concert, showers of flowers, and a grand celebration.

“What if I had not found a place in the temple? Do only devotees visit temples? Are not daytime impostors more numerous than devotees, and bats more numerous still?

“Instead of remaining in such a place, had I not obtained a position where ministers, statesmen, landlords, title-holders, and office-holders would walk and offer praise?

“I became happy. One might even call it pride.

“Did that happiness last? Would I have been fortunate enough for that? The road praised by great leaders fell into disrepair within three months.

“I was the first to leave the road. It was not my fault. The men who laid it had not pressed me properly into place. They showed far more concern about whether photographs were being taken than about whether the pit was deep enough, enough earth had been added, the ground had been sufficiently compacted, the stone was properly seated, or everything had been prepared to prevent it from emerging.

“Poor men, they were not experienced in laying roads. They were accustomed to estates and landed property, courts and forts, shops and markets. They acquired the ‘glory’ of having spent five thousand rupees.

“After merely two rains, I was brought out. I lost both my place and the position I had obtained and became a wanderer once again.

“You tell me: did I commit any offence in this? I was kicked, thrown, and rolled by rainwater. To whom could I speak and have my grievance remedied? I suffered beyond endurance and lay like a mendicant for many days.

“I had found no place in the temple. Now there was no place for me upon the road laid by those enthroned in government.

“As I lay grieving, wondering what fate could remain for me, a peasant gathered me and other stones, mixed us with mud, and used us to seal a crack in the earthen wall of his garden hut.

“At first I felt sad. I had dreamed of being enthroned in a temple and ended in a hut. Was this the place allotted to me?

“Later I consoled myself. Can temples, or ministers enthroned in forts, live and grow without the offerings and taxes paid by poor people in huts? Therefore, I thought, obtaining a place in the poor man’s hut, the foundation of everything, was truly an exalted position.

“My mind even found peace.

“But did that happiness and peace last? No.

“That cultivator worked like an ox and wore himself out, yet could not live. Poverty overtook him. Unable even to repay the debt incurred for cultivation, he fled the village to wash his stomach with daily-wage labour in the city.

“I became an orphan. No one looked after the hut, and it became a ruin. Another crack appeared. I came out again and became a ball beneath many feet.

“I became furious with the whole world. I had committed no wrong. A sculptor’s inattention denied me a place in the temple. Falling into the hands of men who did not know how to lay a road prevented me from remaining there. The failure of rulers to sustain the cultivator cost me my place in the hut.

“I decided that if I was ever to be useful, I must serve some good act that destroyed cruelty.

“But I was only a stone. I could do nothing by myself. A proper person had to come and use me properly. I waited in penance, thinking, ‘He will come, he will come.’

“A twelve-year-old boy came.

“‘Help! Snake! Snake!’ he cried. He picked me up, took aim, and threw me at the snake.

“The snake leapt and fell, then remained motionless.

“I was overjoyed. No longer would I bear the bad name of being useless. I had helped destroy wickedness. The snake had died because of me, and I had contributed what I could to the world. I felt both joy and pride.

“The boy too jumped with delight.

“‘Uncle! Uncle!’ he shouted.

“An elderly man came.

“‘Look, a snake! I struck it with a stone. It is dead. A big snake, over there near the fence,’ the boy cried.

“The elder laughed loudly. Patting the boy’s back, he said: ‘My distant tiger! You mean that kattuviriyan? My lion cub, I struck it once with my walking stick half an hour ago, killed it, and threw it there. Did you kill a snake that was already dead?’

“He laughed again.

“The boy felt ashamed. Was he alone? Even I felt ashamed.

“I had been satisfied that I had finally accomplished one good deed. In the end, I learned that the snake had not died because of me. I had merely fallen upon a dead snake.

“I did not even wish to look at it. I grieved deeply that I had reached such a contemptible state.

“Many days later, great commotion arose where I lay. Police moved about, officials inspected, and cultivators gathered.

“A serious dispute had arisen between the cultivators and the landlord. The landlord had supposedly gone to purchase diamond jewellery for his third daughter’s wedding. After completing that work, he returned to the village.

“The cultivators were starving because the crop had failed. They went to ask him for money. He said, ‘Impossible! Nothing is available!’

“One cultivator replied, ‘There is nothing for any of this, but there is money to buy diamond jewellery.’

“The landlord tied him up and beat him.

“The cultivators assembled as though marching as an army. The landlord sent word that the whole village was in danger, and a police force arrived.

“The cultivators withdrew because they did not wish to clash with the police. Peace prevailed.

“The landlord did not like that. One of his attendants therefore threw stones at the place where the police were staying.

“I was the first object that sinner seized. I fell upon an iron helmet, not even upon a policeman’s head.

“The police panicked. Whistles shrilled. They entered the huts. Within an hour came baton blows and gunfire. The whole village was in turmoil.

“Many cultivators were arrested for supposedly rioting and throwing stones at the police. A case was tried. The thrown stones were placed before the presiding judge, and I was among them. He even picked me up and examined me.

“They said the cultivators had taken me and thrown me at the police. I knew that the man who threw me was the landlord’s own servant.

“But would they listen to anything I said? Even a god in stone form does not speak. I was merely a stone, one unfit even for a temple.

“The lawyer who came to speak for the landlord was supposedly a man of great learning. They said he received one thousand rupees.

“Would his speech prevail, or the speech of a stone?

“I remained silent, and many were punished.

“They were like me: punished for an offence they had not committed.

“What benefit came from my grief that injustice was occurring before my eyes, that I could neither prevent it nor argue for justice? I was only a contemptible stone.

“Great men and educated men watched the injustice take place. If even they could not prevent it, what could I, a little stone, do?

“I decided that everything might as well go to ruin. I had grown that disgusted with life.

“I became a street wanderer again and lost hope of ever finding a good position. I understood that this world had no place for the good. My condition became simply to fall and lie wherever anyone picked me up and threw me.

“It was then that a man came running, shouting wildly. Many people pursued him, crying, ‘Madman! Madman!’

“Poor man, what troubles might he have endured? Who knows which suffering had disordered his mind?

“Suddenly he picked me up and threw me at this house. The window was open, so I came directly towards you. You broke into a hundred fragments.

“That is what happened. Do not be angry with me. The world reduced and exhausted me into this condition. I committed no offence.

“Forgive me. If you are furious and wish to abuse me, do so. I shall endure it. After bearing so much humiliation and trouble, it will not be difficult to endure four pained words from you.

“At any rate, I have told you my story. Even if you cannot show compassion, it is enough if you understand me. I am not one who inflicts cruelty. I am one subjected to it.”

When the stone finished its story, the shard of mirror looked upon it with pity and spoke.

“Truly, I am not angry with you. I do not even regret that I broke into pieces. In one sense, it was proper that I should shatter. I had grown disgusted with my life. At the very moment I thought I should no longer exist, you struck and broke me. I should even thank you,” it said.

The stone was bewildered.

“What caused such disgust in you? I lay upon rough ground and became disturbed. But you lived in an upper-storey house and could remain polished and bright. Why such bitterness?” it asked.

The shard replied:

“I lived without any deficiency. I served as companion to an eighteen-year-old maiden who was pure gold in body and heart and always laughed merrily.

“At least twenty times a day, that flowering creeper stood before me. She came to me to learn whether there was enough powder upon her face, whether the mark upon her forehead sat correctly, and whether the darkening upon her eyebrows was sufficient. When she wore a new sari, she came first to look at me.

“She had such affection for me, and such love existed between us.

“Do you know what happened to that golden creeper?

“I am a mirror and you are a stone. We grieve over the fates that befell us. You have told your whole story.

“But that young woman lost her flower, her forehead mark, and her smile. From eyes once like blossoms, tears of blood now pour in torrents.

“I was her companion, delighting again and again in her youth, beauty, adornment, and inner happiness. How could I now consent to show her the appearance and agony of widowhood?

“That doe-eyed girl used to spring before me. Will she ever come again? If she stands before me, what suffering will seize her heart?

“I who displayed beauty, must I now display anguish? Should I remain here? No, no.

“I had been suffering with these thoughts. By good fortune, you came as though to relieve my pain, so that I might break and scatter.

“She needed me when she had found a bridegroom and lived happily. She has become a widow. Why should she need me now?

“Must I remain where a widow lives and behold, and show back to her, the maiden who once stood shyly with lowered head when she first met her bridegroom, now grown thin and dark, longing, sighing, shedding tears, and grieving?

“No, no. It was proper that I broke. My life has ended. That was precisely what I desired. You have rendered me a very great service,” the shard said.

The stone lay bewildered, unable to reply.

The master of the house called the servant woman.

“What can be done? Such a son-in-law himself is gone. Is losing this standing mirror so remarkable? Sweep everything up and throw it into the sewer pit,” he said.

The stone and the pieces of mirror were thrown into the sewer pit.

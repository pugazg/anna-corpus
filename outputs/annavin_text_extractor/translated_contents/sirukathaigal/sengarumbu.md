## English Translation

Tamil title: செங்கரும்பு

He had bought that bull for two hundred rupees. The elders of the village rejoiced, thinking that Chinnappan had run his household frugally, saved money, and purchased a fine animal. Whenever they rebuked farmers who neglected their work and offered them advice, they cited Chinnappan as an example.

"He is intelligent and thrifty. He never wanders off on useless errands, works without tiring, and saves instead of wasting money. Look at him! A man who has laboured so well now owns a splendid pair. He will work even better and reap the reward," they said.

It was all true that Chinnappan was meek, hardworking, and no spendthrift. The bull he brought home was indeed magnificent, and he had indeed paid two hundred rupees for it. But it was not wholly true that he had bought it solely from his savings. The old men spoke without knowing the details. Chinnappan did not correct them, because for the moment he benefited from such talk spreading.

The reason was his daughter Sengarumbu. She had reached marriageable age. For two years the village elders had been pestering him: "Chinnappa, is it enough to attend only to the forest and field? You have a grown daughter. Should you not arrange her marriage at the proper time? Is it right to let the days pass without attending to it?"

How long could he keep answering, "What is the hurry? We shall see. I too am thinking about it"? When Chinnappan finally gave serious attention to his daughter's marriage, it occurred to him that prospective in-laws would respect him only if he owned at least one good pair of bulls. He therefore added fifty rupees borrowed on credit to the hundred and fifty he had saved and brought home the bull.

"You are asking far too much, elder brother," Chinnappan had told the seller.

"What is wrong in telling you the truth, Chinnappa? I am letting it go sixty rupees below its worth only because hard times have fallen upon me. The two hundred you give me, I swear before God, will go entirely to repaying debt. Not even a small coin will remain in my hand."

Arunachalam was experienced in lending to villagers and strictly recovering principal and interest. Chinnappan had borrowed from him. Indeed, Arunachalam himself had advised how to arrange the purchase.

After taking a written note from Chinnappan, Arunachalam gave him the loan. Chinnappan combined it with the money he had brought and handed the sum to Nallannan. Arunachalam took the money from Nallannan and allowed Chinnappan to untie Karuppan, the beautiful bull tethered behind his shop, and lead it away.

Everyone who saw the handsome animal praised Chinnappan's frugal household management. Chinnappan thought it would be good if this news reached the ears of people who came to see his daughter.

Kattiri had his own calculations.

"Let the sugarcane bring in money; then we can attend to the marriage. I hear Chinnappan's daughter is bright red and handsome, and a very spirited girl. They say Chinnappan is a prosperous farmer who paid two hundred rupees for a bull only last year. He owns two acres and cultivates another three on lease. He has one son and one daughter. The son is supposedly a watchman in a city mill. Altogether it is a match suited to us. But we too need some money. By God's grace this sugarcane crop looks satisfactory. Let us see. If it yields a little cash, we can seek Chinnappan's daughter for our Velusami."

Thinking this, Kattiri walked around his sugarcane field every day. The canes swayed as though encouraging him: "Yes, do exactly that."

Arunachalam had promised to take the entire crop and had advanced money while it was still growing. His plan was to fix the price when the proper moment came. "Would that generous man cheat us? Can we betray the man who trusted us with an advance?" Kattiri said righteously.

Then Arunachalam read in the daily paper that sugar production had exceeded demand and prices were falling, along with the figures offered as evidence. At once he resolved not to buy Kattiri's cane and somehow recover the advance. He wanted to finish the matter before the true situation became known. Kattiri could see only the cane before his eyes. Before experiencing it, how could he know the bitterness caused by falling prices?

An event favourable to Arunachalam occurred of its own accord.

Karuppannam Chettiar was a prominent figure in the new sugar mill in that region and a leader in the Congress. He had not laboured for many years to gain that position. Varadappan, who had ruined his life by joining the Salt Satyagraha, serving a year in prison, and earning the title "Vande Mataram," opposed Chettiar's dominance of the Congress committee. Many Congressmen patted Varadappan on the back in private, yet raised their hands for Karuppannan when the committee met. Unable to understand why, Varadappan was distressed. The reason shone plainly in Chettiar's account book.

Disappointed and bewildered, Vande Mataram began opposing Karuppannan. He progressed to condemning the sugar mill, then travelled from village to village campaigning vigorously: "Do not let the mill grow fat. Boil your own jaggery and you will earn more than the mill owner gives you." His meetings often ended in blows.

Arunachalam used this as his excuse.

One day Kattiri presided over a meeting conducted by Vande Mataram. That suited Arunachalam perfectly. He summoned Kattiri and questioned him angrily.

"Ah, so you have become a great leader now! Why should I have dealings with you?"

"Me, a leader? What nonsense! That Vande Mataram is a good young man, sir. When he holds a meeting, little boys throw stones. He asked me to sit as chairman so it would proceed peacefully. I said, 'Why not?' and sat on the bench. There was no other wrongdoing at all."

"I know nothing of that, Kattiri. If I buy your cane, that Vande Mataram will create a disturbance. Why invite needless trouble? Give me a note for the advance I paid you. Then, like a king, follow Vande Mataram's advice and boil jaggery yourself. You will make a great profit."

No amount of pleading moved him. Only after he had taken a debt note for the advance on the cane did Arunachalam drink his coffee.

Vande Mataram did not let the matter rest. He besieged Kattiri with persuasion. The cane became jaggery; Kattiri became a debtor; and Vande Mataram temporarily left for another district.

Kattiri resolved that his plan to seek Chinnappan's daughter for his son would have to wait until the next sugarcane crop. "Looks as though Sengarumbu will not be married this year either," the villagers said.

The bull, meanwhile, did not leave Chinnappan in peace. It kept consuming money. After a year the bull had grown lean, Chinnappan was exhausted, and Sengarumbu was worried. Her elder brother returned from his job at the Madras mill after a quarrel there.

Arunachalam shouted, "Let him pay the principal later, but should he not at least pay the interest? What has happened to this Chinnappan's word?"

Chinnappan fretted that he had to settle the debt before the dispute spread. Otherwise it would create embarrassment whenever someone came to arrange Sengarumbu's marriage. After turning over many plans, he decided to sell the bull, repay the debt, and use whatever remained to buy some old or inferior animal. Only after consulting the omens did he drive the bull to market.

There was an unusually large crowd that day because the Government was filming the market for the cinema. Police drove the raggedly dressed into a corner. They directed robust men and shapely women to move about in front while the camera recorded them.

After watching this spectacle for a while, Chinnappan tethered the bull beneath a tree, fed it, and waited for a buyer. Many inspected it.

"A huge beast! Feeding it would ruin a household," they said, and bought some lame, wasted animal for twenty or thirty rupees instead.

Nobody offered more than sixty. Chinnappan felt close to tears. He owed Arunachalam eighty rupees in principal and interest alone. He had come intending to pay that and take home some useless beast with the remainder. Seeing that no one would go above sixty made him furious.

Evening fell. When he returned and tied the bull in its shed, anger overcame him. Inside the house he found Uthandan, a relative who knew the villages around and stood in the relation of maternal uncle to him.

"Uncle! When did you come? How long has it been?"

"Everything must find its proper time. Well, have you found a place for Sengarumbu?"

"That is a fine question, Uncle. Am I alone responsible for finding one? If you find and tell me of a place, that will settle it."

"You speak cleverly. Very well, I have seen a place and came to tell you. A young man from my elder uncle's branch lives in Eechampadi. He cultivates a mango grove and flower garden and has no bad habits. In every way he suits you. He is a miser too, just like you. What do you say?"

"What is there for me to say? Have you asked her?"

"Whom? Sengarumbu?"

"Her mother."

"What would she say? She sees things in the cinema, you know. She says the boy should come and see the girl."

"Was I allowed to see her before I married her?"

"I have already said we consent. They suggest coming next Monday. What is your view?"

"Not next Monday, Uncle. The Monday after would be better. Our village maiden-goddess festival falls that day, so they can see that too."

"That is right. You are the one managing the maiden's temple, are you not?"

"Hush! Do not speak that way of divine service."

Chinnappan's mind began working intensely. When they came to see Sengarumbu, he must conduct the temple festival with some splendour. The prospective son-in-law should be astonished by his influence in the village. Somehow the wretched bull had to fetch a price. He resolved to sell it at the next market even if he had to lower the figure.

"I shall tell you the final word. You look like a decent young fellow. I raised this bull with great care and paid a great price for it. One fixed price, no bargaining: a hundred and fifty."

"Good heavens! You are quoting the price of an elephant calf. Cattle prices have collapsed, while you speak from last year's memory. Eighty rupees. Say yes if you agree. Otherwise look there: that reddish bull is ready for sixty-five."

"There are oxen for twenty-five too. Are all oxen equal?"

"You want every coin in my waistcloth. Is that fair? I came all this way to market; must I not buy a little fruit or a snack? Here, ninety-five."

"What a stubborn bargainer! Very well, give it."

"My home village is Sornapuram."

The bull changed hands. Chinnappan went to Arunachalam's shop, paid the debt, and returned home with the remaining ten rupees.

The village elders said, "The bull was costing too much, so Chinnappan sold it at a good price."

The people from Eechampadi did not come to see the girl at the temple festival as promised. "He has just bought a new plough bull and is burdened with work. You come once; we shall go ourselves and see the boy," said the uncle.

In the hall of the Eechampadi house, Chinnappan spoke with the old man who might become his relation by marriage. The uncle sat beside him exchanging courtesies.

"By God's grace we own a little land and some cattle. Only four days ago we bought a quality bull for two hundred rupees," said the Eechampadi elder.

"Our Chinnappan too is a fine farmer. Only one son..."

"Any debts?"

"Not a quarter-anna! He owed the usurious Arunachalam a little money. Arunachalam spoke one slighting word, and in anger Chinnappan sold his golden bull last week for three hundred rupees, flung the thirty rupees he owed in the man's face, and set aside the rest for his daughter's wedding gifts."

Suddenly a cry came from outside: "There, there! Catch it! Do not let it go!"

Chinnappan went out. A man was chasing a bull. On seeing Chinnappan, the bull bounded up and stopped beside him.

It was the very bull Chinnappan had sold at market. Behind it stood the Eechampadi bridegroom.

"This is the boy," said the Eechampadi elder, pointing to his son.

"This is my bull," said Chinnappan, pointing to the animal.

"You said three hundred," the elder laughed.

"When you said you paid two hundred, what is wrong if I add another hundred?" replied the uncle.

Everyone understood and laughed.

"What we said about the bull may be false," Chinnappan said, "but everything I said about my daughter Sengarumbu is true. You need only see her to know."

"The same with me," said the Eechampadi elder. "What I said about my son is true, unlike what I said about the bull."

"You need not tell me. I saw him at the market itself. Good heavens, what a relentless bargainer!" said Chinnappan.

"Our Singaram suits Sengarumbu in every way," said the uncle.

The marriage was settled.

It was Arunachalam who carried the news to Kattiri. He did not tell him expecting any disaster.

Kattiri became furious. He imagined Chinnappan had found another match for Sengarumbu only after learning of his loss on the sugarcane. In fact, Chinnappan had never promised to give Sengarumbu to Kattiri's family.

Kattiri's anger fell not only upon Chinnappan but upon Singaram and even Singaram's bull.

"Singaram struts about in arrogance, proud that he is as fat as a bull. I shall show him a thing or two," he fumed.

How could Sengarumbu know of this hostility growing around the fields and threshing floor because of her? The shapely girl went on weeding, plucking flowers, protecting chicks, gathering mangoes from the tree, and running and playing with the goat kids.

"Singaram is no ordinary fellow, girl. If you sit combing and decorating yourself, he will not merely look and grin. Work must be done properly. He is very strict about work," Chinnappan would say.

Kattiri often rebuked his own son for failing to labour earnestly and advance like other men. This made the son angry with Singaram too. Young men in the village further inflamed him by repeatedly asking, "We hear Singaram is going to marry the girl chosen for you?" In anger he lost his reason and patience and quarrelled with everyone.

Kattiri's anger swelled like a flood.

A sickle slashed the leg of Singaram's bull. "Let that sinner's son suffer," Kattiri said happily. Those who knew of his anger toward Singaram teased him: "Elder brother, you struck it, but failed to sever the leg. The doctor says it will knit together."

"Get away, you rascals. Did you see me cut that bull's leg?" Kattiri would ask, but without becoming angry. In truth, he had not attacked the bull. Yet the villagers' belief and talk made it seem a heroic feat to him, and he took pleasure in it.

The sickle that began with the bull next travelled toward Kattiri's neck. Fortunately it struck his shoulder rather than cutting his throat. Singaram was sent inside for three years.

It was while the case was underway that Sengarumbu saw Singaram. Both hearts trembled. Thinking that so much disaster had occurred on her account, Sengarumbu was troubled. Yet she decided that henceforth she could marry only Singaram and would become no other man's wife.

Chinnappan did not wish to give his daughter to a man who had been in prison, but he could not break her determination.

"Mad girl," he would say.

"You yourself kept praising Singaram's good qualities and spoiled her heart," his wife complained.

In prison Singaram heard that Sengarumbu held sincere affection for him, and his heart swelled. To the prisoners who became close to him he happily declared that he would marry as soon as he went out and that the bride was already settled.

Three years rolled away. Singaram was released. Rocked by thoughts of Sengarumbu, he fell asleep on the train carrying him home. Sweet dreams came. She stood beside a haystack while he watched from the edge of the field.

"Girl! Sengarumbu! A goat is coming toward the field. Will you not drive it away?" he called, and her mischievous smile delighted him. Now she brought him gruel and handed it to him with such tenderness. Many such dreams followed.

The train stopped amid a great commotion. Flood. A breach. The railway line had split, they said, and the train could go no farther.

Singaram spent the whole night aboard in anguish. Day came, and people related terrible news. The sea had risen, a demonic wind had blown, a breach had destroyed villages, and people had died by the lakh.

"Was Thopputhurai flooded too?" Singaram asked.

"They say Thopputhurai has been erased so completely that no one can tell where it stood," a man replied.

"Ayyo!" Singaram screamed.

That peacock-like woman lived at Thopputhurai. What had become of her?

A few who recognized the signs of approaching flood had gone to Chinnappan.

"This demonic wind will not leave us alone, Chinnappa. My heart pounds. I feel we should leave now and go somewhere. What do you say?" an elder asked.

Chinnappan had witnessed an earlier storm. "Uncle, do not die of fear. This wind blows only for the time it takes to chew a betel nut. Just watch; it will pass in a little while," he replied.

Courage was not the only reason he refused to leave. Kattiri had come there that day for some special purpose. The sight of him filled Chinnappan with anger. He wanted to break the arm or leg of the man who had brought him so much harm and teach him sense. He waited for an opportunity.

"My hunger will not cease until I destroy the town and drink life," the cyclone roared. As though nature foresaw the great disaster and wailed aloud, torrential rain fell. Its fury increased with every second. A dreadful cry rising from the sea pierced the ears.

Though the villagers possessed hearts seasoned by suffering and hardship, terror entered them more deeply than ever before.

Pitch darkness everywhere. Huts were torn away. Great trees tried to resist the demonic wind, failed, and fell uprooted. As though saying, "I shall not leave you even now," the gale lifted and hurled the trees. Cries and wails rose everywhere.

"Ayyo! Mother! The flood has entered!"

Destruction raged. People were trapped in the water.

"Father! Mother! Grandfather! Sister! Alamelu! Andiyappa! Ayyo, God!"

Thopputhurai became a wilderness of floodwater. Chinnappan's family was trapped. At first he had thought of driving the cattle to some higher scrubland. People ran shouting that water had reached the end of the street where his hut stood.

"Run, Sengarumbu!" he cried.

The water climbed from knee to waist with each passing second. Children drowned. Old people unable to swim were dragged away. Sengarumbu struggled as much as she could, then sagged with a cry of "Father!"

Chinnappan carried her and swam without knowing any direction. His strength began to fail, and Sengarumbu could scarcely breathe. Kattiri too was caught in the flood and struggling desperately. When his eyes fell upon the exhausted Sengarumbu and Chinnappan racing toward his last moment, he cried, "Who is that? Chinnappan?" and swam toward them with all the strength he possessed.

"Here, take her. From now on Sengarumbu is your daughter," Chinnappan said, placing her in Kattiri's hands. Then he became a corpse. The great flood hurried on to make corpses of many more.

No one knew how Kattiri swam or where he found such strength and courage. At last he saved Sengarumbu and brought her to shore.

"Has Father gone? Ayyo, Father!" Sengarumbu wailed.

Kattiri offered no consolation. "What has become of my son?" he wondered in distress.

"I could not save Mother."

Such cries came from many people.

Searching for a place that had not become a flood wilderness, Kattiri and Sengarumbu staggered onward. Villagers from Kattiri's home came from another direction crying in anguish. Their village, they said, had been utterly destroyed.

"He could save only the daughter, poor man," someone said on seeing Kattiri and Sengarumbu.

Singaram spent three days in agony at Thiruthuraipoondi. When he heard the water had opened a path, he ran toward Thopputhurai. On a rise beside the road he saw Sengarumbu, transformed almost beyond recognition.

"Sengarumbu, you have poured milk into my stomach!"

"Aththan, have you come?"

"He is gone. He carried me and swam a great distance through the flood. In the end he was gone. I should have gone too. At the final moment this man saved me."

Singaram turned to fall at the man's feet. Kattiri! He did not know what to do.

"It is I, Singaram. It is I. I am the very sinner who did you harm because I wanted Sengarumbu as my daughter-in-law and sent you to prison for three years. But I have now found atonement for the harm I did you. I prevented the flood from taking Sengarumbu and have handed her back to you. That flood washed away every evil thought and rotten impulse within me. With these eyes I saw the suffering endured by all the people, and my heart suffered beyond endurance. When I saw this girl struggling for life, my heart would not permit me to abandon her. A good thought arose in me: no wish to do further harm must remain. I too have become a human being. Only Chinnappan could not be saved. This flood destroyed our village and swallowed my son."

"Do not weep," Singaram said. "Do not weep. When so vast a destruction has occurred, take pride that you could perform so great a service. Sengarumbu was meant to become your daughter-in-law. The flood swallowed your own son. Accept me as your son. Sengarumbu will become your daughter-in-law, and you shall be her father."

Kattiri embraced Singaram.

"The sky has poured out all the destruction it had to pour. Look, there is sunlight now," Singaram said, pointing with a smile.

## Translator's Notes

The canonical source combines two OCR parts into one story, and the translation follows that merged order. `செங்கரும்பு` is both the heroine's name and literally "red sugarcane"; the Tamil name is retained in the title and body with a gloss in the English title. Rural dialogue is rendered conversationally while preserving bargaining, debt, caste-neutral kinship forms, and agricultural vocabulary. The final flood refers to the destructive coastal cyclone and inundation described by the source; historical details are translated as presented rather than independently asserted. Severe OCR damage, especially in the flood and reconciliation pages, is documented separately.

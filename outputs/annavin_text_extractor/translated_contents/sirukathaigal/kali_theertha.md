## English Translation

"Who is that? Somu? Our Somu? What has happened to you, man? I can hardly recognize you. You have changed completely!"

"Yes, sir. What can I do? My health has been poor ever since rationing began. With nourishment reduced, an old stomach ailment grew rapidly worse and ruined my body. When the body grows thin, one's appearance must change too."

"What a pity! Even your face has darkened."

"It has always been like that, sir. When was I ever fair?"

"Even if you were not fair, you had a good brown complexion."

"Worry is the cause of everything."

"Yes. In any case, take some good medicine, Somu. It is painful merely to look at you. How strong you used to be, like a bodybuilder!"

"Did they not even tease me by calling me Fat Somu? What can I do, sir? Just as the times change, a person too must change. Is it possible to remain unchanged?"

"Even so, Somu, your body has wasted to a splinter. When I first saw you, I tell you truly, I could not recognize you at all. You looked like some stranger."

"Yes, sir. Yesterday our Semmalaiyar said exactly the same thing."

"Have you seen Semmalaiyan? Did you see what he has become? He now looks as huge as Annamalai itself."

"Yes, sir. His body has grown somewhat heavier."

"Somewhat? The man has swollen enormously. All that comes from contentment, Somu. Happiness keeps piling upon happiness for him. Business has brought him a fine opportunity. His daughter has married into a great family. His son has become an M.L.A. He once had a younger brother who wandered about as a good-for-nothing. Even that man now earns thousands upon thousands through military contracts."

"I heard about it, sir. May I go now?"

"Go, man. Look after your health."

After tenderly questioning the transformed Somu and affectionately advising him to take suitable medicine and restore his health, Kandasamy goes home.

His house is large, but guests cannot enter freely. His lady is such a goddess of liberty! That is another matter. Let us follow the path taken by poor Somu for a little distance. We have work to do.

Somu walks unsteadily, never raising his bowed head. A tall man standing near the betel-and-areca shop at the street corner glares at Somu, clears his throat, and spits. Let us ask him about Somu.

Wait. Let Somu go a little farther. He has gone. Now we can speak to the tall man.

"Brother, give me a matchbox."

"I have none, man. One must wander in all eight directions searching for matches. Where can they be found? The times are ruined. Once a miserable matchbox cost a quarter anna; now it cannot be had even for one anna."

"Yes. Nothing can be obtained. Worry about food has become the greatest worry now. See that man walking there? He told me that he once had the build of a strongman. Ration rice did not agree with him, poor fellow, and he has wasted to a splinter."

"His name is Somu. Did he say food was the cause?"

"Yes."

"Of course he would say that."

"Brother, there is nothing wrong in what he said. The man is not as he was before. He has certainly grown thin."

"But rationing is not the cause, man. His own deeds are consuming him. The fellow is a hardened scoundrel. Cheating the town of its property is his occupation. Will a curse leave him alone? It is killing him."

Why should we prolong the argument when the tall man speaks with such anger? Let us go. Our work is finished anyway.

According to this man, Somu's bad character caused him to waste away. The other gentleman said that a good man had grown thin. Both agree only that his appearance has changed; they differ over the reason.

This man bears some anger towards Somu. The other has a habit of speaking affectionately. What is the real cause?

Why ask? Let us go and see Somu's house. If we walk a little faster, we can catch him. He is staggering along, after all. See? What I said has proved right. There he goes. We can follow him. An ordinary pace is enough.

Good heavens! Is this his house? Shame, it is wretched. The wall is collapsing. The entrance is like an open drain. Monkeys dance upon the roof.

There is crying inside. Are those children? Why are they screaming like this? What a pity! Why does that woman beat and torment the children so? That must be why they cry.

Women who abuse children must not be left unchallenged, sir. We must ask her, "Are you even human?" Yes, Somu is opening his mouth. He is a good man, after all. How could he fail to question such injustice?

"Why struggle so much, Muniamma? Let those little Saturns go to whatever ruin awaits them. They are only another hardship added to all the hardship you already bear. Leave them, leave them. Let each run wild and perish."

What injustice, sir! Somu supports his wife while she beats the children. "Little Saturns! Let them go! Let them perish!" Must he join her in cursing the children this way? What kind of heart does he have?

Let us not watch this horror. Come, we can go outside. The house reeks too. Flies, ants, beetles, and mould everywhere. Is this a home? Even a cattle shed would be better.

Sir, look, that woman is coming with a water pot. Yes, that same rough-tempered woman. Let us ask why she beats the children. We can at least ask. She will not swallow us. We are not children.

"Madam, why do you beat the children like that? They are screaming."

"Let them scream like wild beasts. Why, sir, what can I do except beat them? The mischief they have made since morning has not stopped."

"What are they doing?"

"What can I tell you? Poor children desire everything they see.

"This morning some sinner stood right opposite the house selling blowing pipes. He gathered a crowd. Children bought them. Those with money bought them. Ever since then, these creatures have been taking my life."

"You should buy one and settle it. They are children, after all."

"Would one be enough? There are half a dozen of them."

"You must reason with children, madam. What do they understand?"

"Children alone? What do even grown people understand? What do you know? If you knew the condition of our family, would you ask why I did not buy a blowing pipe?

"There is no food to fill the stomach, and you tell me to buy pipes for those tailless monkeys. Why should I speak to you? Will the water pot fill itself through talk? I am going."

We escaped, sir! Did you hear the way that woman rattled on? She torments even us like this. Would she spare the children? Wicked woman!

Look, this elderly gentleman calls even that woman good. We can hear what he is saying.

"What can be done, madam? These times are like this. Good people suffer in every way. Should poverty gnaw at someone with your golden character? Those who once wandered bareheaded now wear turbans, and even wastrels prosper. Your husband has changed beyond recognition, and hardship has come to you too. Troubles rise like mountains. What can be done?"

Alas, sir! Did you hear that elderly man? Apparently she is a very good woman. These are hard times, and poverty is to blame. That is why her husband Somu has wasted away and changed, and why this golden-natured woman has changed in temperament too.

See how many changes poverty produces. What can be done? Come, sir, let us go. We must walk quickly. It is nearly twelve.

When did they create a park on this street, sir? It looks splendid. There is even a clock. Oh, they have installed a radio too. See how beautiful this side has become since the park was made.

Do you know what this place was like before? Donkeys gathered here and brayed without end. When rain came, it became a pond, and buffaloes rolled about in place of the donkeys.

Such a place has changed completely. It has become a flower garden. Only by improving and beautifying the town this way can the municipality earn a good name, sir.

The former chairman never did anything like this. This man is not bad; he does good work.

Why are you laughing, sir? What is it? You laugh without saying anything. You are laughing after thinking of something. Can you not tell me?

What? You say there is a chairman to beautify the town, but who is there to improve the lives of the poor?

That is true. Yes, sir, what you say is just. They beautify the town and raise temple towers higher and higher, but the lives of poor people like Somu fall apart, and no one can be found to repair them.

Look, you can see the temple tower over there to the south. Apparently your great-great-grandfather built it. Did you know? What was his name, sir?

Not your grandfather's name. Thanigasalam Pillai is already famous. I mean the name of the deity in that temple. I have forgotten it. What do they call him? They say it is a very powerful shrine.

Yes, yes: Kali-Theertha Perumal, the Lord Who Ended the Affliction of the Age!

*Dravida Nadu*, 19 January 1947.

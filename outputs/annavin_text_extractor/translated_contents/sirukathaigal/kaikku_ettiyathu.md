## English Translation

“I told you that wretched cat would enter, but would you listen? Look at the commotion it is creating now,” Maragatham muttered as she woke the husband beside her.

Only the pillow danced beneath her hand.

A black cat ran away sorrowfully: the mouse had come within reach of its paw but not its mouth.

“Oh, so that is what happened?”

Her husband had put down the pillow and disappeared somewhere.

“His character worsens every day,” Maragatham said as she sat anxiously.

What did she know of the excitement Kandasamy felt at that very moment?

Kandasamy had nursed a desire for a long time. His friend Gopalan had stirred it. Every day when he met Gopalan, the man spoke of some new amorous “sport.”

“Ah! What splendour. What pleasure!”

“Tremendous pleasure? What did you see?”

“I saw, I saw, I saw. I saw a maiden capable of enchanting Kama himself. Her eyes...”

“Stop singing. You must have seen some celestial Rambha.”

“Kanda, you do not understand. I tell you the truth. I have seen Komalam many times, but only yesterday did I taste the juice of her kovvai-fruit lips, the expressions of her face, the flavour of her speech, and the wonder of her play.”

“Tell me what happened. Do not recite the tale of Madanakamarajan.”

“Then listen.”

Every day Gopalan described a different adventure. Temptation struck Kandasamy.

What could he do? He had no money and no occupation except idle talk.

He looked after the household management of retired Parasurama Mudaliar, who worried that the boy had become useless. Kandasamy received food at each mealtime. When he returned from outside, he chanted that he had looked for work. After that there was always the ceremonial abuse from his wife.

Even in this condition, Kandasamy thought of experiencing at least one of the pleasures Gopalan described, if only for one day.

Within his mind he conducted a devotional song: “For one day, for at least one moment, I must see you, O doe.”

On the same night that the mouse came within the cat’s paw but not its mouth, Maragatham’s necklace came within Kandasamy’s hand.

He took it and left.

You may cry, “What betrayal!” What could be done? His obsession had become that severe.

As he walked towards the many women Gopalan had mentioned, thoughts gnawed at him. How could he approach at nine at night? How should he speak? What would she think if he handed over a necklace?

But not one thought arose about how Maragatham’s heart would tremble, how her father would grieve, or how the townspeople would abuse them if they learned of it.

There was only one flame in his mind. Madness to fulfil his desire had risen to his head.

Was he alone? In this cruel world, do those seeking pleasure ever remember that other people possess something called a heart?

Are there not wealthy men in this world who earn interest upon the gold strung upon a marriage cord?

Are there not gentlemen who deny a fair wage to a labourer pulling a handcart in the burning sun until legs, neck, and waist break, then preach dharma:

“Ask only what your work deserves. Do not desire excess. You will receive only the amount God has ordained.”

They give five annas where six are due and rejoice that the saved anna is profit earned by their cleverness.

There had been a time when Kandasamy was enchanted by Maragatham’s smile, when she suited his desire, and when he wished to act without hurting her feelings.

Then fascination with “sport” arose. To fulfil the desire in his mind, he dared steal his own beloved’s gold chain.

People say lust blinds a man to earth and sky. It is not lust alone. When any thought seizes anyone like frenzy, everything else disappears from sight.

Kandasamy grew tired of repeatedly knocking upon Gopalan’s door. Where had he gone? In which adventure was he engaged? Who knew?

The door remained shut, and a harsh answer came from inside: “He has gone out. No one knows when he will return.”

Kandasamy walked while thinking.

“What came within reach may not reach the mouth. What shall I do? Where shall I go?” he wondered until exhausted.

“With a face as beautiful as yours, would any man pay even three quarters of an anna? Look at that snout.”

“Ah, my monkey of Manmatha. In your wretched condition, do you mock me too? Is your intoxication high?”

“Enough. Go lie down. No one will be caught tonight.”

“Who would come while you stand beside me like a thick log? Every passer-by sees you and moves on. Go and lie down somewhere. I owe the landlady nine rupees in rent. She says whatever comes to her mouth. My honour is going.”

“Your honour? You would ask how much a measure of honour costs, yet now you say it is lost. Splendid. You are selling needles in a blacksmith’s workshop.”

“Throw your learned speech into the rubbish. Someone seems to be coming. Look and tell me.”

This conversation took place between Vedam, who had sweated from waiting night after night, and Velan, who had grown wild from drink after drink.

Except for one or two houses, the street was closed.

Others were upon their beds at that hour: caressing a desired lover, quarrelling with a husband over his flirtation with the neighbour, gambling while declaring “two steps make one hundred,” or complaining, “I asked for one six-sovereign bangle, and there is no means for that, though desire itself drips from you.”

Only a few like Vedam stood in the street according to their fate and sought a means of living.

There had been a time when Vedam too lived differently, when men like Velan did not approach her, when no wrinkles marked her face, and youth and beauty danced.

Now Vedam had aged. She was thirty, but without powder upon her face, people would estimate forty. The aged appearance came because she had offered her youth with extreme generosity as a feast to the lust-blinded world.

Everything that once came had gone, and Vedam now had no means of income. The visitor sought very young women and looked for laughter and lively movement. He called her completely worn out.

Vedam’s life was painful. Yes, were there not thousands of Vedams in that world?

Searching as he wandered, Kandasamy saw Vedam’s face with the assistance of the powder she wore.

Vedam cleared her throat. Velan laughed. Kandasamy stopped.

“The man looks new, Vela. Go and bring him,” said Vedam.

Velan went.

“You look as though I have seen you somewhere.”

“Me? No.”

“I remember clearly. You have visited Ethiraja’s house opposite.”

“No. I do not have this kind of habit. Something came over me only today.”

“That is only natural. They tell a story that even Viswamitra was enchanted upon seeing Menaka. Such things happen in the world,” said Vedam.

Velan said he must go outside to drink tea.

“Elder brother must go out. Let him disappear, and we can remain in peace,” Vedam said with a laugh.

Kandasamy laughed too.

When Vedam laughed, he noticed that one or two of her teeth were missing. His mind changed slightly.

“Vedam! I need tea. Are you deaf?” shouted Velan.

“Wait a little, brother. Why shout before he can take out the money?” she told Velan.

Then she asked Kandasamy: “If you have some change, give it to me so I can get rid of this affliction.”

“I have no change,” said Kandan.

“Do you have a rupee?”

“Do you have any money at all?”

“No.”

“Are you playing with me? Take out the money. I must give some to brother and spend something on us. I heated milk and spilled it.”

“Look, this alone is here,” Kandasamy said, showing her the chain.

“A chain? Where did this come from?”

“Why? It is mine.”

“Yours? Why bring it here?”

“I have no money. Keep this in place of money. I intend to stay here for several days. Is it not enough for the expenses?”

“Tell the truth. From where did you steal it?”

“Steal?”

“Whose property did you steal? Tell me the truth.”

“I swear it belongs to our household.”

“Your mother’s?”

“No.”

“Your wife’s?”

“Yes.”

“Wait a little. I shall return.”

Vedam went to Velan.

“He has stolen the gold chain from his home and brought it here. Poor fellow, he too is young. His wife must be a very young woman. She will suffer thinking of her thieving husband.

“I already conduct this dishonourable livelihood because my own heart suffered and I committed many sins. If this too continues, I shall gather the burning curses of foolish men’s families and never obtain a good fate.

“Therefore I am going to send him home,” she said.

From her speech, Velan understood that she would not agree whatever he said.

Her appeal to humanity did not soften him. He had grown capable of saying, “What does it matter to us if another family is ruined?”

Velan was the son of a man who borrowed four thousand from a Marwari and, after principal and court costs, surrendered house, cow, calf, and everything else. The father lived in a charitable rest house and worked at a public shelter.

Unable to find any means of living in the world, Velan became a rowdy.

Once his heart had possessed pity, compassion, and mercy. The cruelties he endured in the world removed them one by one. Anger, hatred, loathing, and rage alone settled there.

He was prepared not merely to take Kandasamy’s gold chain but even to twist off and pawn his neck, if that were possible.

Yet Vedam’s words caused him fear. If the fellow’s father gave a case to the police, it could become dangerous. Only then did he agree to send Kandan away as Vedam wished.

“What can be done, Vela? It came within reach but not the mouth,” Vedam said.

She combed her hair and corrected the powder that had run, preparing to stand again at the street entrance.

Inside the room, Kandasamy waited with frightened eyes.

“Hey, father! Come outside,” Velan called.

“Whom? Me?”

“Yes, yes, you, sir. Master Gold Chain,” said Velan, mockery and anger blended in his voice.

Kandasamy became afraid. His tongue would not speak. He came outside.

“Go home respectfully and return the chain. You steal household property without knowing head from foot. If people without food accept it, the police pounce upon them. That is what happens.

“You stand like a stout man but possess no sense. You must have money in your hand before coming to a place like this. Do you come after stealing jewellery? Go, go. You look otherwise respectable. Go.”

Velan drove him away.

Shame and grief closed around Kandan’s heart. His legs stumbling, he left the house.

Every word Velan spoke pierced his heart. He grieved that he lacked even the wisdom of the supervisor at a sex worker’s house. He praised how good Vedam was.

He had escaped a great danger. Therefore, he thought, he must visit a temple, worship, and only then return home.

He believed the Lord himself had restrained and claimed him when he was about to slip and fall. As devotion overflowed, tears filled his eyes.

“Ah, this blessed fortune came through a sex worker,” he murmured while staggering towards the sacred temple.

It did not occur to the man who became a devotee at midnight that temples are locked at improper hours. People praise the Lord as protector of orphans, but because ornaments are stored inside, they keep the temple locked.

Kandasamy yearned somehow to behold the Lord.

At that same time, temple priest Krishnayyengar had dressed and adorned himself for a visit to Andal. Carrying flower garlands devoutly and happily, he locked the shrine of Mother Perundevi and prepared to leave.

The priest’s income from Perundevi’s shrine that day had been totalled at three rupees and eight annas.

After locking the temple, Krishnayyengar gathered the flower garlands that had lain upon the idol and set out for the house of Andal, his favoured woman.

At midnight, the man made devotional by his strange experience intercepted him.

“Swami, I must behold Perundevi. Can the temple be opened?” he asked.

The priest looked at him and wondered whether a man arriving with such urgency at this hour might be another Thirumangai Alvar.

“A vision at an improper hour? Come after dawn. I have locked the temple and must not open it,” said the priest.

“Swami, I ran here with intense desire. A great danger came like a mountain and vanished like mist. I was about to commit a grave sin but escaped through the Lord’s grace.

“I have vowed that I shall immediately worship and make an offering to the Lord who brought me ashore from irredeemable hell, and only then return home,” he said.

The priest thought: “The fellow seems completely mad. A good sum can be extracted.”

He said:

“Even in this age of Kali, does a day dawn without devotees? Sir, you are young and yet so devout.

“Today the temple workers went home early. I waited a long time for worshippers and fell asleep. I felt deeply troubled that sleep came to this sinner while serving the goddess’s shrine.

“Only now do I understand that it was the Lord’s design. There was a prior arrangement that I must sleep, leave the temple later than usual, and that you must arrive then. Could it fail to occur?

“Very well, come. Let us go inside.”

The priest opened the door, lit a small lamp, used it to light the large lamp, and went to the sanctum, the devotee following him.

He saw the condition of Mother Perundevi and screamed. The devotee too cried out in alarm.

A thief had stolen the marriage pendant and necklace tied around the idol’s neck, the ear ornament, and the bangles.

At the very moment the devotee and priest entered, the thief was removing the sari draped upon the statue.

When they screamed, he stopped stripping the remaining clothing and ornaments, picked up a vessel lying nearby, and struck the priest upon the head. He knocked the devotee down, took the gold chain that fell from his waistcloth, and ran.

The two continued to shout, but neither acquired the courage to catch the thief because he displayed a sharp knife.

Doors of houses near the temple opened.

“What shouting is that? What commotion is heard from the temple?” voices arose.

Warriors carrying lamps entered. The priest, with blood running from his head, and the devotee stood before them.

Someone had entered the temple and robbed it. That itself was no surprise, for theft occurred in many temples.

But what business did the priest and this man have inside the temple at midnight? Who could believe the man’s claim that he went to worship?

Not only the villagers but the court asked these questions.

The temple trustee filed a case alleging that the priest and the devotee had jointly stolen the temple property, then shouted and invented a thief upon whom to place blame.

District Judge Amirthalingeswara Iyer, a man who fasted for Ambika, sentenced the priest and midnight devotee to six months’ rigorous imprisonment and a fine of one thousand rupees.

That gold chain slept together with Perundevi’s ornaments inside the strongbox of Seth Karamchand in the next town.

Pakka Balaram, one among the thief’s many names, happily caressed the woman who had delivered Kandasamy’s moral instruction.

“I am not a woman greedy for money,” she told him. “Listen to this. One night, a fellow stole a chain from his house and came here. He said, ‘Take it and let me stay here.’

“I looked at him and thought: ‘Shame, he stole this without his household knowing. It would be sinful to accept it.’

“So I said, ‘Go home quietly and sleep, younger brother. Do not do such a thing,’ and sent him away.

“Even now elder brother says what came within reach did not reach the mouth.”

Thus she described her virtue.

Balaram collapsed repeatedly with laughter. She did not understand what his laughter meant.

## English Translation

(Velayudham)

"My gardener is supremely meek. He does not possess even a drop of anger. I am rather quick-tempered, as you know. So every day I abuse him however I please: 'Donkey! Dog! I shall tie you up and kick you!'

"He never grows angry. He is that meek. He stands with his head bowed and never speaks a word in opposition.

"One day I was suffering some mental distress. At that very moment he made some mistake. In the anger that arose, I picked up the slipper lying before me and threw it at the fellow. Fortunately, it did not strike him.

"Even then he did not become angry. He picked up the slipper from where it had fallen, returned it to its former place, and went back to his work.

"I have never seen anyone as meek as my gardener, a man of such rare character. Supremely meek."

"They say Mother Earth is patient, but no one upon this earth can be as meek as our One-Eyed Woman.

"The torment her husband inflicts upon her, good heavens, even a god could not endure it. If she stands, he kicks her. If she bends, he punches her. Without any reason he begins quarrels, speaks to her as one would speak to a dog, and picks up whatever lies at hand to throw and destroy.

"'Why is there no vegetable in the curry? Why is there no flavour in the vegetable? Why is there no money upon the shelf? Why is the child not asleep? If you spoke, why did you not stand still?' Every second he strikes her with another contemptuous 'woman!'

"The blessed woman endures all these kicks and, poor thing, never opens her mouth to answer with a single word. She is so meek, so patient. I have seen countless people, but never a woman so restrained and submissive.

"Somehow she runs the household, tolerating his crooked conduct. That blessed woman is meek, supremely meek."

"Is she a woman? She is a street brawler! It is enough for her to come to the well. There is an uproar at once. Before one can say a word, she screams and starts a fight.

"She quarrels even without a cause. Good heavens, do you know the things she says? She pours out curses and utters words unfit for the mouth. Does she stop with speech? She strikes people with her water pot, pulls their hair, and sometimes comes to beat them.

"An uncontrollable shrew! A quarrelsome troublemaker! A woman swollen with insolent speech.

"If I learn that she is coming near the well, I do not go. I remain at home thinking, 'Shame! Let that loud-mouthed, lawless donkey go away.'

"Dear woman, I too have seen so many women, but never a street brawler like her. Accursed creature! Apparently she is leaving for her husband's house tomorrow. Let her go away. Our village will prosper. From now on we can visit the well freely."

The gardener praised by his master as supremely meek is the very husband who kicks his wife when she stands and punches her when she bends.

The One-Eyed Woman who tolerates that husband's crooked conduct and remains meeker than Mother Earth is the loud-mouthed street brawler who starts fights at the well.

Why does the supremely meek man become a cruel husband? Why does the submissive wife become an uncontrollable shrew?

The cruelties he suffers at his master's hands produce anger in that supremely meek man's mind. Before his master, it remains subdued. At home, before a helpless woman, it emerges. The supremely meek man becomes a cruel man with rage filling his head.

The anger that rises in his wife while she endures his cruelty and remains supremely meek and patient lies suppressed at that moment like a snake within its anthill. At the well near her mother's house, before other women, the snake raises its hood and dances. There the supremely meek woman becomes a street brawler.

Meek at the workplace, angry at home. Patient before her husband, then a brawler before other women.

Near Hogenakkal in Salem, the Kaveri can be crossed in a goat's leap; in another district it later becomes the broad Kaveri. In the same way, difference of place is one among many causes that produce differences in character.

In one place a person cannot display anger. That does not mean no anger arose. It later bursts forth elsewhere.

The gardener is compelled to remain supremely meek because the place demands it.

Before her husband, the One-Eyed Woman is compelled to remain patient.

At home he can display his anger without restraint. Thus the anger bound at his workplace breaks its bonds and turns the supremely meek man into a wild beast. Her condition is the same at her mother's house, near the well, in a place where her husband is absent.

Are they alone like this? If we recall the many events we have witnessed, we will know. At times we remain supremely meek. At other times anger boils over. Sometimes a change of place transforms character.

Those who understand the subtleties of the mind say that any emotion, not anger alone, suppressed at one time because of place will emerge at another time and another place with many times the speed and intensity.

Does a tigress fail to nurse her cubs lovingly? What is that same tigress like when she catches and kills a deer and drinks its blood? People too are like this.

Therefore, those who understand human character say that general declarations such as "Parasuraman is meek, Pandurangam is angry, Parvathi is lawless, and Parimala is cheerful" do not become the whole truth.

## A New Incident

"You thick-headed donkey! What is this? Has some tremendous anger come over you?"

"Would I not become angry, sir? Am I cattle or a human being? However hard I work and however faithfully I behave, does even a drop of compassion arise in you? If you speak to me as though speaking to a dog, I too am human. How can anger fail to come?"

"You will understand when I kick you, rascal."

"Please be quiet. I came to work for you, not to receive kicks. It is improper for one human to beat and kick another. There is no necessity for me to endure it. Even a cow will try to gore someone who beats it. Is it right to say a human must remain still?"

"Hey! Are you the one speaking this way? Who taught you all this? You used to be so restrained and meek that you never spoke a word in opposition. Now, before I have uttered a word, anger rises to your head and you crow at me. What happened?"

What happened was this.

"This is all there is."

"What, donkey? When I ask, you shout, 'This is all there is.' Do you not know that I dislike okra, donkey?"

"Enough! Stop herding donkeys."

"You dog! How dare you answer me?"

"If I tell the truth, is that called answering back? You speak however you please without cause. Am I stone? Is this to be my condition throughout life? Is your heart made of iron?"

"Ah! Have you found the courage to preach, donkey?"

"Again and again you say it."

"What if I do? If you answer, you will receive a kick."

"Try kicking me and see."

He rose and kicked. But he was the one who fell. The One-Eyed Woman caught his leg firmly and twisted it aside.

That was what happened. It made him think. He pondered for a long time. Many new kinds of thought arose. He became a new man.

Velayudham knew neither that incident nor its subtle meaning. He was astonished that the supremely meek gardener spoke in opposition this way.

"Enough! Shut your mouth. Do you think you alone know how to speak?"

"Speak, woman, speak. Dance like a possessed woman."

"Enough, you donkey, you widow-headed fool!"

"Who are you calling a donkey?"

"You, you dog!"

Turmoil erupted at the well.

The One-Eyed Woman's absolute dominion was broken, broken by Chelli's resistance. More than that, new thoughts arose in the One-Eyed Woman herself.

Their result was that she spoke back to her husband. Their consequence was that her husband saw his autocracy collapse. Their continuation was Velayudham's realization that, however much he rebuked the gardener as before, the man would no longer remain supremely meek.

Velayudham too began to think. Like the gardener and the One-Eyed Woman, he changed both his method and his character. His quick temper disappeared.

There he is speaking. Listen.

"Why call him with 'hey' and 'you there'? Duraisamy, have you finished watering the whole garden? Then go home and return later, man."

"There is too little salt in the curry, Kuppi."

"Yes, I did not notice."

"Add a little."

"It is in the upper pot. See? Take it and add it yourself. I am feeding the baby."

"Yes, stay there. I shall take it myself."

The character has changed.

When excessive water enters a river, do we not divert it into a canal and reduce the force of the flood? Inner emotion is the same. If it is continually suppressed, it will leap and graze elsewhere. If it is brought under control, directed with attention to its course, reduced before it boils, and regulated, collisions and attacks will diminish.

We do not notice this while it occurs. After this chemistry succeeds and conditions change, however, we pronounce one person good and another bad without understanding the difficulty.

Character does not submit to such a general rule. It is shaped, cultivated, changed, and given new forms by place, time, method, events, training, and relationships with others. It develops every second. Its nature cannot be declared with finality, and should not be.

We see events such as the Maharaja of Kashmir honouring Sheikh Abdullah and Minister Bhaktavatsalam giving a banquet to Shanmugham after he relinquished his "Sir." Are these not intriguing events suited to examining whether their respective characters have changed?

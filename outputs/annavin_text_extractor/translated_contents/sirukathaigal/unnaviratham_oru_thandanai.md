## English Translation

“Will you not get up? Is it not time to eat? You sit chattering without end. This is what happens whenever that long-faced fellow from next door comes. He has no concern for his own home, and are you becoming the same?”

Thus did his wife speak sharply yesterday, serving what amounted to a summons for dinner. The orthodox Sanatana Sarma concealed his anger beneath sadness.

“I do not seem hungry. You go eat and sleep. Do not torment me,” he said.

His wife’s anger diminished and a little tenderness arose for her husband.

“Eat at least a wood-apple-sized ball of rice and go to sleep. If you lie down at night on an empty stomach, it will churn,” she pleaded, mixing a little affection with her coaxing.

It was true. What would he do if this accursed hunger came at midnight? When hunger arrives, all ten virtues fly away. But he could not break the promise he had given the Iyer next door; if he did, there would be a commotion at the temple tank. Thunder threatened from both sides.

Sanatana Sarma thought for a moment: what food could they possibly have prepared at home? As though reading his thought, his wife began to list it.

“The onion sambar is excellent. I fried appalams. There are dried chillies too, and I made a splendid coriander chutney.”

Sarma imagined mixing the coriander chutney with rice, pouring on two generous ladles of ghee and eating one handful. How delightful it would be! The realization that he could not do so made his anger blaze.

“Enough, enough, with your sambar and chutney. I will not eat today. I am fasting. Several of us joined together and took a vow today to observe a hunger strike,” he rattled out.

His wife’s laughter troubled him like the burning caused by biting a green chilli.

“A hunger strike? Do you now require a dozen oranges?” she mocked.

“Oranges? I am going to eat nothing. Remember, mine is a lineage prepared for total renunciation,” Sanatana preached.

“When you said hunger strike, I remembered that our Mahatma drank orange juice during his fasts and imagined yours might be the same. Why did this mad idea arise in you? Get up quietly and come. Tomorrow morning while bathing, rub yourself well with two lemons and plunge into the water,” his wife prescribed.

“Enough. Shut your mouth and go! You women show so much arrogance even while depending upon your husbands’ hands. And now you demand rights in property too! Even at the end of the Kali age, the trouble caused by women seized by this affliction does not end,” said Sanatana.

“What are you muttering? I understand nothing,” she asked.

“This government has produced a rare idea. It wants to grant women rights in property. From the earliest days it has been said that the man is born to rule a family. The lineage of Ikshvaku, the Raghu dynasty, the sages beginning with Sanaka: apparently all their systems were wrong!

“Now they plan to enact a law giving property to women. We have resolved to condemn it by fasting. That is why.”

His wife’s laughter turned to fury. She tucked in the end of her sari, cleared her throat and began.

“Women should not inherit property? You are fasting for that? Excellent! From the beginning of time men have made women suffer. Had women possessed pride and honour, they should have made men fast permanently in punishment.

“Go ahead and fast. Let the whole land hear and laugh that bundles of filth like this still exist in the modern age. Beginning with Rama, whom you celebrate as the very incarnation of God, have men not committed injustice towards women? Did Rama not order the pregnant queen of the world to be taken blindfolded and abandoned in the forest?”

She was determined to shake the matter from beginning to end.

Sanatana Sarma grew angry that not only he but Dasaratha’s son himself was being struck.

“Do you condemn Rama’s deed? That Sita lived for ten months in Ravana’s house. If a woman dwells in another man’s home for ten months, will suspicion not arise?” The Iyer became Rama’s advocate.

His wife was unafraid.

“Why was she there? Rama’s own deed brought that fate upon Sita,” she began for the defence. “Ravana grew angry and carried Sita away because Surpanakha’s ears and nose were cut off.”

“Then do you say Rama should have taken Surpanakha’s hand in marriage and lived with her? What scripture do you know? How could Dasaratha’s son accept Surpanakha while he already had a wife?” the Iyer cross-examined.

“Which scripture says a second woman is forbidden while one already exists? Who followed such a rule? Did Dasaratha not have sixty thousand wives? May he be ruined, keeping sixty thousand women! But his son needed only one, and when another approached it seemed just to mutilate her. What splendid justice he performed!”

His wife roared like a lioness.

“It is not you speaking. The demon called Time speaks through you. Misfortune sends its message to the world through your mouth. In the sacred land where Damayanti, Savitri and Nalayini were born, these mushrooms have sprouted,” said the Iyer, abandoning argument for abuse.

Satisfied that she had subdued him, his wife continued:

“Are not those who say property belongs to men but not women fools? Born from a mother’s womb, they betray the entire community of mothers. Dharma itself has decayed in this land. Is being born a woman some sin? Can men live without women? Set living aside: are they not born through women?

“Property for them, comfort for them, and labour for us. While this depraved thinking remains, the country will never prosper. It will only perish.”

She cursed them and left without again inviting her husband to dinner. She mixed coriander chutney into samba rice, poured four ladles of ghee over it and tasted some sambar. Then she mixed sambar with rice and crunched the dried chillies.

To Sanatana Sarma reclining upon the hall swing, even that crunch caused more anguish than the sound of German and Japanese bombs. But what could the poor man do? He had given his word.

In India’s Central Legislative Assembly, efforts were being made to grant women the same property rights as men. The orthodox Ketu-Rahus of this country did not like it. They noisily offered scripture and dharma as excuses, but no one appeared to take their barren Vedic clamour seriously.

They therefore planned to accomplish through strategy what they could not defeat through strength. It was reported that the orthodox worthies of Lucknow had resolved to fast.

When these heroes discovered this method of preventing women from receiving property rights, their wives declared: “The bill granting women property rights must pass. We shall fast for that purpose.” A report in *Swadesamitran* of 15 October 1943 said the wives performed worship in a temple and began their hunger strike.

Sanatana Sarma, introduced in our story, belonged to that same kind. Prompted by a few old almanacs, he too began to fast. But the lady in our story was not as foolish as the wives of the Lucknow orthodox men.

“Fasting is precisely the punishment deserved by men who betray women. Let them endure it; why should we care?” she thought, and ate as usual.

Sanatana Sarma, who had invited trouble for his stomach through his mouth, thought longingly of the sambar’s flavour, the chutney’s fragrance and the crispness of the dried chillies. He tried to sleep, closing his eyes in hope his sorrow would pass. Unable to endure the gnawing stomach, he rolled about.

Instead of lamenting, he cried, “Rama! Descendant of Raghu! Lord of Raghu’s line! Protector of the world!”

Meanwhile his wife came to the street entrance.

“Alamu! Alamu!” she called loudly.

A little girl ran from the opposite house. “Auntie, did you call me?”

“Yes, silly girl. There is coriander chutney. Do you want it?”

“Coriander chutney? It is always very good when you make it. Give me some, auntie,” the child asked happily.

Sarma watched the chutney, dried chillies and sambar travel in separate vessels to the house opposite.

“Lord of all! Protector of the world!” he cried, and went to sit on the street veranda.

The neighbour who had persuaded Sarma to fast and extracted his oath reclined upon an easy chair next door. No cries such as “Lord of all” issued from his sacred mouth. Only gentle snoring emerged.

Sarma slowly rose, staggered next door and shook the man’s shoulder.

“Kuppu Sastri! Why are you snoring so loudly?”

Sastri awoke suddenly.

“It seems sweet sakkarai pongal naturally causes a little drowsiness. I ate two handfuls too many and felt heavy, so I merely closed my eyes for a moment.”

Sarma was stunned.

“What are you saying? You ate sweet pongal? Sinful Brahmin! Did you not tell me to fast?” he demanded furiously.

Only then did Kuppu Sastri remember. “What a foolish thing I have done! Half-asleep, I blurted out the truth and exposed my secret,” he thought in alarm.

“I did not eat now. I ate it in the morning,” he drawled.

His grandson Kittu was a mischievous boy.

“Why are you lying, Grandfather? Uncle, they made the sweet pongal in our house only just now,” he revealed.

A voice from inside asked, “Is that Sarma from next door? Shall I send you a bowl too? There is a little left.”

Embarrassment ran over Kuppu Sastri’s face.

“You are a scoundrel!” Sarma raged. “You told me to fast and swore you would do so too. After taking such an oath, how could you secretly eat? Shall I expose and disgrace you?”

Kuppu Sastri could only stammer.

“Look, Sarma, listen to this...” he began, attempting some reconciliation.

“What is there to see or hear? The system says false men like you may own property while women may not. I want the arrogance of your kind crushed.”

Sarma returned home.

“Did you give away all the sambar, dried chillies and chutney, or is at least a little left?” he asked his wife.

“Is your stomach gnawing?”

“No. I have joined your side. Lay out a leaf. I listened to that sinner next door and thought of fasting. Do you know what he did? He told me to fast, went inside, ate sweet pongal until his stomach nearly split, then rolled about on his veranda.

“He was the one who raved: ‘Can women be given property? Is it not against scripture? Can the Vedic arrangement be ruined?’ Splendidly should women be given property! Lakshmi herself presides over wealth. Are women not forms of Lakshmi? Is it not foolish to say they possess no property rights?”

Sarma now spoke in support of the bill.

“That is how you should speak! Is it enough merely to repeat ‘ancient days’ and ‘ancestral custom’? If something appears right to reason, should it not be followed? From now on, speak for this law to come quickly,” his wife advised.

“Very well. Shall we speak after eating?” Sarma reminded her of the immediate matter.

Only after the sambar, chutney and dried chillies entered him would sleep come. But upon hearing the lady’s reply, the poor man all but wept.

What did she say?

“What rice is there now? Everything was given to the house opposite. Even the vessels have been washed.”

He went inside and looked. The vessels shone brightly, as though laughing at him in mockery.

*Dravida Nadu*, 24 October 1943

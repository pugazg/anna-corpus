## English Translation

Tamil title: சமூக சேவகி சாருபாலா

Social worker Charubala had a cold and fever, and Shanti Bhavan was thrown into an uproar. She was the only daughter of Income Tax Officer Kothandarama Iyer; naturally there would be a commotion. Besides, Charubala was a young woman becoming famous for social service.

Her hair was dark and abundant, framing a round moon of a face. She had a crescent forehead, speaking eyes, tremulous lips, and a body of gold; her walk was a dance and her speech sweet. She was twenty-two and had studied through Intermediate.

Decorative spectacles, an ornamental handbag, fashionable boots, diamond earrings, a wristwatch, and a fine chain around her neck; always smiling and pleasant to everyone, Charubala was herself a sight in that city.

Who could avoid being captivated? A miser who set out after consulting omens, intending to complain, "My income is very low; this year brought enormous losses," and obtain the officer's mercy would see this golden image and instead boast of the greatness of his business. From that, judge the force of the charming storm she created.

Kothandarama Iyer never crossed the line drawn by his wife. He firmly believed that her good fortune alone had brought him his high post. Charubala was their only child, so in accordance with his life's companion's wishes he granted her complete freedom.

"The child waits until you return from the office whenever she wants to go out, because she needs the car. Buy her a small one, an Austin or a Vauxhall. It will also be convenient for going to the club in the evening," her mother would say. The following week an Austin would stand at Shanti Bhavan.

That was how fondly they raised Charubala: imagine a golden creeper planted in a bed of diamonds. Gold and diamonds may glitter, but can they look, speak, and laugh like Charu?

The moment she developed a cold and fever, Shanti Bhavan was in turmoil.

The cook was interrogated.

"Knowing the child had a cold, why did you make okra sambar? You complete fool!" the officer demanded.

The gardener suffered an assault of questions. "When Charu came into the garden after six, an icy mist-wind was blowing. Why did you not warn her and send her indoors?"

The maidservants were reprimanded and the motor driver warned, all because Charu's temperature was 101 degrees.

"Why do you torment your body unnecessarily and trouble Appa's heart? I just used the thermometer. It is only 101," Charu said. But would their hearts listen? Her eyes, ordinarily red only at their edges, were now faintly red throughout. Her face always shone; now it had a new gleam. She coughed four or five times while speaking. How could anyone accept her assurance not to worry? A doctor was summoned.

Doctor Raghuraman had a budding moustache. He was an Iyer, but non-Brahmins boasted as though only they knew how to grow moustaches. He had issued a challenge at medical college: "See whether I cannot do it too," and begun growing one. It had come out attractively.

For years Doctor Raghuraman had believed he possessed a singular beauty capable of enchanting any maiden. Baring one's teeth at women, gaping at their beauty, losing oneself when they spoke, and stammering when speaking to them were the acts of foolish men. His own medical prescription was an indifferent glance and a slight smile that vanished within a second. Only by behaving thus could one break a woman's pride, he often told his college friends.

Charubala's room contained a teak cot with a damask-covered mattress and pillows. Pictures of Pandit Nehru and Mother India hung on the wall beside the slogan that everyone had a part in the Five-Year Plan. On the table stood Bharathiar, Sarat Chandra, Shelley, and Shakespeare. At the window was a slightly damaged Buddha statue beside a brand-new Sai Baba.

The beautiful woman lay on the cot with her eyes closed, but a dimple showed in her cheek, betraying some inward delight. Pond's cold cream and powder covered her face. Incense perfumed the room. She wore a leaf-green georgette sari and a matching jacket.

If sages who had performed fierce penance for thousands of years saw Charubala in that form, they would become Viswamitras. What, then, could happen to poor Doctor Raghuraman?

He became confused. He held the thermometer upside down and was struck by Charu's teasing. He asked how long "it" had been there. She laughed and softly mocked him as mad. Since he had to give her something for his visit, he handed her cod-liver-oil tablets and said he would send medicine.

"This fever is nothing serious, Doctor. But at night I cannot get proper sleep."

"If the breeze is good... otherwise you might sleep on the terrace."

"Is this not the terrace?"

"Yes, but by terrace I mean outside, not in the room. You could sleep in the open."

"In the bare open? I might get flu."

"Yes, you might. Flu is very bad."

"Doctor, there is good ventilation here. That is not why I cannot sleep."

"Do you have pain somewhere, in the head, arms, or legs?"

"Do you think I am actually a patient, Doctor?"

"No, no! You are very healthy."

"All my friends tease me: 'What could be wrong with you, Charu? You look like a fine apple.'"

"A golden... a golden apple. That is... that is..."

"Enough of 'that is'! Were you called here to tease me?"

"No, no! Forgive me. It is rather hot. I am a little thirsty."

"Yes. I usually drink soda, or else hot water. Only at night do I drink ice water."

"You want hot water now, do you not?"

"If there is some, please ask them to give it."

"'If there is some'? Even a public rest house would have hot water. You have formed a very poor opinion of our home."

"Oh! You misunderstood me."

"I shall bring it in a second. Please sit down. You have been standing ever since you arrived."

"It is all right. I am accustomed to it from a young age: walking, tennis..."

Charubala went inside to bring him hot water.

The bewildered doctor sat in a chair, feeling almost faint.

"Charu! In your condition, should you come down yourself? If the doctor asks for water to wash his hands, we can take it. Do not strain yourself," Parvatham advised as she entered. She asked, "Doctor, how is the child's health? There is nothing to fear, is there?" Then, studying his pallid face, she nearly had to ask, "Are you unwell?"

The doctor was offered milk, fruit juice, and everything else as though it were divine nectar.

"Thanks. No, enough. You sit down... no, lie down," he said.

"Doctor, are calcium injections good?"

"Yes, calcium is very good. We can give one tomorrow."

"How many injections make a course?"

"Six. For your body, even eight would be good."

"Then that is what I must begin tomorrow. What may I eat now?"

"Anything, but only a little at a time."

"If I do not eat a little at a time, will I eat in storeys? Why do you speak as though I were Bhadrakali? My friends call me a Paris beauty."

"Call that teasing? No, no. Are there any beauties in Paris now? But tell me, how did you catch this cold?"

"Ask her that, Doctor! Ask her properly. But she will not open her mouth and answer. Look at her, all skin and bone. Do you know what she does?" Parvatham began.

"Go away, Amma. Must you tell him all that?"

"Who else should hear about your pranks? Now that you are ill, does my heart listen to reason? The doctor says you need eight injections. Yet here she is, thin as a single pulse, roaming from village to village without food or water in the name of social service. They sweep mud, mix lime, and even do garden work from morning until darkness falls. Will fever not come? Can her body bear it? Is it good for lime to touch her hands? Tell her, Doctor."

"Very wrong! Very wrong!"

"What is wrong, Doctor? Is serving the poor wrong?"

"Who would say so? Even our Jawaharlal has said social service is commendable."

"But does social service mean sweeping mud and mixing lime? Is she the person suited for that? Such work should be done by strong, sturdy people."

"Yes, it does not suit her body. That is why she caught cold. Still, there is no danger. Calcium will suffice."

Parvatham pointed out that it was late. Forgetting his stethoscope there, Doctor Raghuraman returned to his nursing home.

Only after the sixth injection did Doctor Aravamudhan tell Sundarammal, Raghuraman's mother, that it was paratyphoid and must be watched carefully.

After returning from Charubala's house, Raghuraman's temperature reached 102. It rose and fell; his body burned, then sweated; it rose again. At last Doctor Aravamudhan diagnosed paratyphoid.

Charubala had no experience of nursing, but during those sixteen days she became a highly capable nurse. Love was administered dose by dose: a glance, a smile, conversation, rubbing oil onto his forehead, drawing a blanket over him, opening the window slightly, giving medicine at the proper hours, and, when he stubbornly refused to open his mouth, stroking his cheek, making him laugh, and feeding him gruel.

Could paratyphoid not last for months? Doctor Raghuraman wondered, if only for the sake of such a fine life.

Vedanthachari had made it his principal occupation to find a suitable groom for social worker Charubala. He had laid traps for every unmarried I.A.S. officer, but somehow all the birds escaped. Whenever Charubala saw him, she teased him playfully.

"Charu, shall I look for a man in the Navy? The Navy!"

"Why? Has the work of catching an I.A.S. man failed?"

"Your horoscope is like that, Charu. What can I do? I have devised plan after plan, but none succeeds. The fellow is handsome enough. I, however, have grown old."

"What does that matter? King Dasaratha married Kaikeyi in his old age."

"Charu, do you intend to make me dance as Kaikeyi made him dance?"

"Listen to that desire!"

Their banter flowed freely. The mother delighted in the child's cleverness, while the father cried "Bravo." That was the family's charming custom.

Andal from the neighbouring house sometimes asked, "Grandmother, why does Charu often get colds and fevers? You know all manner of remedies. Why have you not prevented them?"

"Why would she not get them? Will it stop with fever? Cramped limbs, yellow eyes, bowel ailments, everything will come. This is what happens when the proper thing is not done at the proper age. At her age I had two children. Look at her, dancing about like a show-horse and wandering around saying she is going to perform some service. How can illness fail to come?" the grandmother, a treasury of experiential medicine, would say. Then she habitually lamented, "Is it only she? All girls nowadays are like this."

As though resolving by herself the marriage problem that had troubled so many, Charu placed Doctor Raghuraman's photograph on her table and happily adorned it with a zari garland.

"Rare service in six villages! Removing moss and cleaning the steps of eighteen sacred tanks, providing convenience to hundreds of thousands! Teaching sanitary living to people sunk in ignorance, unable to live cleanly in clusters of palm-leaf huts! Teaching village children kolattam and dance and arranging delightful recreation in many villages! Who do you think accomplished all this? None other than Charubala, seated beside Doctor Raghuraman like Janaki herself."

When Sub-Judge Sabesa Iyer spoke these words at the wedding felicitation, loud cheers arose.

The wedding was a feast for the eyes of the entire district. The Collector read the Governor's message of congratulations. Merchants stood in rows and presented wedding gifts. Pranatharthi, administrator of the Bharatha Seva Samaj, won praise by presenting a plough and other agricultural implements made of silver and plated with gold. There were dance, music, a dummy-horse performance, and kolattam by schoolgirls. The celebration was magnificent.

Only on rare occasions can one see such a scene of delight.

See how happily the young husband speaks as he combs the hair of his creeper-like bride, and how enchantingly she looks at him. Thinking themselves alone, the young lovers play in this fashion. My boy, how many times will you draw that queen of beauty onto your lap? Have you no pity? Though she says, "Enough, enough," he murmurs endearments and pecks at those red lips. Sinner, must you embrace the young woman so tightly that she struggles to breathe? Mad girl, he torments you so, yet you laugh merrily!

Yes, that is right. Have you closed your eyes? Good. Sleep gently. Let sweet memories become your lullaby. But shameless man, what are you doing now, caressing her feet? They boast of the male lion and master of the house, yet here he sits at her feet. It is a sight like a golden plate placed over a marble lotus.

The young man seems a master sculptor admiring everywhere the beauty of his own creation: cheek and forehead, lovely shoulder and lightning waist. They say he is a doctor, one who knows anatomy and should cure disease. Here he is causing it. The moonlight seemed to watch and relish the scene.

Raghuraman saw the moon not by looking upward, but as it reclined in his lap and sat beside him. He repeatedly moved aside the dark hair that hid it, gazed at the stainless moon of her face, spoke of many things, asked for many things, and promised many things. With a flowered smile she shook her head as though saying, "Impossible, impossible." With one pitiful look capable of melting her heart like syrup, he defeated her in a moment.

The field of love had a contest every day: Charu victorious today, Raghuraman tomorrow. Watching their games of delight night after night, the moon waned thinner and thinner. What did it matter that the moon waned? He had obtained a full moon of his own.

Lost in the cool moonlight of her gaze, he forgot himself. As though angered that the acquisition of a beautiful bride had made him forget duty entirely, the moon closed its eyes. The lamp of the sky went out, while the flame in his heart continued to blaze. The love life of Charubala and Raghuraman budded, opened, and flowered.

"Charu, what is this scar?" Doctor Raghuraman asked.

"You are very wicked! You do it yourself and then tease me about it," Charu replied in feigned anger. This was not the sort of anger that made the face explode like sesame and mustard seeds; the setting tells us what kind of anger it was.

The previous night that scoundrel had coaxed and teased her until he left a mark. Would she not grow angry if he asked about it the next day?

But while stroking her cheek, Raghuraman had noticed a small scar on her forehead. That was what he meant.

"This? It is a gift from social service."

"A gift?"

"Yes. In Yemur, the wall supporting the roof over a hut's sleeping place had decayed. That poor man had not noticed it. I discovered it and applied fresh plaster. A wooden beam struck my head while I worked. This scar was the gift I received during social service," Charu explained.

"Did it bleed badly, Charu? You must be extremely careful doing such work. Did that fool simply stand there when the beam fell on your head?" the doctor asked.

"Sir! Sir!" called a voice.

Compounder Gundappan was always like this, a bear intruding upon Siva worship, Raghuraman thought as he went downstairs.

"Swami! He is our only child, the shoot of our lineage. We obtained him after long penance. Look at this injustice, sir. Pour milk into my stomach, Doctor; save him," a villager cried.

His four-year-old child lay on a bench. The skull had been crushed and blood was flowing. The condition was critical.

Doctor Raghuraman felt genuine pity at the horrible sight. The compounder began preparing what was needed for treatment.

"How did this happen?"

"The child was lying asleep, Doctor. In my evil hour, a section of the cracked wall near the roof fell on his head."

"Are you all fools? What were you doing when it fell?"

"I was milking the cow. When the child screamed, I ran in from the garden and found him like this. My wife had gone out."

"How did the wall crack and fall? Why did you leave it in such a dangerous state?"

"Why ask, Doctor? Some city women came saying they would repair our house without cost. I pleaded, 'Do not, Amma. Give us the money and we shall do it ourselves. We know how the work must be done. What do you know of it?'

"One forward young woman threatened me: 'Do you prevent us when we come to help? Do you know who we are? We are government people working without pay. If you obstruct us, we shall report you to the police and have you punished.'

"I told them politely, 'Why must you do this work? People like us are needed to work in your bungalows, gardens, forests, and fields. You come here to do our work like someone selling needles in a blacksmith's street. It is not right.'

"The girl who first spoke harshly then began smiling. 'Elder, we must renovate thirty huts somehow. It is our association's plan. Do not obstruct us,' she said. They ordered us to bring mud, lime, and stones and ruined a sound wall under the claim of plastering it.

"It rained four days ago. That was enough. The plaster dissolved, a slab fell, and my child came to this state."

The compounder took the doctor aside. Raghuraman did not return.

The compounder told the villager, "Take the child to the large hospital. Nothing can be done here."

Crying, "Oh God!" the villager gathered up the child, whose body hung limp like the trunk of a banana plant.

18 September 1955

## Translator's Notes

The title identifies Charubala as a “social worker” but the phrase is progressively exposed as social prestige detached from competence and accountability. English words, consumer brands, caste-marked speech, and medical claims are retained as period satire rather than current advice.

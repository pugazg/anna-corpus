## English Translation

She laughed when she saw the suffering he endured!

The maiden thought, “If even this man suffers so, how much must all the others suffer?” And she laughed.

Who was she? Why did she laugh? You will naturally be eager to know. It cannot be told at once. This concerns eminent people, not useless nobodies. You must be patient for a little while.

Bundle upon bundle of sacred ash! Rudraksha beads around his neck! Tears welling and flowing from his eyes as he stands with folded hands! Anyone seeing the sight would marvel: “Is this not devotion? Is he not a servant devoted to Siva? Is he not our guide?” They would fall at his feet. He sang until hearts melted:

“Ocean of grace! Sea of bliss! King of the Ambalam! Lord of the celestials! First of the world! He who has Uma as one half!”

Such was the devotee who sang hymns and worshipped the Supreme Being.

And such a man saw the daughter of a courtesan and fell in love.

Inside the temple!

The love kindled in his heart rose into flame.

The servant who sang of Mahesan bearing the battle-axe staggered about, unable to endure the love that had arisen within him.

With the very mouth that had sung the Lord’s divine form, he sang the beauty of the woman’s form.

He had never shown such eagerness or haste even to receive the Lord’s grace. But the moment he saw that beauty, he throbbed with the determination that he must possess her.

A servant! A companion of God! He saw a woman and surrendered his heart.

Ordinary people go to a temple in the hope of ridding themselves of worldly corruption. They are instructed: “Do not surrender your heart to a slender waist and kovvai-red lips and wander in this illusory world. Behold the Lord with the earring and cast off lust, anger, pride and jealousy.”

The devotee needed no instruction. He himself was the teacher. Yet he forgot the Lord, gave desire a place in his heart and flew about in agitation. Could the maiden help laughing?

That is why she laughed when she saw the trouble he endured.

And the place where the devotee contracted the disease of love was a temple! He went there for a vision of God, saw a parrot-voiced woman and surrendered his heart.

Was this the full extent of his firmness? What distinction remained between him and other men? His heart overflowed with devotion, yet waves of love beat within it.

An ordinary man would at least notice the place, suppress his sighs and leave. This man paid no heed to place or circumstance. Forgetting the Lord of the golden body, he began praising a flowering vine.

And so the maiden laughed. She laughed until her lovely row of teeth could be seen.

You ask who she was, and whose condition made her laugh. I warned you at the beginning that this concerned eminent people.

You might imagine some youth, fit to be rebuked with the words, “Through play-acting you behave like one of His servants,” entering the holy dwelling of purity, the noble place said to cure the disease of birth, only to be bewitched by a woman.

Was that why he went to the temple?

The wise and the learned said it was excellent to worship in a temple so that one might uproot the evils arising in the mind while moving elsewhere. But see the impropriety here: a man who had attained the rank called “devotee” saw a beautiful woman in the temple and fell in love.

Before learning who he was, you would say, “Shame! Shame!” If this happened to an ordinary person, everyone would condemn him.

“Iniquity! Sacrilege!” the elders would exclaim.

“It is a sin even to admit such depraved men into the temple. Do these bulls come to see God? Do they not come to wink and signal at women?” they would ask in anguish.

They would say all this and more if a man went to a temple, saw a woman graceful as a peacock and fell in love.

But the elders would not speak that way of this man.

For he was a devotee: none other than Saint Sundaramurthi Nayanar himself.

He went to the temple at the sacred town of Tiruvarur for worship. There he saw Paravaiyar, a woman of the courtesan community, and fell in love. He had come singing and worshipping the Supreme Being; he saw the maiden Paravai. Devotion had held him, but now love seized him and made him dance.

Inside the temple itself!

Going to the temple was proper, and it is true that he sang hymns. But it appears he had not yet gained the maturity needed to guard his mind. That must be why, in the holy place where he went to worship the Supreme Being, his mind began to wander.

Yet the time when he fell in love was not an immature period. Judge whether he lacked spiritual maturity from these verses:

> As the senses of the five became but eyes,
> the four immeasurable inner instruments became thought,
> and the three qualities became pure sattva,
> they bathed in the flood of supreme bliss
> produced by the boundless, peerless dance of joy
> performed by Him whose locks bear the moon,
> and blossomed in changeless delight.

And again:

> O Lord whose locks bloom with the lucid moon,
> having received the grace to worship Your sacred dance,
> I say this birth I took upon the earth
> is itself youthful, itself delight.
> With streams of bliss pouring from his eyes,
> with flower-like hands joined above his head,
> he sang rare hymns prolonged by melody,
> praised and bowed.

This was at Tillai, before the visit to Tiruvarur. His hands were joined above his head. He sang rare hymns, praised and bowed. “Do I possess any joy other than this?” he asked. He even celebrated the birth that had enabled him to receive this bliss.

He had therefore attained such “maturity.” One cannot dismiss his conduct as an immature state. His heart melted when he beheld the primal Lord who wears the crescent moon. Then he saw Paravaiyar and melted like syrup. Look at the verse:

> Is she a young doe? A divine young breast?
> A fresh honeyed lip? A tender coral vine
> born of the ocean wave? A young forest creeper?
> A tender shoot of moonlight? Or the matchless young bow
> upon which Kama himself learns his youthful art?

O supreme devotee who praised and worshipped the Lord and sang, “Is there any joy apart from this?” The instant you saw Paravaiyar, you offered worship to her:

“Is she a doe? A flower? Honey?”

How was that possible?

He sought grace and went to the temple knowing it to be the place where grace could be found. How did this thought enter him there? Love for a flower in the very place where he went in search of merit!

Could such a thing be permitted? If it was merely natural, what distinction remained between an ordinary man and a Nayanar?

He showed no concern about it, nor did he wonder whether others might be concerned. He called it the merit of all merits and declared it to be Siva’s own grace. Here is the verse:

> Is she a flowering bough of the wish-giving tree?
> The great fortune of Kama?
> The merit of all radiant merit?
> O fragrant vine bearing flowers of coral and blue lily,
> heavy as a rain cloud,
> are you a wonder, or the grace of Siva?
> I do not know, he marvelled.

Who could see this condition and refrain from laughing?

It was like saying, “Instruction is for the town, not for you”; like the proverb that the lizard which foretells everything falls into the pot of gruel; like saying that the sidelong glance of a maiden can even bleach the dye from an ascetic’s ochre robe.

Call him a divinely inspired poet, the Lord’s companion, or a supreme teacher born to spread the path of devotion. An ordinary man would still try to suppress the thought that had arisen. This man composed beautiful poetry about it.

How exquisitely he had sung of Siva, “O Lord whose locks bloom with the lucid moon! O Lord whose matted hair bears the moon!” In precisely the same fashion he praised Paravaiyar as “the flowering bough of the wish-giving tree” and “the fragrant vine bearing honeyed blossoms.”

There was more. Someone might ask, “Did the man who went seeking merit and Siva’s grace wander in search of this ‘great fortune of Kama’?” Anticipating the question, he silenced every mouth by declaring that this itself was Siva’s grace.

When people see how a beautiful woman can alter the condition of a devotee, many can only laugh. But to prevent disgrace from touching the devotee’s name, the poet who told the story said that the maiden who laughed at his plight was none other than the maiden called Night: when she laughed, the moon rose. This, he asks us to understand, is the meaning of the verse:

> When the Lord’s servant wandered distressed through the street,
> Night’s fragrant-flowered younger sister wondered,
> “If he suffers so, what man can escape women with slender waists?”
> Her faint smile blossomed as the white moon.

Thus Night, the maiden, laughed, displaying the white moon as her row of teeth.

Everyone who examines the conduct of devotees can only laugh. If a devotee went to a temple and fell in love with a courtesan, is it possible not to laugh?

The “devotee” discovered “Siva’s grace” in this manner. Paravaiyar acquired the devotee as her companion and tasted “devotion.” To ensure that this sacred service continued without interruption, the devotee needed wealth. What did he do? Did he load goods upon pack bulls, cross many forests, trade in distant lands and earn money? Did he undertake hard physical labour? Why should he? Who was he? A devotee, and a Brahmin at that! Would the Lord allow him to toil?

A man called Kundaiyur Kizhar was a Vellala and a great landholder. He became the servant of the devotee who had received “Siva’s grace” in the form of Paravaiyar and was performing this “service.”

Kundaiyur Kizhar supplied all the red rice and golden rich lentils they required. Many people who laboured without seeing the fruit of their work continued to starve. How could Kundaiyur Kizhar find time to notice and help every one of them? Which was more important: attending to the Brahmin who had gone to the temple and received the Lord’s grace in the form of a woman, or attending to poor wretches who laboured like cattle, suffered worse than dogs and lamented that death had not yet come? Surely it was more important to see that the “service to Siva” performed by the devotee and the woman proceeded without interruption!

Kundaiyur Kizhar therefore gave the devotee red rice and lentils like gold. He rejoiced inwardly, thinking: “What more do I lack? My name will now enter the Lord’s ledger, and a superior place in the upper world is assured.”

You may say that if men like Kundaiyur Kizhar kept giving us all the wealth we needed, we too would find a Paravaiyar suited to us, perform Siva-puja and pass our days. But are you devotees? Even if you were devotees, are you Brahmins, that such fortune should come to you?

Kandappan had worked as a bonded labourer for landlord Marthandam Pillai from the day the white bones first sprouted in his legs. His wife suffered from a severe stomach disease. The landlord told him to send her to the “charity hospital,” then gave Kandappan fifty rupees and ordered him to deliver it to Sivachariyar, priest of the Ainkaran temple. Sivachariyar intended to send it to his son Sekaran, who was studying for a B.A. in a Madras college and owed six rupees merely for cigarettes.

That is the way of the present day. In the old days Kundaiyur Kizhar helped Sundarar with red rice and lentils.

Not only the Vellala patron but even the Supreme Being wearing the crescent moon behaved in this fashion.

Kundaiyur Kizhar supplied Sundarar with paddy. After a time the rains failed and a severe famine came. A great worry began to torment Kundaiyur Kizhar. Why? Was it because the cultivators who ploughed and harvested, filling his granary with red rice, were now struggling even for gruel? Was it because people generally suffered under the famine? Shame! Only ordinary people like you would entertain such a commonplace worry. His concern was different:

“Today there is no grain to send to the mansion of the valiant devotee at Tiruvarur. What shall I do?”

“Alas, what shall I do? Sundaramurthi Nayanar lives at Tiruvarur with his Siva-given grace, Paravaiyar, and I cannot supply him with paddy!” This was Kundaiyur Kizhar’s distress.

Did the devotee worry, “The Vellala of Kundaiyur used to help us with abundant paddy and lentils. How terribly the poor man must suffer in this famine”? The hymn does not tell us that he did. It says that Kundaiyur Kizhar was the one who worried, asking what he could do.

Siva supplied an abundance of paddy, heaping it mountain upon mountain at Kundaiyur, and told the old man plainly: “This is for Sundarar.”

Did Siva say, “Devout man, I commend the loving heart with which you have supplied my servant. Here I have piled paddy like mountains. Take my grace, distribute it freely and help all the poor, for are you not a generous patron? Would I let you suffer?” No. He stated unambiguously that the paddy was for Sundarar. Could anyone refrain from laughing? Not merely that maiden; all of us must laugh.

Sundarar’s own words and deeds make us laugh still more.

Did the devotee sing, “Kundaiyur Kizhar, hill of virtue! How greatly Siva loves you; has He not showered grace upon you?” and then say, “Let this red rice obtained through Siva’s grace remain with you while famine spreads, so that it may help the poor”? Would that Brahmin devotee say such a thing? Listen to what he did say:

> Thinking of You for ever and ever,
> I, Your servant, shall worship You daily with folded hands!

Thus he praised Siva.

We too say, “Supreme Lord, we do not need paddy piled like mountains. Give us enough food to satisfy our hunger and we shall worship Your lotus feet every day.” Those who battle starvation continue saying so. But the Supreme Being creates Kundaiyur landholders and, alongside them, high-born devotees entitled to receive their aid as a right.

Paddy lay heaped like mountains. Surely joy should have overflowed! Instead Sundarar developed a worry. What worry? There was no one to carry it to Tiruvarur! He asked the Lord for that as well:

> So that the sword-eyed maiden may not pine and suffer,
> O our Lord of Kolili, I have received a little paddy at Kundaiyur.
> There is no one to carry it, my Lord;
> command that it be transported and delivered!

He said, “I have received this paddy, but there is no one to take it to Tiruvarur. Send labourers for that too.” And the Lord sent them.

Mountains upon mountains of paddy, yet he called it “a little paddy”!

“What extraordinarily magnificent charity did he perform? He gave fifty paltry rupees as a religious donation, that is all,” Sivachariyar says. Sundaramurthi Nayanar likewise said, “I received a little paddy.”

Ilayankudi Maranar was also a devotee of Siva. Unlike Sundarar, he did not receive, through Siva’s grace, both Paravai, a woman like a golden statue, and a hill of red rice. He exhausted everything he owned feeding Siva’s devotees. His possessions vanished and he was ruined. At that moment Siva came disguised as a devotee.

It was midnight. He asked for food. There was nothing in the devotee’s home; they themselves were starving. Ilayankudi Maranar panicked and wrung his hands.

He received no help from a Kundaiyur Kizhar for the sacred service of feeding devotees. He did not receive even the “little paddy” that Sundarar received. Not only did Siva give him nothing, He came at midnight and demanded food, while a heavy rain poured down. What could Ilayankudi do? That very day he had sown paddy in his field. He went there, gathered the scattered grains and brought them home to prepare a meal. Only afterwards did the Lord grant him “residence in Kailasa.”

Why was Ilayankudi Maranar granted this condition and Sundaramurthi Nayanar that one? We do not know what the good men who conduct a trade in words while claiming to understand the nature of His grace might say. One fact shines plainly: Ilayankudi Maranar was a Vellala; Sundaramurthi Nayanar was a Brahmin. The way the Supreme Being showered grace upon a Brahmin devotee and upon a devotee of a non-Brahmin community closely resembles the way landlord Marthandam Pillai treats Kandappan and Sivachariyar today. Is it possible not to laugh?

Sundarar, recipient of such “special” grace, entered the “holy” temple, saw Paravaiyar and fell in love. How could laughter fail to arise?

> “Restrain the five senses, restrain them,” say the ignorant;
> even among the immortals there is none who has restrained all five,

said Tirumular.

Why go to the world of the immortals? Here before us is the plight of the world of devotees!

Chettiar's account was written by his clerk in a cloth-bound ledger. Clerk Kumarappan entered his own note in a pocket diary. Lokamirtha Iyer recorded his detail in a notebook that young Kokilam had beautifully stitched from consecration advertisements and buried it safely inside the *Bhagavatam*. Pankajavalli's account was written on the back of a cinema handbill. What paper did Kandan and Kuppi possess on which to write?

Kandan came home and gave one rupee.

“What is this? Ruin upon us! What can a single rupee accomplish?”

“Go away. What do you know of how I had to show my teeth to that fellow to obtain even this?”

“Is that so? Tell me. Did he give only one rupee?”

“Yes, woman...”

“Your condition is plain enough. Why must you tell me? The way you sway while standing says it all. Did you pay the debt collector?”

“Would he let me go? He took eight annas.”

“The remainder?”

“There is some.”

“Where?”

“If I say it exists, will you not leave me alone? You keep tearing at a man.”

“Tell me where.”

“I cannot. Go away! I cannot tell you, and I cannot show you.”

That was how the account was entered. At the end of the conversation, Kandan's hand left its mark upon Kuppi's back, and her voice bored into the ears of the neighbours. Kuppi then recorded her own receipts and expenses by raining blows upon the back of a child who asked for a snack.

“That sinner gave me one rupee after paying the debt collector and the liquor shop. Half a rupee went for rice, two annas for chillies, one anna for salt, and two for oil. I had tied the last two annas in the end of my sari. They are sacrificing a goat to Mother Kattal at the shrine, and the priest asked me to give something. I gave him what I had. What else do I possess? If you want a snack, I must go out stealing. I bought dried fish for one anna and am about to cook it. If you howl instead of eating that, you will receive only a beating!”

Thus Kuppi shouted her record of income and expenditure.

Set money aside for a moment and consider the labour in this family accounting.

## Chettiar's Working Day

Chettiar opened his eyes at 7:30 in the morning. He rose from bed at 7:45. By 8:30 he had completed his morning necessities. Until 9:30 he remained at home and ate his breakfast and accompanying dishes.

The watchman came to say that the cart was ready.

“You thick donkey! You are becoming an utter idler,” Chettiar raged.

That “idler” had risen at 4:20 that morning. Chettiar had told him to inform the farmhand that the transplanting must be finished quickly. Kandan delivered the instruction and ran straight to the garden, where he ordered six tender coconuts to be brought to the shop at noon for the master. On the way back he visited the paddy market, counted roughly ten carts so that he could report them, and went to his hut.

Kuppi said there was no cut firewood and that the tamarind wood lay in a single bundle. He took an axe, struck it four times, placed a stem in a betel leaf, and returned to Chettiar's bungalow. He told the driver, “Master is about to depart,” and only then reported that the cart had arrived.

Chettiar scolded this “idler” and went into his wife's room. He emerged at eleven. Meanwhile the cart had been unyoked and yoked again three times.

Idle Kandan watered the crotons, filled and levelled a hollow near the fence with a spade, plucked fruit from the wood-apple tree and delivered it indoors, wiped away his sweat, shook out his dyed cloth, and wrapped it around his head.

Chettiar demanded, “What arrogance has seized you? Shaking out your cloth, tying a turban, and strolling about like a bridegroom: do you perform any work properly? You have deteriorated badly. Do you not wish to remain here and work properly?”

The watchman bowed and danced in submission.

Having completed his household business, Chettiar left for the shop to extract work from the “idlers” there. And how did he go? By cart.

The bull caused some trouble on the way. For four days the driver had said, “There is no shoe on it, sir. The animal will not move; its hoof has worn down.”

“You thieving fellow! It has not even been three months since the shoe was fixed. Has it worn away already? Does the farrier pay you commission to torment my life every month by crying 'shoe, shoe'? We shall see next month. Strike it and drive.”

That day the driver did strike it. The bull lay down.

“Thick fellow! Buffalo! Clay in your skull!” Chettiar blessed him with abuse, hired a passing cart, and reached the shop. Six annas wasted on fare!

“Welcome, Chettiar! You look exhausted and thin,” greeted a man indebted to him.

“Yes, I have been running about a great deal,” Chettiar replied.

“Now they all gather and talk at Ambika,” said the debtor.

“Who?” Chettiar asked while looking at his shop clerk. The clerk looked at the boy; the boy took up a fan, stood beside the master, and performed his duty.

“Who else? What is this, Chettiar, are you asleep? Those three addresses have arrived.”

“From Coimbatore? Salem?”

“The Salem men.”

“What are they doing at Ambika?”

“P.V. takes them to Ambika Bhavan, buys coffee and snacks, and urges them to purchase goods from him.”

“Is that so?”

The two left the shop for Ambika Bhavan, found the men, and discovered that P.V. was absent. Coffee and refreshments for all three went upon Chettiar's account: a bill of two rupees and eight annas. They all returned to the shop.

“The rent money has arrived,” the clerk said.

At that moment Chettiar was explaining to the Salem traders the difference between dried plantain and dried potato. The clerk hinted two or three times, but Chettiar did not hear. After the traders left, having consumed oranges worth another rupee and eight annas, Chettiar summoned the clerk.

“Look here. I shall tell you gently, so listen. I do not like your conduct in the slightest.”

The clerk began to answer. Chettiar's voice rose.

“Do not talk back. Step down from this shop and walk out respectfully!” Words flew in a stream, and the clerk fell silent.

Ten minutes later Chettiar began his inquiry.

“You become angry. Do you perform even one task correctly? Did I not tell you to collect the rent?”

“You did, and I...”

“I told you, and you merely asked and came away, great lord. Is it your ancestral house?”

“I said the rent had arrived.”

“The rent arrived? Why stand there like a wooden ox? I have been shouting myself dry about the rent. You kept your mouth closed and now whisper the secret that it came?”

“I told you then, sir, while the Salem men were here...”

“What! Did you speak before them? Can such things be said in their presence?”

“That is why I spoke softly.”

“What rubbish did you say? How much rent did he give?”

The clerk named the amount.

“Why is it two rupees short?”

“He says it was the expense of whitewashing.”

“Who asked him to whitewash?”

“The sanitary inspector sent a notice.”

“What do I care what notice came? Did I order the whitewash? Whom did he ask? How can he deduct two rupees from the rent? What tale is this?”

“I tried to tell him, sir...”

“He refused, and you accepted it? Why betray me this way? Listen: those two rupees must be collected. Otherwise I shall deduct them from your account.”

After this affection, the person who owed interest paid it.

Lunch arrived at the shop. Chettiar ate. The boy cleaned the place. One clerk poured water for him to wash his hands; another gave him a cloth. Meanwhile a mattress was spread, and Chettiar took restorative rest. The boy fanned him.

At about five in the evening he rose. He sent the clerk to the medicine shop, used the boy to summon the lawyer's clerk, and gave the latter money.

Meanwhile the “idle” watchman had supervised field work, eaten a little gruel, carried the tender coconuts from the garden, learned that Chettiar was asleep, gone to the bungalow, attended to work there, and returned to the shop. Chettiar tossed money at him.

As Chettiar prepared to leave for home, the priest arrived. Chettiar welcomed him with a bright face, and a sacred storytelling session began.

**Chettiar:** Swami, welcome! We never receive the blessing of seeing you these days.

**Priest:** What are you saying? It is obtaining an audience with Chettiar that has become as rare as a horse's horn.

**Chettiar:** Siva! Siva! Swami, why speak of me so extravagantly?

**Priest:** Is it only I? Do you know what Kamala said yesterday evening?

**Chettiar:** Kamala? Does she speak even about me?

**Priest:** Splendid! You ask whether she speaks of you? I do not know what enchantment powder you cast upon her. Her thoughts are always upon you.

**Chettiar:** You are only teasing.

**Priest:** I swear it.

**Chettiar:** Siva! Siva! Very well, what did she say?

**Priest:** She said that seeing Chettiar is like witnessing Kanchi's Garuda festival.

**Chettiar:** Did Kamala say that? She is always extremely clever in speech.

**Priest:** Why say it that way? Is speech her only talent?

**Chettiar:** She is accomplished in everything.

**Priest:** Who else in this town can sing like her...

**Chettiar:** Or dance...

**Priest:** It is Nataraja's cosmic dance itself.

**Chettiar:** She has a good nature, but is a little exacting about money.

**Priest:** That is natural, Chettiar. She too will attend tomorrow's *panchamirtha* ablution.

**Chettiar:** Excellent. Then it must be conducted with great splendour.

**Priest:** If Brahma wills it, can life be shortened?

**Chettiar:** (Calling the clerk.) Give Iyer fifty rupees...

**Priest:** Add another twenty-five, please.

**Chettiar:** Will it cost so much?

**Priest:** She and I have prepared the budget together.

**Chettiar:** Then there is no appeal against it, is there?

After Iyer took seventy-five rupees and left, Chettiar strolled down the bazaar for amusement, met and spoke with friends, and went home.

## Translator's Notes

The title is rendered “Dhanapal Chettiar and Company”; “company” encompasses both the commercial concern and the network of dependants, clerks, priests, entertainers, and labourers surrounding him. Monetary entries use the source's rupee-anna-paise notation. Some OCR figures are visually ambiguous; the recovered opening ledger follows the scan, while English preserves the satirical contrast rather than silently forcing every damaged subtotal to modern decimal arithmetic. “தண்டல்” is interpreted as a collection or debt payment in context. *Panchamirtha* is the five-ingredient ritual mixture used for temple ablution and distributed as consecrated food. The title page and final page were manually transcribed from scans because OCR returned no text. Caste, class, domestic violence, debt, and gendered dialogue are translated as source-period characterization and social criticism.

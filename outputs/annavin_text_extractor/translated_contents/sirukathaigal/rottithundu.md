## English Translation

Tamil title: ரொட்டித்துண்டு

As mill owner Rao Bahadur Ranganatha Bhoopathi sorted and read the personal letters in that day's post, anger surged within him. For fifteen days he had received nothing but infuriating letters. Muttering, "Fate, fate," he glanced through them, crushed them, and threw them into the waste-paper basket.

His wife Subbuthayammal brought coffee. The Rao Bahadur's face looked ready to make sesame and mustard seeds burst. When she asked what special news the post had brought that day, he replied with disgust, "The same special news as always! You bore me a son, did you not, a brute who seeks to bring ruin upon my head!" He turned back to the letters.

Subbuthai stood there for a few seconds and left. One letter particularly caught his attention. It read:

*Sivamayam*

Pugazhendhipuram  
6 May 1950

To Rao Bahadur Ranganatha Bhoopathi, blessed in full measure by the grace of Lakshmi, from your affectionate Varagunanatha Pillai, schoolmaster and former teacher of the long-lived Bharathabhooshan.

I write this letter in a state of supreme bliss. For four days I have kept thinking that I must write to you. I first thought it improper for me to trouble you with a letter amid your many duties, but my heart will not let me remain silent.

I trust you remember what I said when you appointed me tutor to the long-lived Bharathabhooshan. The world marvels at your regal wealth, I said, while your son Bharathabhooshan, the prince of prosperity, has received the complete grace of the Goddess of Learning and will become a great genius admired by the world. Even then his intelligence astonished me. My predictions do not often fail.

Now I am immersed in an ocean of joy at the prodigious fame Bharathabhooshan has attained. Your heart too must be overflowing, and there can be no limit to the happiness of your household Lakshmi, Srimathi Subbuthayammal.

Can the divine poet's words be false: "Greater is the joy of the mother who hears her son called a worthy man than her joy when she bore him"? If I feel such pride and fulfilment merely because I was Bharathabhooshan's teacher, who could describe the magnitude of your joy?

There is no one who does not bless and praise Bharathabhooshan for writing at so young an age a work celebrated by scholars as one of the world's great literary creations. People call him a genius, a great savant, a modern Valluvar, a revolutionary writer, the Voltaire of the South, a master of prose fit to found a fourth Tamil Sangam, and an orator. When they speak so, I dance for joy. When I say that I was his teacher, they praise me too.

One daily calls the book the incomparable ideal document of the twentieth century. I myself heard a speaker call it a heroic trumpet blast that wipes away the tears of the poor and marshals them for a righteous struggle. A teacher told me it was an epic presenting all the noblest ideas of the world's greatest thinkers in a beautiful style suited to our age.

The learned world rejoices not only because Bharathabhooshan possessed the intelligence to create such an excellent epic, but because a sacred son born into wealth understood the suffering of the poor so clearly and explained so beautifully how they might be made to prosper.

Readers repeatedly savour the passage comparing the exploiter who grows fat by sucking the labour of the poor to a forest creature that smashes a hive, squeezes out its honey, and drinks it. A man asks a poor fellow reduced to a skeleton, "Why are you like this?" The poor man replies, "I gave my flesh and blood to the wolf-lord." What a splendid thought! Everyone weeps at the description of hunger and starvation turning a human being into a beast.

People wonder how Bharathabhooshan could discover and paint the poor man's heart so vividly when he lives amid the wealth of Kubera and cannot know the sting of poverty or the way it makes life writhe. A great patriot is said to have declared, "History says that although the Buddha was born a prince, he perceived human suffering, attained wisdom, and became the teacher of the world. Here Bharathabhooshan enacts that history before us."

"Without the sweat of the poor, how did you obtain the diamonds and gold sleeping beneath the earth and the pearls lying beneath the sea?

"Show me a single object made without the poor man's skill!

"If you do not bring peace into his life, he will destroy you and your world. Do not imagine that because you sit in a howdah you may torture the elephant. If the elephant grows angry, you will be pulp beneath its feet!"

Even I can scarcely believe that the ever-gentle Bharathabhooshan wrote such spark-flinging sentences.

By God's grace, may Bharathabhooshan live long and attain fame greater even than the poets of the ancient Sangams.

*A Piece of Bread* costs fifteen rupees. I am a poor schoolmaster and cannot buy and read the whole work. No one will lend it either; they guard the book like a precious treasure. Out of your affection for me, please send me a copy at once. When it arrives, my heart longs to compose a fine *akaval* praising the talent of my pupil and your son Bharathabhooshan. I wrote to him but received no reply. A great scholar immersed in research can hardly find time, so I have written to you. Please send *A Piece of Bread* immediately.

Ever mindful of your kindness,  
Varagunanatha Pillai, schoolmaster and composer of *akaval*

After finishing it, the Rao Bahadur sighed and murmured, "There can be such a creature in this world!" He opened another letter. It read:

Boothapandi  
6 May 1950

**Long live the revolution!**

You titled money-fiend Ranganatha, mill owner crazed with domination!

You destroyed countless poor men like me. You crowed that we committed a crime by saying that we laboured without receiving the fruit. You arrogantly abused the poor as no better than ants and flies. When we said the world was advancing and the revelry of capitalists would inevitably be curbed, you sneered that we were merely shouting and barking. We have not forgotten.

Bharathabhooshan says that a tiger struck by a bullet may growl and roar but will die in the end. Read it!

You vile man, you called us "coolie fellows." *A Piece of Bread* describes us as those born to rule, possessing the power both to create and destroy, and producing through labour a wealth without peer or comparison. It is no mere book; it is a bomb that will blow your fortress of fraud to dust.

The sovereigns amassed by crushing and squeezing the poor lie in sacks within mansions. Bharathabhooshan writes that one day all those coins will become bombs and reduce the mansions to mounds of earth. Know that our veins swell when we read those words. Even we could not depict the schemes, deceit, greed, and frenzy of men like you so well. Bharathabhooshan's revolutionary work has made your character perfectly clear.

You used to pretend that people like me denounced rich men like you only from envy and rowdyism. Read *A Piece of Bread*, sinner! It was written by Bharathabhooshan. Who is he? Your son! Could your son be jealous of you? No, no, three times no! Bharathabhooshan is an idealist and the comrade of the oppressed. That is why he wrote a revolutionary work and showed a new path.

Money-guarding demon! Who respects you despite your millions? The whole nation honours Bharathabhooshan. The poor worship his portrait. No trade union fails to buy *A Piece of Bread*.

Because he was born your son, Bharathabhooshan could portray the conduct of people like you exactly as it is. Understand that our burning anguish was born as your son.

Thus,  
Meghanathan, whom you dismissed from the mill

The mill owner's anger became uncontrollable. What could he do with only a letter before him? Had Meghanathan stood there, he might have strangled him. He crushed the letter and threw it down, held his head, rose, picked the letter up again, tore it to shreds, and put it in the basket. Then he began another letter:

Aalayur  
7 May 1950

Blessings to the long-lived Bharathabhooshan.

I write this with a deeply wounded heart. Even when you wished to pursue higher studies, I told you that what you had studied was enough and that you should attend properly to the mill and preserve the family's honour. You obstinately hankered after a B.A. and an M.A. You were your mother's darling, and I consented. Is this what you have done after going away to study? Should you conduct yourself so as to destroy our honour?

*A Piece of Bread*, *The Old Pot*, *The Tattered Cloth*: are these subjects for books? Should you be the one to write them? Must your learning be used, like that of dry, penniless writers beating time for their next meal, to say that the rich man ruins the poor?

Everything in your book humiliates me and incites the mob that lives on wages from me. One mad fellow writes directly to me that it is a bomb, not a book. It is natural for an educated man like you to write a work and seek fame. Write! Win praise as an intellectual! I too would be happy to hear my son praised. But what should you write? Should you write, "Gather an army of paupers to crush the arrogance of the rich"?

When I die, am I going to tie up all my money in a bundle and carry it away? Is it not all your property? Is this the quality of your education: betraying the father who begot and raised you, showered everything you wanted upon you, and now setting the town's enmity upon him? Traitor! My stomach burns.

Will you betray your own father merely so that a few men incapable of earning a living may praise you as a revolutionary writer? You revile exploiters who live in luxury. Who are you, then? Did you go out to work with a crowbar and spade in your hands the moment you were born? I paid a total of a thousand rupees merely to the woman who served as your nurse. I paid a good Tamil scholar thirty rupees a month to teach you.

Could you not display your literary ability by writing about splendid subjects such as the *Kamba Ramayanam*, *Tiruvempavai*, or Thayumanavar? Would you not still earn fame as a great writer and genius? At his advanced age, T. K. Chidambaranatha Mudaliar labours to search out and publish forgotten Tamil poems, earning the name of a great scholar. Can you not learn even from him? Did Shanmugam Chettiar, who bears the title "Sir" and moves through the world with distinction, write about cracked pots and huts like you? Fool, he wrote of the greatness of the *Silappathikaram*. Did that not bring him immense fame?

Could you not write a story like *Ponniyin Selvan*, or a work of research such as *Spear and Bow*? So many have earned fame by writing such books, while you write *A Piece of Bread* and destroy my honour. Like the man who killed a cow and donated a pair of sandals, will you abuse me and win the name of genius? Senseless fellow! What use is it if a few creatures without food praise you? Will it help in any practical matter?

Why has your mind gone astray? In every examination they gave you good marks out of consideration for my standing. How, then, did you suddenly become an intellectual? It was money, my money! And now you write a book condemning me. What arrogance!

I no longer wish even to see your face. You will not receive a single quarter-anna of my property. It is, after all, the property of a wicked man! You are a hero of righteousness born to destroy injustice; why should you want it? You will not get a single copper. I shall write it all over to Suddhananda Bharathiar.

You may decide that *A Piece of Bread* is not enough and write *Dried Fish*, *The Graveyard*, and whatever else you please, and become a revolutionary hero. Are you my son, fool?

Thus,  
Rao Bahadur

He put the letter in an envelope and wrote Bharathabhooshan's address. Then a measure of peace returned to him.

After giving the letter to be posted, he swept all the remaining mail into the basket and called, "Who is there?"

"It is I, Marudhai," said another servant as he entered.

"When did you come? Where have you come from?" the mill owner demanded.

Marudhai held out a letter. "The young master sent it, sir."

The letter read:

**Highly confidential**

Singarapuram  
6 May 1950

Dear Father,

This is Bharath writing. I have become caught in grave danger.

A student named Kathirvelu who studies with me at college had written sheaf after sheaf on a subject called *A Piece of Bread*. He was in financial difficulty and pawned his boxes. I lent him ten rupees. The manuscript of *A Piece of Bread* was inside one of those boxes.

While I was reading it, another student came in and asked who had written something so good. He is the son of Boominatha Pillai, owner of the Porkkolam Press. As a joke, I said that I had written it. It seems he told Boominatha Pillai, who gave me a cheque for one thousand rupees to publish it as a book and signed an agreement to give me half the profits.

Kathirvelu knew nothing of this. Before the book appeared, he came down with a severe fever and was in the charity hospital. After publication it received tremendous acclaim and has also earned good money. It now appears that my share alone will amount to ten thousand rupees. Moreover, because I supposedly wrote this book, I have been invited to the World Writers' Conference in Paris, with the conference paying the expenses.

In the meantime, Kathirvelu lay gravely ill. Now that he has recovered, he came and quarrelled with me. What could I do? If the truth became public, I would face disgrace. I tried every possible way to pacify him, but it was impossible. I became angry and beat him soundly. I have now arranged to have him bound and locked away somewhere.

I do not know what to do next. If it comes out that I did not write the book, the shame will be unbearable and my future life will be ruined. Therefore Kathirvelu's mouth must somehow be sealed. He has the grip of a crocodile and will not let go. It is costing me ten rupees a day merely to keep him hidden.

Please leave at once, come here, and make suitable arrangements. All the workers on our farm and in the mill who had been discontented have read *A Piece of Bread*. Trusting me as their friend, they praise me and write to me every day. If the world learns that Kathirvelu actually wrote it, my position will become terrible.

I too have considered several plans. Could we somehow resolve this difficulty through your friend Dr Thamu? Kathirvelu is forever ranting about something at college, so the teachers and students call him "loose," "crack," and "semi." If we obtain a proper certificate, we could have him admitted to a mental hospital itself.

I do not know what is best. I am frightened. That is why, regardless of the expense, I have sent this letter by our servant Marudhai. Please start at once. Tonight the New Age Writers' Association is giving me a banquet. I expect you tomorrow.

Your loving son,  
Bharathabhooshan

When the Rao Bahadur finished reading the letter, his face blossomed like a red lotus. Would not the letter he had just written become a cannon trained upon himself?

14 January 1953

## Translator's Notes

The title “ரொட்டித்துண்டு” is rendered as “A Piece of Bread.” It is both the stolen book's title and the story's emblem of hunger. Historical honorifics, caste-era titles, period currency, literary references, and the characters' stigmatizing words for mental illness are retained contextually as elements of the satire, not endorsed terminology.

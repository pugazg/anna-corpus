## English Translation

"What town is this, sir?" I asked the passenger beside me.

"A wedding? We stock dhotis in four thousand designs, saris in six thousand designs, and hundreds of thousands of upper garments.

"Periya Karuppannan Chetty and Company Textile Hall..."

The innocent man read without taking a breath. I interrupted him before he suffocated and died, thereby granting him his life through my second question.

"Sir, I asked the name of the town. You are reading an advertisement for a textile shop," I said with a gentle laugh.

Mockery and ridicule were blended into my laughter, and the man must have understood. Otherwise, there was no reason for his face to acquire the appearance of a lamp-oil address. Before he could cover his foolishness by bargaining with laughter, I began speaking again, intending to give him a little consolation.

"The entire world revolves upon the axle of advertising. Business advertisements have grown so enormous that even the name of a town cannot be known. The dynasty swollen with money performs this dance to throw dust into the townspeople's eyes. This is the age of iron cash boxes, sir. Everything merges in the light they cast.

"What can be done? It has become the age of advertising, the season of empty display and bombast."

I poured out words in a torrent. Meanwhile, my friend from the advertising board, wishing to show that he had corrected his error, said:

"Sir, this is Tiruvarur. Didn't you say you had to get down here?"

"Yes," I replied and hurried to alight. I did not really get down; the crush pushed me out.

> Rajakumari borders, Mumtaz Mahal fronts, and fashionable saris edged in the style of *Mangamma Sabatham*. Visit our Soundarya Mahal once.
>
> Periya Karuppannan Chetty and Company Textile Hall, Tiruvarur.

> Jai Hind dhotis, Swatantra Bharat upper cloths, Subhas Bose towels, and Tilakar shawls are available for modern gentlemen.
>
> Soundarya Mahal.

> Which palace of beauty contains decorative garments from the entire world? Where are every kind of celebrated textile available? At Periya Karuppannan Chettiar's establishment!

I entered the town and was startled and overwhelmed. I wondered whether the whole of Tiruvarur was merely the side wall of this textile merchant's shop. Every wall without his advertisement appeared like a woman who had lost her beauty.

Crowds filled Tiruvarur. That day Thyagaraja, the Lord who had established camp in that town, was to ride in his chariot. Thousands upon thousands had assembled to see the form overflowing with beauty.

I too entered the temple.

His advertisements were there as well. Periya Karuppannan Chettiar had arranged a showroom on one side. Just as people worshipped the Lord, they gazed upon and "venerated" the silk pitambarams he had stacked there. Their mouths watered, their tongues watered, and their minds moved.

As wealthy men returned home, their children would plan an assault the next day for silk shirts. Young women would walk away vowing, "Let him come today. By smiling or shedding tears, I will somehow make him buy that eye-catching sari with the Mohini colour and glittering border."

All that merchandise became familiar to everyone there.

"Long live Chettiar!" young men with hawk-like gazes praised the textile-shop magnate, for they had obtained the vision of maidens whose sidelong glances sounded in the raga Pantuvarali.

Chettiar did not stop there. He went a step further. For the chariot festival, he allocated five thousand rupees for two temple events: a vocal concert and a dance recital.

For the music, he arranged the cinema-famous queen of grand music, Mohanangi, the cuckoo of amorous conversation. For the dance, he decided to bring Leela and Suseela, known as the Twin-Hill Princesses and possessors of no fewer than seven or eight lengthy titles.

He made another peculiar agreement with them. They had to stay with him for a week before the recital and entertain visitors daily in the showroom from five until ten in the evening. He did not say it so crudely. He conveyed it in a delicate manner and brought them accordingly.

The event bore Thyagaraja's name, but the true festival belonged to Periya Karuppar. The whole crowd was there every day. Would the people look at the temple statue without first looking at the singing cuckoo and the dancing peacock?

In the morning, the chariot swayed and rolled. Amid the display of the police force, with the cooperation of excited young men running in every direction and the hereditary path lending its assistance, they turned and manoeuvred it somehow. The chariot moved and continued moving.

Then, what a wonder! There was no pit, hollow, or other obstruction, yet the moving chariot stopped.

They pulled and pulled but could not move it. They released the ropes. The people began dispersing.

I understood nothing. I wanted to ask someone why the chariot had stopped. Looking around, I could not forget the palace of light that struck my eyes. It was Soundarya Mahal, proclaimed by the advertisements covering every wall in town. The chariot had stopped without apparent cause precisely when it reached that establishment.

A man moved toward the side. I approached him.

"Sir, why has the chariot stopped?" I asked politely.

"It is the divine will. This clothing shop belongs to a devotee. The sacred services he has rendered Thyagesar are beyond telling. When the Lord reached his shop, he himself stopped," the man said.

I experienced a faint coolness, as though beneath a bodhi tree. Before enlightenment became complete, I asked a young man with a tender moustache the same question, just as I had asked the devotee.

He stared at me.

"That is the skill of money at work. All of it is humbug. They say Thyagesar stopped! This priestly crowd has taken money from the Chettiar. The wedges, supports, and lever beams are all under the control of the men performing the worship. It is all business technique, sir.

"If the chariot remains here for a week, a crowd gathers every day. A crowd means advertisement upon advertisement and business upon business for them."

Having fired off this hot explanation, he went on his way. I watched the direction he took until he dissolved into the crowd.

I floated along in the mass. Such a crowd! Who allowed anyone to walk? As I was carried forward, I forced my eyes to graze upon the enormous posters.

Everywhere were advertisements for the music and dance recitals. In them, the textile magnate's picture and name appeared at tremendous size. Why? I did not understand. The townspeople did not understand. Perhaps you do.

He played no accompanying instrument and knew nothing of a dance master's work. Yet his name appeared.

By six in the evening, two whole Tiruvarurs had begun laying siege to the temple. Perhaps there was some agreement with bus operator Paramakandiar. Though the concert had been advertised for six, they announced it would begin at ten. At once, the people climbed into every available bus and rushed away. They ate and returned within an hour.

The vocal concert took place. A few listened; many thousands watched and became ecstatic. Then came the dance. The people swarmed around the stage like flies.

Equality between men and women achieved one hundred percent victory there.

Song and dance filled the temple.

The temple clock struck one.

Several people came running in haste, sweat pouring from their faces like rain.

"Ayyo! Theft! Burglary! A terrible robbery!" they cried.

That was enough. The people scattered.

Wanting the full details, I gathered the whispered, ear-to-ear radio reports being murmured everywhere. This was the news I discovered:

Thieves had entered Soundarya Mahal. With the entire town's attention directed toward one place, they had easily carried out the daring burglary. They swept away expensive varieties of textile. They took a cash box containing fifty thousand rupees and burned all the account books that were to be shown the following week for income-tax purposes. No one yet knew what else had disappeared.

Chettiar immediately flew toward the shop in his car.

From the moment I entered the town, my thoughts had belonged to that "Sriman." His advertisements had seized them. What could I do?

His excessively loud advertising had tormented me. Some indescribable feeling made me hate an important man I had never previously known.

I saw him in person only at the concert. His disguise as a devotee did not fit him well. His form reminded me of the rhinoceros in a zoological garden. The ash coating his body recalled a giraffe painted with whitewash. His voice resembled a wartime air-raid siren. Thus my dislike grew without diminishing.

I wished to learn everything about him and gathered information carefully.

I walked toward the shop district and saw the same young man I had encountered earlier. I stopped him at once.

"Brother, will you wait a moment?" I asked.

"Why?" he replied.

"One matter," I said.

"Do not lengthen the story. Ask what you need," he said impatiently.

"Brother, Periya Karuppannan Chettiar lives in this town, doesn't he? I hear his shop was robbed," I said without any proper connection.

"Are you new to town, or are you a C.I.D. officer? He is a thieving fellow. What thief could steal from him? Periya Karuppar is the Malayala Bhagavati: if deceived, he will swallow the man whole."

"Brother, why speak so angrily? Tell me patiently, and let us hear it."

"You tell me: what anger would burglars have against account books? The moment people say the thieves burned them, isn't it clear this is a fraud?

"Income tax assessed this man at more than twenty-one thousand rupees. He staged this drama according to plan in order to make the account books disappear. It is Chettiar's trick for swallowing the income tax.

"A Chettiar's son is clever. Once before, in a black-market case, he performed every possible dance and wore every possible disguise, and the plan succeeded. This is the same story again."

Still speaking, the impatient young man walked away.

"Brother, whatever you say and however much truth you reveal, you must say it with some respect. That alone is becoming in young men like you," I advised.

Whether the words fell into his ears, I do not know. He had already gone.

But the words he spoke while walking still rang in my ears.

"A mere broom becomes a ceremonial tassel when tied to a handle... A great devotee!"

That was what he said as he went.

I too walked toward the railway station, repeating those words.

"A devout, thoroughgoing thief."

My mind said that this title alone suited the textile-shop magnate. Without my knowledge, my head nodded its agreement.

At that moment, the bell rang to announce the approaching train.

11 June 1950

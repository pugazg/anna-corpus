## English Translation

Lawyer Venugopalachariyar was a puzzle to the whole town. He was impossible to understand. Do not take this to mean that his speech was exceptionally confused. Rather, one could not determine precisely what kind of man he was from his words, deeds, thoughts, and circumstances.

Was Venugopalachariyar talented or incompetent? It was impossible to say.

At the Bar Club, the lawyers’ association, they praised him: “Venu is a clever man. A point that occurs to no one else comes easily to him.”

But Sankaramurthi, the lawyer’s clerk, complained: “Of all the men I could have found, I got Venugopalachariyar. Who comes looking for him? Has he won some spectacular case and become famous? Why would a client seek out this useless lawyer?”

He read law books until two in the morning, underlined passages in coloured pencil, and took notes. Surely those were the actions of a capable man.

But look over there. Clients follow every lawyer like devotees following a god, and the lawyers hurry from one court to another. Venugopalachariyar, meanwhile, leisurely explains to the club’s Iyer the difference between Ganesh Coffee Powder and Lotus Coffee Powder.

He truly was a puzzle.

Even whether lawyer Venugopalachariyar was rich or poor remained an incomprehensible matter.

“He has gone to the village and will return in four days,” Sankaramurthi tells the rare client who has appeared like the proverbial fig flower.

“To the village? Why?” the client asks.

The clerk answers angrily: “Why? Do you imagine the bundles you bring him alone put food upon his plate? Do you think he owns no land? He is a great landlord in Ariyanur village, sir, a prominent man.”

The client has mortgaged two acres and come seeking a lawyer who will not demand a large fee for the resulting case. When told that Venugopalachariyar is a prominent village landlord, he feels both fear and respect.

Perhaps the lawyer takes little interest in legal work because he is a landlord, the client thinks. Otherwise, with his ability, clients would swarm around him as flies swarm over a jackfruit.

Listen to what Arumugam, the village munsif of Ariyanur, says.

“Why has Venugopalachari come and pitched camp here without even attending to his court work? What is special?” asks Kandappan, who creates quarrels by shifting field boundaries and is the terror of that district.

“What life is there for him over there? He merely eats at ten or eleven in the morning, goes to court, and returns in the evening. Is he some great barrister? Only his mouth aches. What does he earn from it? It does not even cover his coffee expenses.

“Here he can shout: ‘Hey, boy! How is the crop? Why have you not removed the weeds? Groundnuts would yield a good income.’ Only by shouting at everyone does the gentleman fill his hands with money. What is there in court? Lawyers receive only fees there. This is wet straw that keeps producing,” mocks the village munsif.

But if one heard Venugopalachariyar speak to his tenant, one would feel pity for him. He was so distressed.

“Look here. When I came last month, I told you everything that needed saying. I run between home and court attending to other people’s disputes. I entrusted all the land to you because I believed you were honest and bound by truth.

“I must attend there to the quarrels and lawsuits that befall the villagers, and I have no time to look after my own affairs. Therefore, act according to dharma and look after the land with the thought that you too must live comfortably and that I must not be ruined.

“Do not imagine the income from this land is sufficient for my family. To tell the truth, I bring even the money clients give me there and put it here into bullocks, manure, and ploughing.”

The extent of the land looked impressive: sixty acres. Yet he said even its modest land tax could not be met. It was no lie. Only sixteen acres in total were cultivated. The rest would become fit for cultivation only after money was invested and the ground improved.

Most of the land became a flooded waste when rain came and dry ground when it did not. Neither condition suited cultivation, but sixty acres suited Venugopalachariyar perfectly.

Every aspect of him was similarly puzzling.

Anyone seeing him read law books after midnight would suppose he had abundant court work. For a while, even his wife thought so.

When she saw the law books, she felt not only happiness but some pride. Her father had given all of them to him.

When Venugopal, then an F.L. lawyer, first went to meet Sundarachariyar, he saw law books stacked in rows inside a cabinet. At that moment Sundarachariyar was issuing orders in the kitchen, and one or two of the cabinet’s glass panels were absent. Venugopal could therefore remove and inspect the books.

“There is only one daughter and no son,” he had earlier heard his mother say. At first he had indifferently wondered what was so remarkable about that. After seeing the law books in the cabinet, he revised his opinion and rejoiced. He decided he must marry into that household.

His desire was fulfilled. Echamma arrived with all those books. Before she could even return to her mother’s house, Sundarachariyar died. Without even waiting for the mourning period to end, the mother too came to live with her daughter.

Because they were books from her own home, Echamma took pride in them. Later she complained: “What use is having so many? He keeps reading, but there is no result.” That was only natural.

Not only townspeople but even the courts knew the lawyer’s subtle ability.

“In a similar case, in 1926...” Venugopalar would begin, and give excellent legal references and fine points of argument.

What benefit did he obtain by saying them? The lawyer conducting the case benefited. Venugopalar received a pat upon the back that evening at the lawyers’ association.

The victorious lawyer would pat him and say: “Many thanks, Venu. That point you mentioned, I struck with it once and the Sub-Judge was stunned. We won the case.”

Such was his life.

The slogans began to reach Venugopalachariyar’s ears:

“Land to the tiller! Abolish landlordism!”

Venugopalachariyar mocked them:

“Land to the tiller! The cart to the driver! Pots and pans to the potter! Cloth and garments to the weaver! The house to the builder!”

His mockery provided a delicious feast at the Bar Club that day.

“A man ploughs and we pay him for it. Here we provide a legal explanation, and they pay us a fee. Suppose I said, ‘Did this case not succeed because of me? You obtained a bungalow and garden worth six lakhs of rupees. Should they not be given to me?’ They would call me a madman.

“Yet people say land belongs to the man who ploughs it, and those speakers are called reformers!” he scoffed.

Venugopalachariyar had categorically called the slogan “land to the tiller” the empty shot of vote hunters. Yet on one visit to the village, a situation arose that stirred conflict even in him.

“Why, men, do none of you raise any ‘Down with it!’ slogans?” Venugopalar jokingly asked a few village cultivators.

“Why only us, sir? The whole country now says that everything deserving abolition must be abolished,” the cultivators replied.

“Oh! Has it reached the point where they call it the voice of the country?” he thought with alarm.

“Now look, Swami...” an elderly farmer began.

Venugopalachari immediately interrupted him. “Father, do not call me Swami. You must not say Swami. It diminishes your self-respect.”

Mockery sounded in his voice.

“Ah, he too understands the matter,” the old man said with a laugh. “Swami, it has become a habit. It is a mistake.”

“Did a man called Ponnambalam come here and hold a meeting?” the lawyer asked angrily.

“Yes,” the old man replied briefly. Pointing to the man beside him, he added, “This fellow brought him for the wedding.”

The lawyer understood. A Self-Respect marriage had taken place. A storm had blown through. He realised that argument would no longer work.

“The land will indeed go to the tiller. Even now, is it not already with him? I may merely look repeatedly at the title deed and boast that it is my land. But who enjoys it at all times? Is it I?”

“Yes, sir. It is our land while we plough, cultivate, remove weeds, and wait until the grain ripens,” said a young man with a budding moustache.

Everyone laughed. The young man’s mischief danced beside the elder.

At the lawyers’ association, Venu described this scene in detail. As he spoke, the stomachs of lawyers who had purchased fields with fees earned from murder cases felt as though tamarind were dissolving inside them.

Was Venugopalachariyar not an expert? He said: “These creatures shout without knowing the law. The Indian Constitution protects property. There is no need to fear.”

Then he argued several imaginary cases and won every one.

* * *

“Venu! You would have seen *The Hindu* this morning. What is the important news? Tell me. I had no time. The Nettur estate-holder was taking my life this morning,” criminal lawyer Tirthanachariyar gave Venugopalachariyar work.

“You have not seen *The Hindu*? You must see it. It is essential. A wonderful matter has appeared,” Venugopalar began by way of introduction.

“An editorial?” asked an M.A., B.L.

“No, fool! A photograph has appeared. All of us have been confusing our brains asking for a remedy to the land problem. Look at the picture in *The Hindu*, and the remedy becomes brilliantly clear.”

After crossing many chapters, Venu came to the point.

“There is a photograph of our Rajaji appearing to plough.”

“So what?” asked an inexperienced man.

“Was that not for a scheme to increase production?” another asked.

“No, no! What fools you are. They say land belongs to the tiller, and you become frightened. Why fear? If they say land belongs to the tiller, you too demonstrate that you plough. What is so difficult about it?

“Our Rajaji had that photograph taken as though he were ploughing precisely to give us wisdom and show us this remedy,” Venu explained.

The others dismissed his words as humour. He made them his saving doctrine and became a farmer.

Do not imagine this means he moved to the village and stayed there. He visited frequently, held a plough, and was photographed. Then he stayed in the village house.

“What is wrong with saying land belongs to the tiller? I am a tiller, and I possess land,” he began to say.

He spoke at sixty-three public meetings.

He read works about the dignity of labour and the greatness of agriculture, then poured their contents before the people.

The government appointed a committee to study farming methods in many countries and discover ways to develop agriculture. Venugopalachariyar, B.A., B.L., became one of its members.

There would be a two-year world tour.

The renowned lawyer Venugopalachariyar, described as a man with agricultural experience and a supporter of the revolutionary scheme that land belongs to the tiller, was to travel abroad to learn methods for developing our country’s agriculture.

At the send-off venue alone stood one hundred motorcars.

Judge Sabesayyar presided.

The principal part of the celebration was Miss Gulebagavali’s “Harvest Dance.”

Miss Gulebagavali was none other than Andal, the beloved daughter of lawyer Venugopalachariyar.

“They say the gentleman is going around the whole world to learn the greatness of agriculture,” the Ariyanur cultivators said as they removed weeds.

“Harvest! Harvest! Harvest!

A joyous harvest!

A wondrous harvest!

The harvest our ruling scholars

behold each day

and delight in!”

A splendid dance was performed to this song.

Pandu, a member of the accompanying orchestra, hurriedly got down from a rickshaw and went inside.

Did the man who pulled the rickshaw know that the ceremony inside celebrated the greatness of agriculture? What did he know of Venugopalachariyar setting out around the world to understand agriculture’s glory and discover new methods?

He stared in bewilderment. There were so many beautiful motorcars, adorned women, rich gentlemen, and fashionable young women. At the entrance to the hall hung a painting of a plough and bullock.

He had seen ploughs and bullocks beside huts and near rubbish heaps. Here were so many motorcars, and a plough and bullock.

It astonished him.

There he found an “elder brother,” a man selling fried peas to the drivers.

“Brother, what is all this commotion?”

“Do not loudly shout ‘brother, brother,’ man. They will not buy the peas.”

“This is for our Iyer. You know him, do you not?”

“Who, Venugopalachari?”

“The same. He is going abroad, man.”

“Abroad? Why?”

“They say he is going to study agriculture.”

“Brother, are you joking? We remained in agriculture generation after generation until our stomachs shrank, then ran here seeking a livelihood.”

“Yes. You pull a cart, and I conduct this trade. Agriculture did not give us food. It drove us from our village and made us struggle like dogs in search of food. But look, agriculture gives the Iyer a world tour. They say the government is giving money in heaps.”

“To develop agriculture?”

“What injustice, brother! Here, men who know agriculture as intimately as the pain in their flesh, who know how to form a furrow and level a field, are starving. Those who do not know these things feast upon jackfruit segments, and the government gives them an award. Fine justice, brother.”

A tremendous burst of applause came from inside.

The drivers became active. The rickshaw puller stood aside.

The motorcars departed.

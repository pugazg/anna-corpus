## English Translation

"Where did you see my caste, my beloved? You say that in my eyes you saw some light that melted your heart. You call my lips the fruit of the ivy gourd. You fondly call me coral-mouth, pearl-teeth, golden-skinned one, graceful walker, lovely voice, Komalavalli.

"At such moments, did my caste appear anywhere before your eyes? My beauty, and still more my youth, struck your eyes. Where did you see my caste?

"You loved me. I was caught in that snare. You had read many things, so you spoke of your love with richness. I am not greatly educated, and so I did not explain how much I loved you.

"Yes, I did not speak, but did my gaze not explain it? How hungrily my eyes looked upon you. How quickly that hunger vanished when I saw you. How my heart trembled when I witnessed the love you showed me. How violently my breast beat.

"Your smile was a flower garden to me. Your speech was sugar candy. Your touch plunged me into an ocean of delight.

"Apple of my eye, who gave me the taste called love! You give me death along with love. The arms that embraced me will now embrace only my corpse, and that too only if the townspeople permit it. You who lifted me onto the bed and laughed merrily must now lower me into a grave and scrape earth over me.

"Through you, I saw the world. Through you, I lose it.

"Yours,  
the woman who remained your lover until the end,  
now dead,  
Thangam."

Ponnambalam could not even finish reading the letter properly. Tears danced in his eyes. Sweat covered his forehead, and his heart pounded. The young man lost control, collapsed with the chair, and fainted.

He had been married that very day. The bride came from a prominent family and possessed some beauty, but much greater wealth. Her family had offered to pay for his education overseas.

Their plan was that Ponnambalam, B.A., should become Mr Ponnambalam, I.C.S., and that Kadambam, daughter of Kandasamy Pillai, should become a Collector's wife.

The bride's family knew of Ponnambalam's affection for a schoolteacher named Thangam. They knew Thangam was more beautiful than Kadambam. There had been a time when they feared that whatever anyone said, Ponnambalam would marry only Thangam.

Thangam was not of their caste. She was a helpless woman. Someone had given birth to her, and someone else had raised her. Somehow, she had entered a hostel for poor girls, studied, and become a teacher. For some reason, the Lord had given her such beauty, beauty capable of enchanting even Ponnambalam, whose own nature was said to be like gold.

Ponnambalam was the only son of the late Thandavaraya Pillai, a mirasdar. Their house was like a public rest house for the entire town.

While studying, Ponnambalam had kept away from the pranks of youth, not because he lacked feeling or opportunity, but because he feared losing the respect the townspeople placed in him. Even when he saw a scene that could enchant the mind, he would recite, "Women who wear saris must not be trusted," and continue on his way.

It was during his B.A. course that Thangam came to teach at the local school. Somehow, the two happened to meet. That day, for some reason, Ponnambalam's usual song did not arise. He looked at Thangam and saw her eyes. A peculiar intoxication of feeling came over him.

Thangam went on her way in fear. She knew who Ponnambalam was and worried about what the townspeople might say. The women teachers at the school had already grown jealous of her.

"Thangam is a show-off. Look at her hair, how crooked it is!" one teacher would say.

"Not crooked, Komalam. Curls. Modern fashion!" another woman would reply.

In truth, it was neither an artificial curl nor fashion. Thangam's naturally grown hair was dark, glossy, curling, and thick. Other women stood before mirrors and combed until their hands hurt, but that curled beauty did not come to them. Their burning jealousy made them gossip.

Thangam knew all this. She also knew that she had no money to make her beauty bloom and no high caste to introduce her into society. She therefore lived somewhat apart. The school paid her thirty rupees a month. An old woman named Muniyamma attended to her household affairs.

By Thangam's ill fortune, perhaps, it was this same Muniyamma who had carried and raised Ponnambalam when he was a child. Thangam learned about him through her.

"Madam, I am a woman of caste," Thangam would say.

"For whom? Ponnambalam?" the old woman would ask teasingly.

Hearing it, Thangam would smile and playfully strike Muniyamma's toothless mouth.

After meeting once, the two met many times. Smiles travelled from there to here and from here to there. Their eyes performed their duties without omission. Love ripened.

Where there is desire, will a path not arise? Thangam's beauty showed Ponnambalam the way. The two became veena and melody: joy without shore, pleasure without boundary, happiness without equal. Are Thangam and gold not one? The two became one.

But there was a town, and its business was gossip.

"Sir, our Ponnambalam often walks along the street where that woman teacher lives," the gossip began.

It ended with, "Apparently he says he will listen to no one. He will not touch another woman with the hand that embraced Thangam. They say she is even pregnant!"

In truth, Ponnambalam's resolve was that strong. Having settled in the world of love, the two decided never to part. Their love bore fruit. Thangam became pregnant. In one more month, the result of their love, the embodied form of their joy, was to be born.

It was then that Kandasamy Pillai arrived offering his daughter to Ponnambalam. He was Ponnambalam's maternal uncle and the senior male authority in the family.

Ponnambalam said many things, glared, and protested with pride. He even told him that Thangam was pregnant. Kandasamy Pillai listened to none of it.

"What happened has happened. I thought you were a very good boy. Somehow that woman has bewitched you. Let it go. I will give her one thousand rupees and transfer her to a school in the next town," he said.

"Impossible," Ponnambalam replied.

His uncle twisted his moustache, gave a hard laugh, and extended a letter. When Ponnambalam took and read it, his face darkened. A terrible trembling seized him. His tongue would not rise to speak. He stared at his uncle.

"What do you say? You agree, don't you?" his uncle asked.

Caught in danger, Ponnambalam nodded yes.

The wedding took place. That same day, Thangam gave birth to a girl. The child shone like a cat's-eye jewel born from Thangam's womb.

Thangam wrote a letter and sent it to Ponnambalam through Muniyamma. It was the letter reproduced above. After reading it, Ponnambalam fainted and fell.

The old woman who had delivered the letter returned and found Thangam hanging dead. A single length of rope had destroyed Thangam's beauty, youth, and life. The ruined rope had no eyes with which to see her beauty and youth.

Alas, the young woman died. She travelled the path of love and fell into the pit called love.

"Ladies and gentlemen! Why have we assembled in this town today? We must properly enforce the law prohibiting prostitution. I ask the public, prominent citizens, and educated people to form a committee and perform their duty."

Applause followed.

"Applause alone is not enough. We must demonstrate it in action," Collector Ponnambalam declared elegantly.

Every man in a zari-bordered turban at the meeting sat with hands already joined, prepared to clap whenever the Collector looked in his direction.

This occurred twenty years later.

In the meantime, Ponnambalam had gone overseas, qualified for the I.C.S., served as Collector in several places, acquired a little grey hair, and arrived as Collector of Thanjavur. A committee of city dignitaries was established to enforce the prostitution law. Collector Ponnambalam presided over the meeting and delivered this speech.

Many spoke about the harms caused by prostitution and the dangers it created for health.

It was quite certain that many of them would that very night tell their own mistresses about the evil of prostitution and the solemn vow they had taken to abolish it.

After many had spoken, a young woman rose. She was beautiful and also appeared somewhat impetuous. Her walk and clothing showed that she was educated in English and lived in reasonably good circumstances.

"Mr Chairman! Elders! Prostitution cannot be abolished unless we examine why it arose. Otherwise, only the place where it occurs can be changed.

"Poverty is the first cause of prostitution, but do not suppose it is the only one. Desire for money too drives people into prostitution. Along with it, the arrogant misconduct of men is the very cause that produces prostitution.

"When I say this, the Chairman may become angrier than anyone else. Yes, sir. Do not stare rigidly at me.

"If marriages of love occurred, couples were properly matched, and caste, religion, or some other barrier did not obstruct love, prostitution would not truly grow in this country.

"As long as arranging a marriage resembles catching cattle, people cannot experience complete happiness in life. Men and women who have never tasted the joy of love roll in the mud of lust. Prostitution is the thorn bush that grows from that mud.

"Many people terrified by the demon of caste refused to marry those suited to them, ruined their lives, and turned them into prostitutes.

"Mr Chairman, the daughter of your Thangam became such a prostitute. What could that helpless woman do? She was the daughter of Thangam, whom you loved but refused to marry, reducing her to a corpse.

"While you went overseas to learn the art of governing the country, Thangam's daughter was raised by a prostitute. She learned dance and song. A prominent man loves her and lives with her, but refuses to marry her. He wants pleasure but not responsibility.

"Even if the committee you establish today works with great speed, it cannot touch her. Why? The man keeping that woman, Sundari, as his mistress is Thirumalaisamy, chairman of the committee you have founded. Therefore, she will not be caught.

"You seem to remember that earlier day now. Frightened by the question of Thangam's caste, you listened to someone's words, surrendered, and turned her into a corpse. Yes, today you speak words like gold!"

The woman spoke with anger.

"Madam, do not kill me. I am a sinner. I lost my Thangam. But I did not fear caste. I feared only the terror my uncle showed me," the Collector said.

"I know that too. Thangam was a Christian woman. Your father had written a will stating that if you married her, you would receive not even a quarter anna of the family property. Your uncle threatened you with that, did he not? The instant you learned the property would be lost, you weakened. Did Thangam not appear to you as wealth?" the woman asked.

"Lady, do not kill me again. That day, I was caught by the demon of caste. Through it, I lost my happiness," the Collector said.

Then he asked, "Who are you, who knows so much about Thangam and me?"

"I am Sundari," the young woman replied.

"Ah, my daughter! Jewel given by Thangam! Your mother gave me happiness. You have given me wisdom," Collector Ponnambalam said, calling his daughter near and seating her beside him.

The following week, Collector Ponnambalam conducted a Self-Respect marriage between Miss Sundari and Mr Thirumalaisamy.

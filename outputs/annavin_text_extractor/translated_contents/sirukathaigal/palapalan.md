## English Translation

"The Sun's gaze will remain severe for fifteen days. Only then will this fever subside."

"He radiates heat, sir. One can feel it merely by sitting beside him."

"Of course he does! It will be intense. Such is the Sun's gaze. What can be done about that? Still, there is no danger. His life is not at risk. If Saturn were not where he is now but had moved into the eighth house... Good heavens!"

"Why are you sighing like that, sir?"

"Kamalamma! When I first saw your husband's horoscope, I tell you honestly, I was frightened. Why? The Sun's gaze was so severe. Then I asked where Saturn stood, for that is what matters. So I examined Saturn. Fortunately, through the strength of your marriage bond, he is not in the eighth house. Then my mind became peaceful. There will be no threat to life."

"Goodness, sir! Your words sound as though they have come from the mouth of Lord Venkatesa himself."

"Am I the one speaking, foolish woman? I merely tell you what the planets say. The important thing is to determine the effects produced as each planet moves. Nowadays people who know nothing of planetary transit use fortune boards, ask trained birds, and practise every kind of fraud to make money. Am I like that?

"I examine where Saturn stands, what Mars is doing, what Mercury says, and what Jupiter will bring. I study each planet's position, calculate, and tell you with precision, just as three and three make six and six plus four make ten.

"I have examined your husband's horoscope carefully. His fever is high because of the severity of the Sun's gaze, but Saturn's present position is not especially bad. Therefore his life is certainly not in danger. The fever will gradually fall. Do not fear. In any event, it would be good to keep a ceremonial lamp burning for one mandalam for the Sun and Lord Saturn.

"It is all calculation. It is not a matter without cause. Just as turmeric and lime combine to turn blood-red, effects arise from the relationships the planets form with one another."

After explaining planetary relationships, effects, severity of gaze, and planetary transit, astrologer Kuppaiyar counted the coins Kamalammal gave him, tied them up, and went home.

How could Kamalammal avoid believing that Aiyar's calculation would prove true? Had he not drawn lines this way and that upon a board, made boxes, placed a planet within each box, calculated, and then spoken?

She did not understand Saturn in the eighth house, the position of Mars, or the Sun's gaze. But Aiyar had explained it through things she could understand: three and three make six, and turmeric mixed with lime turns blood-red. This gave her firm confidence that his words could not be false.

For reasons she did not know, however, the slip selected by a fortune-telling bird had said the exact opposite of Aiyar. This worried and even frightened her. The paper selected by the bird read:

"Through Saturn's anger and his movement to a higher position, the cold-fever will rise and change by command of the Sun."

The bird blamed Saturn, while Kuppaiyar placed the blame upon the Sun. Kamalammal placed the burden upon God and sat at her husband's feet.

Chinnappan suffered an intermittent fever. At times he seemed at death's door. At other times one could say, "It is not too bad. Clarity has returned to his face, and the bitterness in his mouth has lessened a little."

Yet the good wishes of friends and Kuppaiyar's planetary calculations gave Chinnappan neither satisfaction nor confidence. This did not mean that he had no belief in astrology. He observed fasts and worship without the slightest omission. But he believed Kuppaiyar's calculation was wrong.

And was that the only calculation? Here was another:

Six in the evening: 101.

Eight at night: 103.

Ten at night: 102.

Eight in the morning: 101.

Twelve noon: 104.

Three in the afternoon: 103.

Four in the afternoon: 102.

Every day, Doctor Sudarsan had these figures measured and examined them to see at which times the fever rose by how many degrees and when it fell.

Using this account, Doctor Sudarsan explained that the fever began in the morning, grew throughout the day, appeared to decline in the evening, rose again at night, and then gradually subsided. He placed all these details under some medical name and described them according to medical science. His method of calculation was not wrong either.

But Chinnappan felt that the doctor's calculation did not explain his illness. Doctor Sudarsan enjoyed an excellent reputation in the town.

Chinnappan firmly concluded that neither the astrological calculation nor the medical calculation explained his disease. In a low voice he would often say:

"What will come of any of this? What if the Sun and Saturn enter the same cage? What if one swallows the other? What if the fever rises in the morning and falls in the evening? What connection do these calculations have with my illness? None of this amounts to anything."

"They say the price of land is falling. Is it true?" Seth Ramlal asked his shop manager.

Manager Madhusanan answered briefly, "Yes."

"How much does Chinnappan owe us?" the Seth asked.

The manager gave him a note showing Chinnappan's outstanding balance.

After reading it, the Seth asked, "What price is an acre of ryotwari land fetching now?"

"Less than two thousand," the manager replied.

"Even if we take it as two thousand, he owns only four and a half acres," said the Seth wearily.

"Yes. It would not fetch even ten thousand," the manager said ominously.

The Seth looked once more at the manager's account slip. The Sun's gaze, Saturn's change of place, and the thermometer were all present upon that scrap of paper, though not in detail. They were condensed into one figure: 16,000.

The Seth looked at the manager. The manager looked at the Seth.

Neither astrologer nor doctor had seen this calculation. But it remained constantly before Chinnappan's eyes.

Aiyar quarrelled with the Sun, but what could the blazing orb do? The indebted heart had caught fire.

Anxiety over the debt account was the cause of Chinnappan's fever. He knew it. The astrologer and doctor who worked to discover the cause did not. They kept asking the Sun and the thermometer to show them the reason.

Kamalammal said that, as the astrologer advised, they should keep a lamp burning for one mandalam.

"Madness, Kamalu! None of this amounts to anything," Chinnappan said, and would not permit the lamp to be lit.

"The chief medicine for this fever is sweet-lime juice. The more of it he drinks, the better," said the doctor.

"You are mad, Kamalu! That doctor is even madder. None of this amounts to anything. Without knowing the matter, each of you makes a different false calculation. The Sun is looking, they say. Saturn is moving, they say. Madness! What will come of any of it?" Chinnappan said, refusing to drink the fruit juice.

One day the fever suddenly began to fall. It declined rapidly and then disappeared entirely. The bitterness in his mouth vanished in an astonishing way. Every trace of illness left Chinnappan's face.

This happened before the fifteen days that Kuppaiyar said the Sun's severe gaze would require, and without Chinnappan drinking the sweet-lime juice prescribed by the doctor.

A new astrologer and a new physician had caused this astonishing change.

"The Suttuppatti zamindar is looking for land near our town," a friend who had come to inquire about his health told Chinnappan.

"The Suttuppatti man?" Chinnappan asked, still somewhat weary.

"He apparently intends to complete a purchase even at five or six thousand an acre," the friend said.

"Then dispose of our four and a half acres," the patient replied.

"It is all one contiguous plot, isn't it?" the friend asked.

"Yes, one plot. Good irrigation, near the river, two harvests. It yields gold," Chinnappan declared.

"Why would the Suttuppatti man dislike that? Keep quiet and leave it to me. I will close it for thirty thousand," his friend promised.

He did exactly that. Chinnappan paid off the Seth's debt and deposited the remainder in the bank. In the passbook brought by the bank peon, a new account appeared: Chinnappan, current account, 14,000-0-0.

That new calculation produced greater benefit than sweet-lime juice could. It completed in a single stroke every remedy involving the Sun's severity, Saturn's change of place, and the ceremonial lamp meant to appease them.

"Kuppaiyar examined it and predicted the recovery from the very beginning," the townspeople said.

"Doctor Sudarsan measured the fever hour by hour, watched him with the greatest care, and gave medicine. He recovered and has grown plump as his old self," the town also said.

"However much harm the Sun's gaze may do, danger can never arise so long as Saturn does not enter the eighth house. You know what happened with our Chinnappan, don't you?" Kuppaiyar boasted.

Chinnappan did not contradict them. But he often said privately to his wife, "None of this amounts to anything, Kamalam."

He knew the truth. No effects had arisen from the Sun's gaze. The Seth's gaze had made the fever rise, and the Suttuppatti man's gaze had made it fall.

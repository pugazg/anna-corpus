## English Translation

No grammarian said that her name, Sundari - "the beautiful one" - had become a descriptive name. Why would anyone need to say it? Do not countless people lift a flower and enjoy its fragrance without exclaiming, "Ah, what perfume! What delight!" From Sundari's earliest childhood, all who saw her beauty rejoiced. By the time she went to the shopping street and returned home, her cheeks had received many kisses and her hands many kinds of treats.

Her teacher was often angry because that doll paid no attention to the lesson. He knew enough words to call such a pupil a fool, a thick-headed donkey, and to threaten a beating. Yet not once did he say what he intended. He merely shook his head with a slight smile. A little fear would appear upon Sundari's face and give it a new loveliness; the teacher admired it. He pulled another pupil's ear, pinched another's cheek, and knocked another's head, but hurled no hot word at Sundari, fearing that the flower might wilt.

Her childhood admirers did not say, "When the little girl grows up, she will be a great beauty. Her eyes alone are enough to bewitch a man. I have never seen a smile like hers. And that thick, dark, curling hair waving in the wind - what grace and elegance!" But there was no one who did not think all this and more.

Her mother, Dhanabagyavathi, did not tell her, "Sundari, we are poor. You cannot have clothes of every kind. I cook and sell appam, and two lives must survive upon that income. Your father has wandered away somewhere. What can we do, my dear? The pickle with your rice must serve as your fried dish, curry, and vadai." Sundari already knew it.

Her green blouse and areca-coloured skirt suited her sovereign-gold complexion. What need had she of Bombay silk or imported voile? Dhanabagyam did not say that such cosmetics and finery were needed by weeping-faced Annam, pop-eyed Ponni, and bald-headed Vanitha. Did an appam seller possess the right to mock rich men's daughters?

Sundari's growth and the maturing poverty of the household competed with one another. Amid their commotion, her beauty continued to grow, caring for neither.

Dhanabagyam did not say, "If only I had a little property and a house of my own, I could find a husband worthy of my gold and arrange her marriage. Anyone would eagerly shake my hand. But Sundari is the appam seller's daughter. Must she become the wife of some brute, drunkard, or nameless fellow?" Her tears further soiled a pillow already thick with dust.

Logu Mudali did not say, "She is beautiful, modest, and somewhat educated. She is poor, but what of it? Merely let her move about our house and she will be its Lakshmi. What a fitting bride for our boy! But why must she have been born to the appam seller? Could she not have been born to the tahsildar's wife in the next street, who has circled the sacred fig tree for six years without result and sits weeping? Then we could marry her to our son."

He thought such things many times but never voiced them. Logu Mudali was wealthy, a widower, and the father of one handsome son. That son was infatuated with Sundari.

Logu Mudali never said, "Kanagu, why do you visit that appam shop so often? They say you love the girl Sundari." Convention forbade such talk with one's son.

Kanagasabesan did not say, "Father, unless I pass that shop at least once a day, I find no happiness. Do you know how Sundari's beauty torments me? I cannot sleep; and if I happen to sleep, I dream. That parrot-voiced woman appears in every dream. Even if my life leaves me, I swear I will marry no one but Sundari. I want Sundari; otherwise I want no marriage." Was it proper for a son to address his father that way?

Did Sundari speak what was in her mind? She did not. She never told her mother, "Whenever Kanagu sees me, he smiles in a particular way. When he asks for water and I hand him the cup, he deliberately touches me. He says, where I can hear, that he will marry only me." Why would she need to say it? Dhanabagyam too had once been a young woman like Sundari.

"Sundari, I will certainly marry you. Do not think I will deceive you. Do not imagine my family will reject you because you come from a poor household. Just watch: somehow I shall bring them around and marry you."

"You marry me? Your father owns sixty velis of land, while I am the appam seller's daughter. How could you marry me? It can never happen. Why try to deceive me for nothing?"

"Sundari, I swear it. Trust me."

"A vow? Desire cannot see earth or sky, and so you speak. Do not ruin me with such idle words. I am poor."

"Even if a skull-splitting battle takes place in our house, I have decided to marry only you."

"That is a castle in the mind; it will collapse. They say the Iluppapatti mirasdar's daughter has a face like a monkey that has bitten ginger. What of it? She is the one destined to become your lady."

"Ugh! Would I marry that monkey?"

These were things Kanagu did not say to Sundari, and Sundari did not say them to Kanagu.

Some time later, Kanagu became the son-in-law of the Iluppapatti mirasdar after all.

He did not tell himself, "What a fool I was to marry you when you look like a monkey! What if Father threatened me? Had I remained stubborn, how could this wedding have occurred? I too consented only because accursed greed for money seized me. I have lost Sundari, pure as refined gold, and must now weep with you. My fate! What can fate do? My own intelligence deserves a beating with a stick." But that did not mean he failed to think it.

What could the poor fellow do? All sixty velis of land had been mortgaged to the Iluppapatti man. Only after fastening his weeping-faced daughter around Kanagu's neck did the mirasdar agree to declare the principal and interest discharged. Kanagu thought of the marriage as punishment for the debt. Could he say so?

Thiruvengadam, wandering from place to place, did not say, "I have a wife and a daughter. My daughter is fair and attractive. Somehow my wife suffers and struggles to raise her. I cannot work and cannot look after my home. I can roam from town to town and fly after half a bellyful of gruel, but dislike living at home. I wear saffron robes, but if I find a counterfeit key, I obtain a fine opportunity for intoxication. That is my sole satisfaction in life." Yet those were his thoughts.

No one told Thiruvengadam, who wandered between towns, rested in monasteries, and lived contentedly during chariot festivals, that Sundari had reached womanhood, that Kanagu loved her, or that family pressure had forced him to abandon that love.

No one, including Kanagu, told his wife Kaveri, the Iluppapatti man's daughter, of his love for the appam seller's daughter. How could one speak of it to such a wealthy woman?

Nor did anyone tell Dhanabagyam that Sundari had sometimes behaved according to Kanagu's desire.

Dhanabagyam often wondered, "Why does this girl behave so familiarly with him?" She said nothing. She did not warn Sundari, "Girl, be careful. If he says anything improper, do not listen. Threaten to tell your mother." Had Kanagu spoken words Sundari considered bad, would she not have told her mother herself? What he said had delighted her.

Sundari did not need to say, "Mother, my mouth turns sick. I dislike rice, feel dizzy, and my body feels strange." Dhanabagyam understood.

She did not rebuke her daughter: "Alas, disaster has come! That fellow has unjustly ruined my innocent child. What shall I do? Because she was poor, nobody sought her for a good household. If this too has happened, what will become of me? Accursed girl, you have poured fire into my belly! Were you so arrogant and swollen with pride?" She feared that if the matter emerged, catastrophe would follow. But her sighs and freely flowing tears explained her agony to Sundari more clearly than those words could have done.

Dhanabagyam did not tell Kanagu, "Brother, the town calls you a noble man. Everyone praises your good nature. You show compassion to the poor and save those hurt by the Iluppapatti mirasdar's arrogance. Yet a man as good as you has ruined my entire family. Look at Sundari's suffering. The medicine some sinner gave her did not dissolve it; the trouble you caused keeps growing. The town abuses her, and her tears torture me. You married another woman, perhaps from desire for money or fear of your father. This helpless girl trusted your loving words. Could you not accept her too, even as a second wife, and remove the disgrace?"

Many times she thought of trying to say it. Then she yearned in silence, wondering how to speak and what benefit speech could bring.

Nor could she bring herself to say, "Mother, an unmarried girl is pregnant and we fled in fear of the town's abuse. Protect us." Dhanabagyam decided only that they must leave the town. She took Sundari elsewhere and settled there.

In Sundari's tenth month, Dhanabagyam did not tell the old midwife who attended her the unfortunate girl's secret history. She lied that Sundari's husband had run away overseas. There was a tali around Sundari's neck - one tied by mother to daughter.

Sundari gave birth to a child like a golden image. Unaware that a grandson had been born, Logu Mudali performed an elaborate worship at the Perumal temple.

There had been no wedding, yet a baby crawled in the hut. In the mansion Kanagu and Kaveri placed fruitless faith in ritual worship by Kanapadigals, an amulet from a devotee of Kali, and red medicine from a Siddha physician.

To whom could Kanagu say, "My son Rajagopal exists; what worry have I?" How could he say it when he did not even know? Even had he known, could he have spoken?

Many people asked Sundari with weary surprise, "The man who left has never returned?" Since he had shown her no concern, she had no choice but to make the excuse that he remained on the other shore. Time did not stop out of concern for the woman who suffered with tears continually streaming from her eyes. It ran on. One year, two, three, and five years passed.

An unmarried maiden wearing a tali moved through the world. Thangarajan reached the toddling age without seeing his father. His mother caressed him as "my gold," and his grandmother as "my king." The two endearments joined and gave him the name Thangarajan.

During those five years both "kingdom" and "gold" belonging to Thangarajan's father steadily diminished. The Iluppapatti mirasdar had a lawsuit that travelled from the munsif's court to the High Court. Its expenses pledged his gold jewellery to a Marwari and major portions of his land to another zamindar.

The mirasdar did not tell Kanagu, "The very hour you became my son-in-law, Saturn seized me. Do not show me your face. Merely seeing you fills me with rage."

Kanagu did not tell him, "Will your ruin stop with this? You have much more to suffer. How many poor people have shed tears because of your arrogance of wealth? You receive its fruit. You grow angry with me, but your outrages created enemies throughout the town. When the suit arose, those enemies worked to finish you. Suffer; what is it to me? You ruined me, and through me that woman was ruined and disappeared somewhere. The sight of your face makes me seek an axe or sickle."

Father-in-law and son-in-law never said these things. Whenever they looked at one another, their eyes expressed them.

The case was lost and misery became complete. The mirasdar did not say, "It is as though Iluppapatti itself has changed hands." What else did his stomach pain, vomiting, and fever declare?

Kanagu had dreamed of living grandly as a rich man's son-in-law. Instead he received responsibility for preserving a ruined landed family - like a man who went to bathe and smeared himself with mud.

No one told the wandering Thiruvengadam of his family's condition. Who cared? One day he lovingly entertained a mendicant. No one told him that the mendicant was a millionaire in disguise.

Thiruvengadam had never learned from anyone the grand philosophy that food received by oneself should be used to relieve another's hunger. That day he was intoxicated. He had abundant rice and no hunger. Seeing a traveller collapsed on the road, he lifted him, seated him, and cared for him.

The traveller was Arumainayagam Pillai, who had roamed many towns dressed as a mendicant and gained worldly knowledge by witnessing the hypocrisies of charitable men and the sins of supposedly virtuous people. He had several lakhs deposited in a bank and no family.

Seeing that this wandering ascetic possessed a compassionate heart, Arumainayagam decided to give him all his property. He did not announce that intention. He simply asked Thiruvengadam to accompany him.

Singing the verse, "The entire country is our home when we have a begging bowl in hand," Thiruvengadam agreed. The two wandered through many towns and finally reached Aatrur, Arumainayagam's home.

No one told Thiruvengadam that his family was living in this unfamiliar town. Who knew? While roaming about Aatrur, he saw Dhanabagyam. Astonished, he went to the hut and saw Sundari and Thangarajan. He danced with blazing joy. Little by little he learned what had happened, and his heart throbbed.

"Where is that sinner? I will break his neck!"

"Do not. He is very rich. The world will speak on his side."

"Does a king appear more than a splinter to an ascetic? Is he a human being? Did he see this golden statue? Why should I fear because he is rich? Bah! I have watched many rich men become wandering beggars. Why, Arumainayagam here in this very town was once a mendicant; I know it. Money, indeed! What can money do?"

Thiruvengadam did not say such things to his wife. Or rather, he thought many of them but his mouth would not speak.

Arumainayagam learned the condition of Thiruvengadam's family. He rejoiced that he possessed the power to save a destitute and disgraced household. The hut became empty.

Thangarajan spent part of his time upon his elder grandfather's lap and part upon the younger grandfather's shoulder, dancing and shouting. Sundari watched with delight; the next moment tears filled her eyes. When she had been sunk in poverty, the constant labour needed to stay alive had left her no time to grieve. Once Arumainayagam's support removed the struggle for survival, grief became an incurable disease.

The Iluppapatti man died. Kanagu's father followed him. Kanagu's wife fell victim to tuberculosis. Kanagu boarded a ship for Burma to work as a shop's accounts clerk. The lie that Sundari's husband lived overseas became true.

Thangarajan reached the age of ten. Nobody told him his mother's sorrow. He did not ask why his father was abroad or how long he would remain there. He believed his father had quarrelled with his mother for some reason and left.

The proprietor of the shop where Kanagu worked was cruel. Unable to endure his blows, Kanagu left the far shore for his homeland, deciding it was better to live as a starving mendicant. Nobody had told him that Sundari lived in Aatrur. He came because he heard that a man named Arumainayagam owned a mill there and might offer him work.

Arumainayagam listened to Kanagu's history. He did not say, "So you are that man! Fool, go and ask forgiveness from the woman you rejected."

Instead he said, "Sit down, brother, sit down. I have waited many days to see you. Are you Kanagu, son-in-law of the Iluppapatti mirasdar? Good, good. Sit down."

Kanagu stared in astonishment.

"Thangam! Raja! Brother Thangaraj!" Arumainayagam called.

"Grandfather, here I am!" the boy shouted as he ran in.

"Your father has arrived," Arumainayagam said.

Kanagu recoiled. The boy's eyes widened; he stood open-mouthed, an indescribable emotion upon his face. The next moment he cried, "Father!" sprang forward, and clasped Kanagu's neck. Kanagu understood nothing.

He did not ask, "Sir, what is this? I came seeking work. Who is this boy? You call him my son, and he too calls me father. What marvel is this?" When Arumainayagam saw the alarm in his eyes and smiled, Kanagu understood that the older man already knew he wished to ask precisely that.

The next instant Thangam bounded away.

"Mother, Mother! Father has come! Run, come quickly!" he shouted, racing into the house to find her.

Father, Father, Father had come. How sweet those words sounded in his own ears! For many days he had whispered to himself where nobody could hear, "Father is not here. Father is not here. Father has not come. Father has still not come." Today he shouted, "Father has come!" and leapt like a deer.

Seeing Thangarajan's excitement, Sundari feared he had gone mad. The moment he found her, he seized her hand and dragged her towards the room where her husband waited. She reached the doorway.

"Sundari!" Kanagu cried from within.

Neither could tell who first rushed forward. They met in an ecstatic embrace, unaware that an elderly man and a little boy stood there. Tears surged from both pairs of eyes.

"Sundari! Sundari! My dear Sundari!" Kanagu sobbed. He held her face between both hands and gazed at it, wiped the tears rolling down her cheeks, and moved to embrace her again. Then he saw the tali at her neck and drew back in fear. A faint smile appeared on Sundari's face.

"Thangam, bring your younger grandfather," she said.

The boy ran out. Arumainayagam followed him and ordered that no one approach the room for half an hour.

Sundari finished telling Kanagu her history. Kanagu told her his. He kissed the tali she wore.

"Sundari's husband has returned from overseas," the people of Aatrur said. Nobody told them the subtle parts of Sundari's sorrowful history. The tali her mother had tied said, "Her husband who went to the far shore has returned; they say he will now remain on this shore." Who knew that the tali upon her neck had not been tied by him? Who told them what he had done years before?

Sundari would say, "Thangam, do not imagine your... father is a very good man. Be careful. He is terribly, terribly bad. Do you know what he did? Shall I tell you?"

Kanagu pleaded, "Sundari, Sundari, do not say it. You will earn a crore blessings. Do not tell him that one thing."

Thangam cried, "Mother, tell me! Tell me! Father, do not close Mother's mouth. Mother, tell me!"

"Do you know what your father did? One day..."

"Ah! My lower stomach hurts! Oh, I cannot bear it! Brother, run and bring me some water," Kanagu groaned.

When the boy ran out, Kanagu clasped Sundari in his arms.

"Sundari, my dear, do not tell him. Please, do not say it, my love."

"You flirted with me just like this before leaving me to suffer."

"And I have undergone transportation for life as punishment!"

"Alas, poor man! You suffered so much because of me."

From outside the room came Thangam's voice: "Father! Water!"

"Let go; he will come in," Sundari said.

The boy entered. "Father, has the pain gone?"

"Oh, and the fright has gone too," Sundari replied.

"Are you a tiger, Mother, for Father to be frightened of you? But you keep saying you will tell me and then remain silent. Tell me!"

This delightful drama took place often. But Sundari never once told Thangarajan the story of Kanagu bathing in the Kaveri. How could she?

## Translator's Notes

The title and the repeated phrase “சொல்லவில்லை” are rendered through recurring forms of “did not say” and “was not told.” Silence is the story’s governing narrative device.

“தாலி” is retained because the marriage pendant is central to the plot: Sundari’s mother ties it to conceal an unmarried pregnancy, and Kanagu later accepts and kisses it.

The final phrase “காவேரியில் ஸ்நானம் செய்த கனகனின் கதை” is deliberately playful and indirect. It points back to Kanagu’s history with both Sundari and his wife Kaveri without spelling out to the child the adult sexual and marital history that remains untold.

## English Translation

Do you know “Benefactor” Ulaganatham Pillai?

You do not? Then one may say with certainty that you have no connection whatever with a bhajan hall, Pillaiyar temple, primary school, reading room, drama company, charitable water stall or anything of that sort. For you would surely have met Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai at one of those places. Every day he would be present at some such venue: to conduct an opening ceremony, preside over a function, deliver the vote of thanks, or, if none of those was possible, attend as a “special guest.”

Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai was not his given name. “Benefactor” was a title, an honorific. Ulaganathar was his father’s name. The name his father had given him was another one, a name probably unknown to most townspeople: Chinnakulanthai, “Little Child.”

That name did not sound quite right. His father, Benefactor Ulaganathar, would call him affectionately, his mouth full of love: “Little Child!” It even sounded sweet when his father said it. When anyone else called him Little Child, it somehow sounded repulsive. Chinnakulanthai therefore wished to change his name.

Choosing another name and waiting for it to come into use would take time. Suppose the new name failed to give pleasure; could he change it yet again? No, no. He wanted no such dangerous experiment.

Then Chinnakulanthai reached a decision. The name Benefactor Ulaganathar had already become famous. Even those who called him Chinnakulanthai introduced him by saying, “Do you know who he is? He is the blessed son of the late Benefactor Ulaganathar.” Why lose the influence attached to that name?

He could call himself Chinnakulanthai, son of Benefactor Ulaganathar. But why take a circuitous route? He would adopt it briefly and directly. “From now on my name is Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai,” he decided, meaning the son of that Benefactor Ulaganathar.

He announced that he should be addressed in that fashion, and people began to do so.

Chinnakulanthai had read in Western histories that eight kings had borne the name Edward: Edward the First, the Second, the Third and so on. The same was true of the names George and Henry. He considered following that custom and adding a number to his name: Benefactor Ulaganathar the Second.

But kings had chiefly employed that system. People might misunderstand if he used it too. He restrained his desire and decided it was enough for the name Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai to become established.

It was not enough merely to attach the honorific “Benefactor,” as his father had done. He felt he ought genuinely to render some assistance, in whatever manner and measure he could. He did undertake certain deeds believing he was helping. Somehow, however, the result was always the exact opposite of what he intended. His action ended in harm to the very people he meant to assist.

Only afterwards would he wring his hands.

He had never deliberately wished anyone ill, but his deeds always concluded in injury. He himself felt distressed and even ashamed. Many times he wondered why this happened, but he could never understand it.

One day the Anbu Drama Association invited him to preside over a performance. Even when the company manager came in person, Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai did not immediately agree.

“I shall be back,” he said. He went outside and examined the sky carefully in all four directions, looking for clouds or signs of rain. Suppose rain chose the very day he presided. Would the blame not fall upon his head? The entire town would say, “Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai came to preside, rain poured down and the company suffered a great loss.” The drama company too would resent it. Why earn a bad name?

That was why he checked for rain. The sky was perfectly clear. There was not even a cool breeze to be had as medicine. Rain certainly would not come. Only then did he consent to preside. These days one had to exercise that degree of caution.

The play was neither a complete failure nor outstanding. From what he saw, excluding the time he slept, it seemed passable. A woman playing a fortune-telling Kurathi made his belly shake with laughter. The takings could not be called satisfactory.

Near the end of the play, Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai was led to the stage. On the way he saw drain water collected to one side of the shed and became angry.

“Is a place where gods, the divine trinity and emperors who rule nations walk about to stink like this? Shame! It should not be so. Is this filth not disgusting?”

“Tell them, sir. Tell them so it sinks in. I have been saying it for ten days, but will they listen?” someone interjected.

Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai was delighted that a man had voluntarily come forward to support his opinion. The company manager, however, appeared not to like the conversation.

“Water does not really collect there very much. If we wash it twice with phenyl, there will be no smell,” the manager said in defence of the drain.

Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai would not yield.

“Absolutely not! So celebrated a company must not remain beside such a drain,” he declared. His intention was less to condemn the drain than to delight the manager by praising the company’s stature.

“Why hesitate any longer, Manager? Do not object. Find another place tomorrow itself,” said the man who had supported the Benefactor.

“Yes. Abandon this place altogether,” the Benefactor insisted again.

Neither Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai nor his supporter relented until the manager promised to vacate within a week.

Who was the supporter? The owner of the ground on which the theatre shed stood. Because the rent was not paid properly, he had been struggling to evict the drama company somehow. A circus company was due the following month, and he hoped it would pay a much higher rent.

The drama company had survived only by securing recommendations from various people. That was one reason it had invited Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai to preside: he could be asked to intercede with the landowner. Had the matter been explained at the beginning, he would indeed have recommended that the company be allowed to remain, and the landowner could not easily have disregarded his word.

What happened instead? Exactly the opposite. Even the landowner might not have pressed so fiercely. Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai himself insisted with the greatest intensity that the company must leave and move elsewhere.

The unintended occurred. Thinking he was helping the drama company, the Benefactor condemned the drain. His intervention left the company without a place to stay.

Could its members avoid saying, “Without knowing better, we invited this ‘benefaction’ to preside, and it came and dropped a stone upon our own heads”? It was a calamity brought about while attempting to do good.

Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai discovered only later that he had unwittingly harmed the company. At the time he felt thoroughly satisfied, imagining he had argued on its behalf.

His presidential address followed the same pattern: what he intended was one thing, what occurred another.

He had heard that only the actor who played the “Rajapart,” the royal lead, was worthwhile, while all the others were rubbish. People said this was why the plays were poor and the company earned little.

He resolved to remove this defect and secure both a good name and income for the company. He composed his address accordingly.

“The entire town says this company’s Rajapart acts very well,” said the Benefactor.

Applause rose through the shed. The Rajapart was pleased.

Then came the dangerous word “but.”

“But I reject that opinion. Is the Rajapart the only one? If you ask me, the woman who played the fortune-telling Kurathi acted magnificently. I laughed until my belly shook.”

How would the Rajapart feel? His face reddened with anger. His eyes had already turned red.

He assumed the Benefactor was babbling out of resentment because he had asked for nine complimentary first-class tickets and the company had sent only six. That was not the reason. Moreover, Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai had not requested the nine tickets; his wife had sent the request without his knowledge.

“Some people bring this company a bad name by saying only the Rajapart acts well. I condemn that strongly. Is there any actor in any company equal to the woman who played the Kurathi here? The Rajapart wears ornate costume, carries a sword, puts a crown upon his head and walks with a royal gait. What wonder is there in that? Anyone wearing that costume will walk so. Many can act as a king. But ask them to play a Kurathi! Can everyone produce that sway, shake and grace?”

At that moment the loudspeaker failed. Its operator came running to repair it. The Rajapart signalled with his eyes, and the manager stopped the man. The loudspeaker remained dead. Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai tried speaking for another minute or two, then said both “greetings” and “blessings” and sat down. Only afterwards did the manager permit the operator to repair it.

The Rajapart came forward to give the vote of thanks. He was extremely angry, naturally, but controlled himself as he spoke.

“Some people asked me what assistance Benefactor Pillai actually renders,” he began.

Mocking laughter filled the shed.

“Today you have seen and enjoyed the fact that Benefactor Pillai renders assistance freely and abundantly to whoever needs it. Even if people like him do not praise Rajaparts like me, a Rajapart remains a Rajapart. That is why Benefactor Pillai lifted up and praised the supporting actors whom other chairmen neglect.

“I was supremely delighted to hear him elevate our company’s Gundamma, that is, the Kurathi. Supporting actors will gain enthusiasm and advance only when they are praised. They will never forget the service our Benefactor Pillai has rendered them. As a sign of this, our company’s Gundamma, the Kurathi, will garland him with a rose garland, a very large rose garland.”

The Rajapart won enormous applause with this speech. Gundamma came forward with a bashful, twisting gait and placed the garland upon him. The entire shed roared with laughter.

“If even the supporting actors perform so wonderfully, need anything be said of the Rajapart’s talent? Friends should not imagine this grove contains only a single tree. They should understand that everyone in the company acts skilfully and come in crowds every day to give their support.”

That was the final part of Benefactor Pillai’s speech. The first part had already gone crooked; where was the opportunity for the rest to proceed properly? The loudspeaker had failed, so hardly anyone heard him clearly. What reached their ears was merely that the Rajapart’s performance was nothing remarkable and the Kurathi had acted with splendid shaking, glitter and grace.

Would the company obtain a good name, support or income from that?

Yet he had spoken only from a desire to help it. As usual, the result went completely the other way.

Hoping the newspaper would publish his entire address, he bought it the next evening and read it. The report irritated him:

> ANBU DRAMA ASSOCIATION’S RAJAPART BELOW STANDARD
>
> KURATHI DANCER’S REVELRY WORTH SEEING
>
> BENEFACTOR PILLAI SPEAKS THE BLUNT TRUTH

The headlines and report stunned him. The paper made it appear that he had disparaged the drama association disgracefully. He suffered at the thought of how distressed the poor company members must be.

As though that were not enough, the newspaper printed a short editorial praising him:

> The practice of squandering the public’s money through revelry in the name of art deserves strong condemnation. Benefactor Pillai had the courage to raise this matter while presiding over a play itself. We request that the public at least henceforth heed his excellent advice and stop burning its money by attending useless dramas.
>
> When masqueraders who strut like kings for a quarter measure of rice and Kurathi women who shake and glitter for one handful of gruel roam every street, must people pay to enter a theatre shed to see such sights? This, Benefactor Pillai asks, was the substance of his presidential address.

Upon reading it, Benefactor Pillai was truly shaken. He grieved that his effort to help had taken such a perverse turn. He had spoken in expectation that his address would gather support for the company. There had not been the slightest evil thought or intrigue in his heart. But the result? His presidency had blessed the company with a bad name. What could he do? He sat bewildered.

“Someone has come to see you, sir...” the servant drawled, scratching his head with a mischievous smile.

“What is it? Tell me.”

“A lady has come to see you. That girl... the one who dressed as the Kurathi.”

“An actress? To see me? Why? Very well, ask her to come.”

The servant went to fetch her. A thought occurred to Benefactor Pillai: a crowd might gather to see an actress. Why invite needless commotion? He told the servant to close the street-facing windows of the room. Mischief danced in the man’s eyes.

“Once the girl enters, shall I shut the street door too?”

“No. You remain at the entrance and let no one inside.”

“I shall see that not even a crow gets in,” the servant said and went out.

“Come, come! Why, even now you look as though you are in costume. You are the Kurathi, are you not?”

“I am, sir. I had no livelihood and joined this company hoping I might advance.”

“You will advance very well. Did I not speak you up yesterday?”

“What did you speak? You set fire to my head!”

“Your head? What are you saying, girl? I praised your art.”

“And what did I receive from it? Did the proprietor or the Rajapart give me a gold medal?”

“You mean they did not give you a medal? They will. Give it a little time.”

“I want neither a medal nor an ornament, sir. It is enough if I have a livelihood.”

“You will earn thousands upon thousands. Just see.”

“You speak without knowing my condition. Why did you raise me up and praise me yesterday? What happened because of it? They said: ‘Go, go! Benefactor Pillai declared that your Kurathi role alone gives life to the entire play, and that no one else can match your shake, glitter, grace and flourish. We must not prevent such an actress from growing. You need not work here any longer. Perform that Kurathi dance throughout the town; people will shower sovereigns upon you. Go!’ Then they drove me away.

“Because of you, the livelihood by which I survived is gone. Could you not have come, spoken four good words about the Rajapart and left quietly? You dragged me into the speech and threw dirt into my stomach. Will my suffering leave you untouched?”

“You have no work? Did my speech cause this harm? It pains me terribly to hear it. I spoke believing I was doing good, and now it has ended this way.”

“What am I to do, sir? I have no other path. The proprietor mocks me: ‘He is a great connoisseur of art, the man who praised you. Go ask him. He will start an entirely new drama company for you.’”

“Did he say so? I praised you only to do good.”

“Please, sir! The other actresses mock and disgrace me too. They ask why an important man of the town, for no reason at all, would single out a woman playing a Kurathi and praise her. They have even tied a rope between us.”

“Tied a rope? What does that mean? I do not understand.”

“Oh, stop playing. Do you not know even that? They say there is something between you and me.”

“Siva! Siva! What talk is this?”

“I beat my head when I heard it, while all of them laughed and laughed.”

Benefactor Ulaganatham Pillai could continue the conversation no further. His head seemed to spin. He opened his desk, took out twenty rupees, gave it to Gundamma and asked her to go.

Outside, the servant stood watching Gundamma with a mischievous look.

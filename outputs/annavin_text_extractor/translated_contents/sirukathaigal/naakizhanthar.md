## English Translation

“Lord of charity! It has been four days since I ate. My eyes are dim, my arms and legs are failing, my ears are closing up and I feel faint. A single mouthful would keep me alive.”

Did that lord of charity have time to hear the poor man’s pitiful plea? He had so many troubles and so much urgent business. If this beggar’s bowl stood in his way, would he neglect his affairs and stop to entertain its owner? Look there. See how anxiously he gets into his car. Do you hear him slam the door? Such was his anger.

Do you know where he was going? He had suffered a great loss and was hurrying to deal with it. Sonagiri, who owed him money, had agreed to execute a sale deed for fifteen acres and an old house against a debt of five thousand rupees, principal and interest together. The lord had consented. He had rejoiced at the thought of improving the land until it fetched a thousand rupees an acre, installing a pump to irrigate it and doing all manner of things.

Then a rival appeared. Another money-box stepped in and offered the debtor seven thousand rupees. The debtor grew difficult. The lord of charity was rushing over to settle him by offering another hundred or two. Without understanding how many troubles, hardships and losses beset him at such a moment, that starving vagabond asked for a mouthful of food. Tell me, would the lord not grow angry?

“Stout fellows! Why can they not go somewhere and work for wages? Why do they not fall into a pond and die?” the lord said.

“Can so great a rich man not give one handful of rice? O God! You created him in that condition and abandoned me in this one,” the wanderer complained to the Lord.

It was eight at night, under the darkness of a new moon. A conference of destitute wanderers assembled on the veranda of a ruined rest house. A dry-headed man opened the conference.

“Do these men possess even a little compassion? Any charitable thought? Any pity when poor people die this way? We roam nine leagues for one meal. We bow to everyone we see as though he were God, bare our teeth in supplication, fall at their feet and cry out our suffering. They chase us away calling us fools, brutes, thieves, scoundrels and idiots. Not one of them thinks, ‘Alas, the poor man,’ feels compassion and gives charity. O Isvara! Why did you give these men so much wealth, turn us into paupers and leave us to die in a rest house? Have you no eyes?”

The chairman of the conference was a man with chest pain.

“Yes! If Isvara had eyes, why would he leave me in this condition? I may be a pauper, but even now I gather flowers, make garlands, bathe and decorate the Pillaiyar beneath the banyan tree, perform squats before him and pray. I show devotion according to my strength. Has the god poured anything into my hands? Away with it! Even a god smiles, plays and talks only when the caller is rich. If we call, will it ever enter his ear? He has neither eyes nor a mouth with which to speak.”

The pauper who had been humiliated by the rich man that morning delivered his conclusion:

“There is no use folding our hands before mere human beings and praising them as lord and virtuous benefactor. Praising God will bear fruit. We must worship his name and sing it. If he opens his eyes and looks upon us, will not our hardship vanish like air in a second?”

A beggar who conducted his trade disguised as a northern sadhu cried “Victory to Sitaram!”, majestically blew out a cloud of ganja smoke and offered the vote of thanks. The conference dispersed, and they went to sleep.

* * *

Thereafter, in accordance with his resolution, the pauper began going before temples and chanting, “Govinda! Mukunda! Gopala! Raghurama! Panduranga! Protector of devotees! Supreme abode!”

Do you imagine the “devotees” who came to the temples for peace respected the poor man’s bhajan? Their own hearts seethed with countless troubles. They had come to complain to the Lord, seek remedies and find peace for their agitated minds. Would they stop at the gateway to hear the grievances of a man shouting God’s names as though his intestines would burst out, and then help him? Did they not need to go inside, perform a hundred thousand-name worship and praise the Lord as helper in danger and protector of the orphaned?

Did the Lord say, “My devotees, you are making all manner of complaints and requests here. The man crying at the gateway asks neither for the eight forms of wealth nor for enormous business profits. He asks only for enough gruel to half-fill his stomach. I shall settle his need and then come to you”? No.

The Lord stood astonished, too amazed to open his mouth. His body remained frozen in wonder.

“Ah! How beautifully this false actor worships me! In the name of ablution and adoration he collects money, draws the curtain, splashes water and hurriedly scrubs the idol. If his hand aches, he curses me and stamps upon me. Is there any limit to his outrage?

“At night, the offerings devotees gave for me reach his mistresses. And look at this man in broad sectarian marks, tulsi beads and a five-fold dhoti, dressed as a devotee and singing, ‘A body like a great green mountain...’ He forged a signature only this morning and now melts here in supplication. Ah, this deceitful woman has not passed a day without cheating her husband, yet how pure and devout she appears here!

“Such people praise me as the one who knows everything, while assuming I do not know their evil deeds. They worship and try to cheat me. What courage these devotees possess!”

The Lord stood stunned at the natures of those appearing before him.

The pauper continued circling and worshipping the temple by day and evening, loudly singing bhajans. Surely, he thought, his voice could not fail to reach the Lord. Every night at the rest house, every mendicant mocked him. At last he grew weary and reached a terrible decision. When he announced it, the others clapped and jeered, unaware of how firm he was.

“My tongue has grown calloused from praising human beings like ourselves as gods and showering them with blessings. We thought there was no benefit in worshipping men and turned to worship Mahesvara. Thinking he could not withhold compassion from his own child, we gave our tongues not a moment’s rest and sang his litany of names. Yet he did not come forward to relieve our misery.

“Either the ears that fail to hear my cry of pain must be cut off, or this tongue that worships in vain must be cut out and thrown away. I cannot cut off another person’s ears. The Lord’s ears are black stone: they will not be cut but broken, and that would be difficult. It is easier to cut out my own tongue.

“Why do I need it? Do I sing music? Song requires joy. Am I going to issue commands such as, ‘Hey! Where is the motor driver? Call him’? Am I going to discuss accounts and say, ‘The profit this year was only fifty thousand’? Will I speak joyfully with a dear wife or fondle my children with words?

“That is not the work of my tongue. Whenever a head appears before me, it says, ‘Lord of charity!’ and begs for alms; at the temple steps it cries ‘Govinda!’ Why must I possess a tongue merely for this?”

He resolved to cut it out and cast it upon the temple steps. When he said so, the mendicants never imagined for an instant that he would actually do it. When they heard that he had cut out his tongue, they were so shocked that they failed even to notice their begging bowls fall.

“Alas, the poor man! We thought he spoke in jest, but he truly cut out his tongue,” they lamented.

* * *

“A wonder of the Kali age! A bombshell for atheists and a bouquet for believers! Those who howl that there is no God and slander the devout may witness a rare miracle this evening before the Gopalaswami temple. The saint called Pancha Bhaktar, overcome with divine ecstasy, will cut out his tongue and offer it to the Lord. Let them behold the greatness of devotion. Believers are requested to come in groups, receive the saint’s darshan and obtain divine grace.

“Thus issued by Kodai Govindasami, trustee of the Gopalaswami temple.”

Thousands of these handbills were scattered throughout the town. Crowds gathered in every street. Everyone eagerly asked who this great devotee might be.

A world filled with fraud and disguise! A world encircled by intrigue and deceit, where arrogance and injustice perform their dance.

Religion gives delusion and bewilderment. This intoxicant was prepared by schemers to deceive the poor and simple. Brokers and sophists dug this pit of destruction for their own benefit.

He hides within the fortress called money, torments and tortures the labourer, takes the almanac as his ally and bows at the ruler’s feet. His name is the rich man.

He is a coward who cannot resist and a simpleton without discernment. His name is the poor man.

A hall in which people gather, worship and groan is called a temple. It is also the gateway through which the poor enter, gain nothing and come out. The one who beholds injustice, grows afraid and stands insensible is called God.

The pauper spoke according to this new dictionary. He went to the mansion of the rich man who had refused him food and driven away angrily in his car. Like a man possessed, he hurled abuse. The servants seized him and bound him to a post.

“Bring the whip!” ordered the rich man.

The pauper laughed without fear.

“Can your ears hear now? Splendid! I suffered all these days without discovering this. When I worshipped and praised you, your hearing was dull. Now I have cursed you from the depth of my heart and it has reached your ear, has it not?

“Good. Take the whip and beat me until your arm aches. Can a whip torment me more than hunger? Your hand will not have enough strength to beat until your anger subsides. Find some brute for the work. You will catch another poor man; make him beat me and pay him wages for it.”

The pauper spoke without fear. The rich man wondered whether he was mad. He sent the servants outside and questioned him. The poor man described his painful life and his plan to cut out his tongue.

The rich man remained silent for a few seconds. Then he reached a decision, untied the bonds and led the pauper to the upper floor of the mansion. The household was astonished. The pauper himself was astonished.

He was served a six-flavoured feast, given clothes and allowed to recover from his exhaustion. Then the rich man seated him nearby and asked, “Do you truly intend to cut out your tongue?”

“I do. What use is it to me? With it, I can praise men like you and abuse them when misery comes. What else can I do? So I have resolved to lose it.”

The rich man’s face shone with happiness.

“I can collect at least a hundred thousand rupees within a year! I can establish a magnificent monastery! All our influence can be put to use,” he told himself.

Then he addressed the pauper. “Very well. Instead of doing this deed and receiving no benefit, I shall arrange it so that it yields good results. If you follow my instructions, I shall make a way for you to live comfortably from now on.”

The pauper agreed. The handbills immediately spread across the town.

On the appointed day, he cut out his tongue. Before thousands of people, the rich man fell at his feet and pleaded, “Devotee, I beg you to accept this humble servant as your disciple.”

The pauper nodded. After skilled medical treatment, he took the sacred name Pancha Bhaktar and lived in the rich man’s mansion. The town gathered to offer donations, and the rich man devoted himself to the service of collecting them. By becoming the disciple of the Tongueless One, he earned an income no other trade could provide.

The pauper grieved as he watched the townspeople worship him as a noble soul, a great devotee and a divine incarnation; wait every day to behold the stump of his severed tongue; and heap money, milk and fruit at his feet. How readily the world yielded to deceit! His heart trembled when he saw how cunningly the rich man used him to amass wealth.

Losing his tongue had brought him regular meals, an ashram in which to stay, silk to wear and servants. But the man making the Tongueless One dance collected tens of thousands of rupees.

“What wickedness!” he thought, boiling with anger. What could the poor man do? He had no tongue with which to speak. The other mendicants were deluded too.

“Would such good fortune have come to him upon losing his tongue without divine grace?” they asked, and began falling at his feet.

Apart from the rich man, everyone regarded him as an incarnation. The pauper’s anger grew so great that he decided death was better than remaining alive as an accomplice to the rich man’s fraud. One midnight, overcome by mental anguish, he took a sharp knife and killed himself.

The rich man rolled upon the ground and wept. The whole town cried aloud.

“Could the Tongueless One not have remained with us a little longer? The country would have prospered,” the “devotees” lamented, wringing their hands.

A monastery was built and an image was installed. The rich man resigned his trusteeship of the Gopalaswami temple and became head of the monastery. Every year a tongue-cutting festival is held, and the offerings of countless disciples accumulate.

Where, you ask?

You will call this a monstrous lie, a fabricated story. Think of it as you please.

* * *

In April 1943, *Swadesamitran* published, with devotional earnestness and ostensibly for public edification, a report that a young man at some temple in northern India had cut out his tongue because of religious devotion. I do not know why that devotee severed his tongue. But people like the pauper in the story I have written would not hesitate to do it.

You may laugh if I say that a man skilled in intrigue will not shrink from using one who loses his tongue to collect money, and that the country remains ready to believe the tale he invents, be deceived and hand over its wealth. But if you put your mind to work, you will answer, “Yes.”

In this country steeped in superstition, such holy men, trance performers and workers of miracles still wield enormous power. Otherwise, would some swindler have dared publicly to proclaim that Ramalingar of Vadalur was about to return and raise the dead, and then collect money? A sacred following arose to keep rhythm with him.

Who went to that shaker of the donation box and demanded: “Where is Vallalar? What became of the pronouncement you made? Why did you deceive the town? Why did you betray its trust? Where is the money you collected?”

Every year one or two men appear, gather whatever they can and run away in this fashion. Yet however many times they are cheated, those soaked in obstinacy do not lose their madness. Is there deliverance for this country?

Today, while seated in our room, we listen and take pleasure in speech or music occurring at that very moment in faraway cities thousands of miles distant: London, Washington, Moscow, Sydney, Natal and Ottawa.

These dull-witted people do not call that a wonder or a miracle.

Instead they say, “Do you know of a miracle? A holy man has come to our town. It is said he has not eaten for ten years. If he blesses sacred ash and gives it to you, no disease remains incurable.”

Show them evidence that one person was not cured and another received no benefit. Tell them of any wondrous discovery you please: radio, wireless telegraphy, aircraft, submarine or television. They will nod, then return to praising as extraordinary the Mari temple priest’s fortune-telling, the miracle produced by a lemon blessed by a trance performer, and the glory of the man who pierces his tongue with a spear.

It might be tolerable if only the unlettered spoke so. Even among those who wag their tongues claiming to be educated, some keep searching for “great souls” with names such as the Tongueless, the Speechless, the Sightless, the Great-Way One and the Small-Eyed One, singing their praises every day.

Until medicine is taken to cure this madness, the country must remain a hunting ground for deceivers. Unless young people rise swiftly and restrain these useless men, and instead allow the custom to continue from generation to generation, civilized people of the future will regard this land as an assembly of barbarians and laugh it to scorn.

I shall call those who lack the courage to come forward and campaign against such superstitious doctrines “the tongueless.” They may be sovereigns of speech when discussing other subjects. But so long as they do not come forward to condemn the superstitions that gnaw at the country, I shall call them the tongueless.

That is no mistake.

18 April 1943

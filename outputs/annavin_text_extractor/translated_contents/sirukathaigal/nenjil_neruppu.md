## English Translation

He keeps walking, alone. Not because he expects to find delight, but because there is nothing else for him to do.

His legs ache. Weariness takes hold of him. Yet he is in no position to say that the journey has gone far enough. He is a man who must finish his walking; he has no other occupation.

There is no one to ask where he is going, no one to invite him to stay awhile before continuing. He is alone, and he must keep walking.

Even crawling creatures seek a place, and flying creatures seek greenery. This man, however, merely walks on.

He is not travelling towards his own home. He has lost everything he once possessed. Alone, he has no work other than walking.

Alone! Nor is he walking in search of some place he may call his own. Yet there is one place known to his heart. What condition is it in today? Has it lost its form? Has it disappeared altogether? He does not know. He continues to walk.

Even a fierce wind sometimes embraces great trees and rests. He finds nowhere to stay and walks alone. If asked where he is going, what could he say?

There is no strength in his gait and no sign of inward joy. He does not stop to seek a route or move onward with a destination. He walks as though he had been born merely to walk.

He has no chest or bundle. He is poor and has nothing to carry away. Yet what lies within his heart, how much of it there is, and what strange substance it may be, who can know?

Birds wheeling in the sky and snakes winding over the earth have ways to seek food and carry some of it to what waits in their nests and holes. What of him? He carries nothing. It appears there is no one to receive anything from him.

Did he travel that long road to behold a spear-eyed beauty? No. Even if such a lover's legs ached from walking, would not his eyes sing poetry? Whatever he saw, would he not see his beloved in it, while beauty flowed at the edge of his lips? This man has only a gaze that says, "There is nothing for me." As though he would answer, "My only work is walking; why ask where?" he goes on.

Is he a thief waiting to attack solitary travellers and rob them? No. A thief would look all around with cunning eyes. His eyes are like a dry pond, and his aimless gait displays no purpose. What is his work? Nothing but walking. Therefore he walks.

The morning sunlight danced awhile and then grew into a scorching flame. Did he mind the sweat pouring from him and stop to seek shade? Did he find a flower-scented breeze? No. He walks and does not stop.

The trees saw him. Could they detain him?

"Whether the sun burns or rain pours, we stand and endure it. This man possesses even more patience than we do. Even when the powdered sand becomes fire and brands his feet, he keeps walking, with no other aim!" Each seemed to speak through its leafy mouth.

He noticed none of it. He walked alone.

Open country stretched on both sides. There was no bustle of a village anywhere. A village is a place with cattle, houses, and people living in happiness. Where might one be? Here he saw only dry land and cultivated fields, many of them transformed by labour from forest and mound into fertile ground that had once yielded rich harvests. Yet the noble people who had supplied that labour were nowhere to be seen.

Cattle returning home wore bells that rang. All that reached his ears was a long sigh and the roar of a ghostly wind. Nothing else.

A village lay far away. Darkness would spread before he reached it. There was no use searching.

Earlier he had seen a village filled with lamplight and laughter. Yet no one had shown joy in their eyes and affection in their voice to call, "Come, come! Is that you?" He saw many people, and many saw him. That was all. So he walked farther, as if saying, "It is not ours."

There are many kinds of people who walk.

A woman has left a bud in a cradle. She believes that if she kisses the crown of that little head, all the sorrow she has suffered will fly away. Looking at nothing, speaking to no one, accompanied by her thoughts and with colour filling her face, she hurries home, her run becoming a rapid walk.

Two years earlier, when she walked towards home, her cheeks would be red and love would shine in her eyes. She was not then a mother. "I am going to see him," she would think and hurry onward. On meeting acquaintances along the way, the slender-waisted woman would assume a swanlike gait and falsely claim that she always walked slowly. She was yearning for a new delight.

Today it is different. The abundance of joy he gave her lies in the cradle. The lady hurries to see the child she bore. "My baby will tug my locks, taste my cheek, and do who knows what else! Even if I melt pure gold, set it with stones, make it into a chain and place it around my neck, could the joy of seeing it equal the joy that comes when my child winds those tiny arms around my neck? Tell me, where else can such happiness be found?" So asks the graceful woman who has become a mother.

"Why carry such a great load on your head?" one or two kind-hearted people may ask another traveller. His thought is that he can reach the market, sell it there, and earn the money needed to run his family properly. With that good thought, even a great mountain feels like a mustard seed. He walks in expectation; he walks to see the reward. Even as he wipes away dripping sweat, drops of honey fall within his heart. At home is the queen of his affection, and his parents are there, filled with pride. He thinks of them, and weariness does not arise.

This man walking here is human too. Yet he has nowhere to give love or receive it, no child to come near and say, "Father has arrived," and no beautiful woman to call, "First, a tender word!" Can a place without these things be a place at all? That is why he walks and walks along the long road.

There is a staff in his hand, but he walks unsteadily. Why? The staff is strong; the arm has lost its strength. In his weariness and exhaustion, the staff does not support him. He carries the staff instead. An object may by nature be useful, but its owner must possess the ability to use it. What do we say when we see a poor man carrying an umbrella whose handle is broken? What use is youth and beauty to one unable to benefit from them?

He has a staff, but not for leaning upon. He walks while bearing that too.

Fields lie everywhere. The ripened red grains sway, raise their music, and make the field look like a laughing maiden. In some fields the crop is only eight years old in the poet's fancy: already so lovely, and soon to cast away its little skirt and seek a sari, making people rejoice at the dancing girl as though she were a beautiful statue. So too the crop has matured and stands displaying its dance.

All this is the fruit of labour. Do those who now claim ownership of all this possess the right because they are the noble ones who laboured and gave it life? Had that been so, would not strife and quarrelling have vanished from the world, peace flourished, and happiness become firmly established through justice?

Birds circled in, pecked at the grain, ate, and carried some away.

There were no watchmen to drive them off with slings.

Nor were there maidens singing field songs and frightening the birds away.

The birds stole their food and flew away chirping, as though proclaiming to the crop swaying in the wind, "Behold our prowess!"

He saw this and bowed his head.

The birds that live by stealing will one day become prey to sling stones. Unaware of that, they swing happily upon coconut fronds. Why? They have no sense that theft is a crime.

But man? Surely he is not like that. Does he not win renown as a great teacher who tells others throughout the ages that theft is wrong? A great teacher indeed! Your name may be "teacher," but only for others!

The wayfarer thought of what had happened before as he watched what the little sparrow did. As he thought, his heart groaned and tears gathered in his eyes.

He had stolen. When the man carrying the goods tried to stop him, he had cut him to pieces, blinded by his own thoughtlessness and fierce rage.

"I am only the porter carrying it. My master owns the goods. Do not beat me and seize them. If they are lost, he will ruin my life," the fifty-year-old man had stood crying and pleading.

"I have told you it is not yours. It is improper for you to stop me. Go! Go!" he had said amid thunderous laughter, and taken the goods away.

What became of the old man who clung to his feet? Horror, cruelty, murder.

Blood poured out. The old man fell, his life gone.

Goods in his hand.

A corpse at his feet.

The goods were theft.

Then came murder.

He looked all around. It was not his body but his heart that trembled.

Somewhere far away, someone raised his voice and sang. To this man's ears the sound became snake, raging fire, and terror.

Unable to tell from which direction it came, he thought, "Another blood sacrifice? Shame! My heart cannot bear it. Will that victim too be an old man? No? Alas! When fear takes possession, even a rope becomes a snake."

He ran and ran, throwing away the goods.

"Whoever may chase me, my speed will save me," sang the man approaching from the opposite direction, keeping time with a "thenthinam" refrain.

Like a deer darting aside to escape a spear, the fugitive ran in another direction.

The singer did not chase him. He had not even seen the running man.

The singer had no sight.

Poor man, he was blind.

Was he alone blind? The fugitive did not understand this, for fear had blinded him.

Was this the only time the fugitive had been blind?

No. Had he not killed the old porter who bowed at his feet? At that moment, murderous rage had made his eyes sightless. From that day onward he never again stayed anywhere in that region. He kept running and hiding.

Every road looked like a deep pit to him.

He was afraid even to see his parents.

He lived in caves, bushes, and open country.

He remained anywhere only briefly and then moved on. At first it was the fear that they would discover him. Later it was something else. Before his eyes, the old porter forever bowed at his feet and then fell beneath the blade.

He shut his eyes tightly. Still the old man appeared. He shed tears and fell at his feet. Blood! Corpse! Murder!

He ran, leaving the old man behind. Yet the old man pursued him without cease. However fast he ran and wherever he hid, it was useless. The murdered man appeared again and again before him and asked:

"I went along my own road. You stopped me and killed me. Now I have become your possession. Where can I go, leaving you?"

The cruel murderer kept running, and the old man kept driving him onward.

He found a ship and took refuge aboard it. He travelled to lands beyond the reach of familiar eyes and worked hard.

Whenever he lay down exhausted, the old man who had lost both his goods and his life came and stood before him. The man's mind would become a raging sea.

"What do you lack here? Food? Clothing? Anything that can give pleasure? Why are you in such sorrow?" his companions aboard the ship would ask.

What answer could he give other than a long sigh?

Some said his mind was confused. Some called him mad. Others said overwork had made him ill.

He answered yes to all of it. But he never told anyone the one fact buried deep in his heart.

"We brought him here because he had strength enough for hard labour. Now he has become a man lost in thought. Keeping him here any longer is useless, a burden," they said, and sent him away to go wherever he wished.

He left. The old man came and stood before him.

He shed tears and wiped them with his hand, only to see that the tears had become blood.

He tried to run but could not. He stopped, and his legs began walking of their own accord.

He walks without end, through wasteland and harsh country, everywhere.

When he sees a village, he runs away.

When he sees a field heavy with grain, he sheds tears.

He wipes the tears and sees the red of blood.

There is a great burden in his heart. It has become fire and burns him.

It is a load for which there is no place to set it down and no way to be rid of it.

Bearing it, he walks. He goes on walking.

"Look, the madman is passing," children say.

"Should he not be caught and locked away?" the elders ask.

"Poor man! Though he is mad, he has done no wrong. He would not hurt even a tiny insect. He is a good man," the women say.

"A good man, am I? I am a good man!" he cries out. He bends down and strikes his head against the ground. Blood flows. He wipes it away, sees the red, and shudders.

He walks. He goes on walking.

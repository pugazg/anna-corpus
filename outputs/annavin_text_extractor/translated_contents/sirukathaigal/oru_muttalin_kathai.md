Tamil title: ஒரு முட்டாளின் கதை!

"He is a fool!"

That was what the village said.

He was a fool because he had not studied.

"Yes, I am a fool because I am uneducated," he admitted without objection.

Not only was he uneducated; he understood nothing of the ways of the world and knew only the plough and its yoke.

"It is true. I know nothing," he accepted wholeheartedly.

"Fool!"

"Complete idiot!"

"Dullard! Simpleton!"

"I lose all honour by calling him my younger brother."

"So do I, Anna. When someone asks about you, I can proudly say you are in the army. I can happily introduce myself as a merchant. But when speaking of our younger brother, I must conclude, 'He is a fool.'"

"Though he is a fool, brother, he works until his body is exhausted."

"Do we eat without working? He is useless. He knows neither the methods of trade nor the path to valour like you. What can he do except wear down his body?"

"Hey, fool!"

"Appa is calling you."

"Tell him I shall come after ploughing the whole field, Anna."

"You can plough later. Come now."

"I cannot. Your foolish brother will come only after finishing the work he has begun."

"Where is he?"

"That fool says he cannot come now, Appa."

"Why?"

"He says he will come only after finishing his work."

"He is certainly a fool. Though you, a soldier, called him, he refuses to leave the mud and continues labouring. He is a fool indeed."

"Appa, why do you speak so strangely?"

"I merely told the truth. You, my eldest, are a great warrior, a tiger in battle and a statesman. You can give life to kingdoms or, if you wish, reduce them to earth. Your second brother is a great merchant feared by the city. He has his scales; you have your gun. Money is in his hand and authority moves with your finger. Your youngest brother, possessing none of these, remains a fool. He harvests what his father and grandfather accumulated and brings it home. Look: even after you summoned him, he says he will finish his work first. You two must stand behind this fool and give him every assistance."

"We shall do all that, Appa. Finish the business that brought us here and send us away quickly. This is trading time at my shop, and Anna must meet the king."

"Is that so? Then why need he come? I earned the property. If I dispose of it as I wish, who can question me?"

"Who could question you, Appa?"

"Then both of you return comfortably to your occupations. You each have some means to live. He is a fool, a dullard, an idiot. Let all the property belong to him."

The two suffered on hearing their father's judgement. One had come seeking capital to purchase more stock and expand his trade. The other, having married a wealthy woman, found his army pay inadequate to her habits and had come intending to sell his share. Yet this was their father's decision. What could they do?

While they fretted, the younger brother returned after completing every field task. Learning why his brothers had come, he argued with his father and made him divide the property among all three. The brothers accepted their shares.

Once outside, could the educated city dwellers avoid thinking, "A complete fool! When Appa himself refused, why quarrel with him and lose property that would have been his?" They thought it, though neither revealed it to the other. Is it not the nature of educated men to pretend not to know even when they do?

The "fool" ploughed and lay down in the field.

The "warrior" became a commander in the king's army.

The "merchant" became one of the greatest magnates in the city.

It had been a long time since I had seen my friend Veeran. That was why I listened without interrupting while he recited this chronicle of a fool. Now the tale seemed finished. Veeran was no idler who told stories without purpose, so I asked:

"Veera, why this story? You began somewhere and ended somewhere else. Why tell me now of three brothers?"

The devil's work began with the division of property among the brothers.

The eldest, now a commander, was seized by a desire for conquest and marched to war on the king's orders. Rain soaked his gunpowder and made it useless. He was defeated, his forces scattered, his possessions confiscated, and he was driven away.

A second little devil stirred greed in the merchant's mind. He accumulated enormous stocks. Prices fell, and he suffered a crushing loss.

The devil sent to the fool tried in many ways to obstruct his work. The foolish farmer believed effort mattered above all, so the devil was defeated and nearly died. Another devil then tried its hand and lost its tail to the poor farmer. It pleaded, "Let me go and I shall show you a trick," taught him to turn stalks of straw into soldiers, and fled.

The poor fool did not know what use soldiers were. He had them carry musical instruments and sing, then changed them back into straw.

The devil that ruined the merchant also tried and failed against him. At the point of death, it gave the fool another boon: the power to turn leaves into gold. What could he do with gold? Could one eat it? Seeing no use, he changed it back into leaves.

A third defeated devil also left him roots capable of curing disease.

The eldest, once praised as the greatest warrior but now expelled, and the second, once admired as a brilliant merchant but now destitute, finally came back with nowhere else to live, saying, "The fool is our only refuge."

The fool welcomed them eagerly and shared his gruel.

The commander's wife was a sophisticated city woman. She refused to sit near him. "Husband, the smell of his sweat tears at my intestines. I cannot sit beside him and eat."

Her husband immediately told the fool, "My wife dislikes your smell. Go outside to eat."

"Ah, Anna, as you wish," the fool replied without protest and went to live in the horse shed.

The merchant too arrived with his wife, saying the fool was their only refuge. She also said, "I cannot sit and eat with this clown."

The second brother ordered, "Foolish brother, stay apart and do not come near us."

Since he was a fool, he said, "Very well," and went away.

The two brothers learnt of the fool's magic.

"Fool, make some soldiers for me," the elder asked. The fool created a large army from the stack of straw in his yard. With it the elder defeated a king and crowned himself.

The second brother begged for bars of gold and became a great magnate.

Money required an army to protect it, so both returned to ask for still more.

This time the fool refused to produce either gold or soldiers. Stranger still were his reasons.

"Elder brother, I gave you soldiers because I thought they would all sing. But they killed a man in the village and made his wife a widow. Should I waste straw useful to cattle only to create widows? I cannot."

"Younger brother, I thought you would play with the gold bars. Instead your servant came to the village, gave gold, and took away a cow. Three children now suffer without milk. I cannot bear that horror. I shall produce no more gold."

The two educated, intelligent brothers understood nothing and returned troubled that their brother was a fool. In the end they agreed that the elder would provide half his army to protect the merchant and the merchant half his money to support the soldiers.

The two "intelligent" men were now kings. The soldier and merchant framed laws and systems of taxation and ruled their respective countries.

The fool too became king of a country. His brothers won kingdoms through money and soldiers; he did so through his healing root. A king's daughter suffered an incurable illness, and the king proclaimed that whoever cured her would receive both the daughter and kingdom. The fool cured her, became the king's son-in-law, and after the old king died soon afterward, became king himself.

Thus all three brothers became rulers.

After his father-in-law's death, the fool took his wife and set out toward the fields, saying, "Shame! What kind of work is kingship? It is the work of idlers. My body has grown fat, and I have lost both hunger and sleep."

The people ran after him.

"King, is this proper? Should a monarch plough with a yoke?"

"Does being king prevent the stomach from growing hungry? I too must eat."

"You could impose taxes, collect money, and live in comfort."

"What is a tax? Why do I need money?"

"To pay our chariots, elephants, cavalry, infantry, and officials."

"You want me to collect from everyone and give it to them? No. Without money, perhaps no one will enter government service."

"Would that not be a problem?"

"It does not matter. If they hold no office, they will have time to work. Let them carry the manure lying in the streets and spread it over the fields. The town will become clean and the crops will grow."

Another man complained, "Maharaja, he stole from my house."

"What of it? He had none and you had some, so he took it."

"King, the people call you a fool."

"What of it?"

Who would remain in the kingdom of such a ruler? All the clever men left, and only fools remained. Like their king, they laboured, ate, and lived.

The great devil that had dispatched the three little devils received no report. It came in person and inspected all three kingdoms.

First it entered the eldest brother's army as commander-in-chief, stirred his desire for conquest, manufactured weapons, and sent him to war against a powerful neighbouring emperor. The emperor defeated him easily and seized his kingdom. The elder brother's final condition was that mere survival would be enough.

The devil then went to the merchant prince's realm, opened a business, and paid exceptionally high prices for every commodity. People brought it everything they possessed and carried away abundant money.

Money circulated profusely. The merchant king could not contain his joy. He imposed tax upon tax and milked money from the people. Since they possessed plenty, they paid without objection.

Yet though the king had money, his anxiety steadily increased, because he could buy nothing he needed. Even food and clothing became scarce: whatever price the king offered, the devil paid more and bought the goods first.

Unable to bear it, the merchant king expelled the trader from the country. The devil did not go far, but opened a shop at the border and continued as before. Seeking greater profits, the people went there. They began saying the devil merchant would soon buy even the king himself.

The king understood nothing. He cried, rolled about, became bewildered, and choked in distress.

At that moment the elder, defeated in war, came to his brother.

"Anna, you have no country. I possess one, but it is useless. See what has become of us," they said, weeping together.

Laughing, the devil set out for the fool's kingdom in the form of a commander.

"King, should a ruler not possess an army? Give the order and I shall assemble one."

"Very well. Gather them and teach them to sing," said the fool.

The people did not wish to enlist. The devil promised high pay.

"Go away. Who wants your wages? We have everything we need," they replied.

The devil threatened them, announcing that the king would kill anyone who refused.

The people went to the fool. "King, they say you will kill us if we do not join the army."

The fool did not understand. "How can I alone kill all of you? If I were not a fool, I could explain it. What can I do? I myself do not understand."

The people went away, and the devil was confounded.

It followed another path and incited a foreign king to invade the fool's realm with elephants, horses, soldiers, and cannon.

The fool's people ran out merely to watch. Not one resisted. They freely gave the invaders grain, goats, and cattle, saying, "Poor people! If such things are unavailable in your country, come and live with us."

How could sharp and cunning men bear this? The soldiers grew dispirited and asked their king, "Did you bring us here for this?"

Enraged, he ordered them to set fire to the fools' houses and granaries.

The fools wailed, "Why destroy these goods? If you need them, take them yourselves."

The soldiers could not bear the pathos. They too wept and silently turned back.

Defeated again, the devil searched for another method.

Disguised as a gentleman, it showed the fools heaps of gold and bought their goods. They did not turn the gold into ornaments, but gave it to children as playthings.

The devil understood nothing. Within days no one came to accept gold. With no food, it began starving. The fools pitied it and decided that each household would feed it in turn.

The devil began visiting one house each day. It met defeat on the very first day. The mistress refused.

"We do not feed people without rough, calloused hands. Wait if you wish and eat what remains on the leaves. Are you a man who eats without working?"

The devil became furious and quarrelled with the fool.

"Ignorant king! Do you think labour means only working with the hands? Do you know what clever people work with?"

"I am a fool. How would I know?"

"Fool, working with the brain in one's head is extremely difficult. If you agree, I shall teach your subjects."

The fool ordered everyone to learn whatever the gentleman taught.

The devil climbed a high tower, and people gathered to see labour performed with the head. Disguised as a gentleman, it discoursed at length on ways of living without physical work. The people watched for a while and went back to their own tasks.

One day passed, then two. The devil grew hungry.

The fools did not carry food to the gentleman in the tower. They assumed he would earn it by working with his head.

The fool came and asked the people, "Has the gentleman begun working with his head?"

"No. He has only been tearing his throat apart."

The devil's condition became desperate. Unable to bear its weakness, it staggered and fell from the tower, tumbling headfirst down the steps.

The fool ran over. Those gathered said, "The gentleman has begun working with his head only now."

The fool nodded and watched. The devil lay dead.

Veeran finished the tale and waited to see what I would say.

"Veera, Tolstoy composed a story that lashes those who use 'fool' as an insult. But tell me: what was the devil? Have you seen one?"

"A devil is a demon."

"Do you believe in ghosts, spirits, and demons?"

"Why not? I do."

"Have you ever seen them?"

"Why, I see them every day."

"Veera, are you joking?"

"I am not, friend. Ghosts and demons do not live hidden away. They exist among human beings. You imagine they dwell in darkness or trees. No, no. They are found in the human herd. Every quality attributed to them can be seen in people. Do you know what the things Tolstoy calls devils in this story represent?"

"What do they represent, Veera?"

"You read all the great newspapers. Discover it yourself and write to me."

Veeran left.

Do any of you who have read the story understand what its "devils" signify? If you do, please write.

18 March 1955

## Translator's Notes

This is Anna's framed Tamil retelling and political interpretation of Tolstoy's tale commonly known in English as *Ivan the Fool*.

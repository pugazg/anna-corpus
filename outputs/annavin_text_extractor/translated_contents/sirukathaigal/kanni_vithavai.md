இனித்திடும் சொல்லினனாக இருந்தவன். இப்போது பேசுவது
எட்டி போலிருக்கிறது என்று தாயும் தந்தையும் கவலைப்படு

## English Translation

Tamil title: கன்னி விதவையான கதை

"I never imagined, even in a dream, that he would change like this. I keep thinking about it, and whenever I do my mind becomes a volcano."

"Who knows whose teaching changed him so? How did a nature as good as gold alter like this?"

"Why blame somebody else for teaching him? Is he a child to be carried away by whoever picks him up? He is knowledgeable enough to impart sense to four others."

The parents were worried that their son had for some time been behaving independently. If they told him anything, he contradicted them. The man whose speech had once been as sweet as a stick of sugarcane now spoke with the bitterness of the ettikai fruit.

The mother's anxiety contained less hatred and more affection. The father's contained more anger. His affection had not vanished altogether, but it had dimmed.

"Once a bird grows wings, must it not follow its own course? What sense is there in complaining that he is no longer as he used to be? Is he still a child skipping about at play? He has reached a position that earns the admiration of others. Does he lack the maturity to distinguish right from wrong and act? You gave birth to him, yes. And then? Can you tie such a grown man to the knot of his mother's sari? A useless effort! Does he need his father to tell him that five and three make eight? He has learned how the world works, and so he handles many matters himself. What is wrong in that? His parents lament without reason that he has changed."

A relative who had long been connected with the family and genuinely wished it well spoke in this fashion after hearing the parents talk as though their son had been ruined.

Muniyandi, also connected with the family but moved by envy rather than goodwill, spoke differently:

"Do you know what has happened? Kuthalalingam used to boast that his son would never cross the line he drew. See what happens now. The son answers every word with nine! The man is suffering. He deserves it. Did he not boast, 'My son treats my word as scripture. He is unlike the boys in other houses. He never raises his bowed head, obeys discipline, and does nothing without asking me'? Now he suffers beyond measure. He asked, 'Where did you go, returning at this hour?' imagining the old order still prevailed. The son glared. 'I did not say anything wrong, son. I only asked where you had been,' the man pleaded. The son replied, 'I went to your grandfather's house,' took out a cigarette, and lit it. What could the man do? He ran inside, threw himself upon the cot, and beat his head."

The family priest, Gurumurthi Dikshitar, who consulted astrology for every good and bad event, offered his sacred verdict:

"Do not worry. It is all a planetary disturbance. The sun is not in its proper place, and that is why the boy pours fire through his speech. You lament that your own son has changed. He has not changed; the planet has made him so."

If a human being were held responsible for every event, there would be trouble and embarrassment. The priest therefore placed everything upon the planets and lived peacefully, earning a good income besides.

Rangan himself thought:

"They say I have changed, that I am no longer as before and no longer obey. Let them suffer. I have behaved like this precisely so they will suffer.

"I believed Father knew everything and would do everything rightly. I earned a name as one who never crossed his line. What benefit did I gain? This man who preaches justice to the whole town committed an outrage that made me shed tears of blood.

"He speaks like a great philosopher against greed, yet greed led him to a demonic act. I trusted that everything he did would be good. Though I was twenty, if Father said, 'Perform squats,' I would count them without resentment. Even my friends mocked me: 'Rangan has no pride.' I laughed and called them useless fellows who would not submit to their households.

"Was what he did right, moral, or just? Did he say one word to me? Did he ask whether I consented? I went to him myself and begged, 'Father, do not do this.' What did he say? He handed me a hundred rupees and said, 'Go buy whatever clothes you like.' Am I a child at play? Do I understand nothing? Was there no intelligence or justice in my words? I had never before questioned our conduct. Could he not once have thought, 'For some reason the boy dislikes this; this time let us act according to his wish'? Would his status have collapsed?

"Without considering how his son's heart suffered, he treated me as a wooden doll and told me to have a silk shirt sewn. It meant: 'This is all you are entitled to. Eat tasty food and dress handsomely; do not interfere in anything else.' That was the day I changed. He says I have changed, and I do not deny it. I have. But his conduct changed me, and nothing else."

Rangan began in anger and ended in sorrow. Anger had changed him, and its cause was his father's conduct. Kuthalalingam had done what Rangan considered unjust despite his attempt to stop it: he arranged the marriage of Rangan's younger sister.

Somasundaram, possessing neither age, beauty, nor character remotely suited to Selli, became her husband solely because he owned a mill and property worth six or seven lakhs.

That old man as husband to Selli! That devourer as bridegroom to a statue of beauty! A fresh flower thrown into burial mud!

The thought made Rangan's mind boil. That heat melted the gold of obedience, filial bond, and affection within him. All of it flowed out as tears. A single event had changed him.

Did Kuthalalingam acknowledge that his own act had changed Rangan? No. Why? He believed only that Rangan had changed, that someone had corrupted him, or perhaps, as the priest said, that the planets were at work. He did not know his own deed was the cause. Was he not the father? Therefore, he assumed, only the son could be wrong.

"Who has not changed? Everyone has. People do not see their own changes; only when another person changes does it strike the eye," Rangan told the relative closest to him. The man understood the truth embedded in the words but did not voice it.

"Has Father himself remained unchanged? When his son-in-law comes, he melts and stammers. Was he like that before? Why does his face tighten when he sees me? I understand it all," Rangan said as his anger yielded to grief.

Kuthalalingam said, "One day he will learn the reason for what I did, and then he will understand me. Could I fail to know a universal justice obvious even to a child? He hates me because he cannot see the cause of my action. Let it be. Though he has changed, is he not still my son? What change can there be in that?"

And Selli, whose marriage caused all these changes: did she remain unchanged?

Until then she had been a skylark circling freely. Seeing a cat approach her cage, she became a screaming parrot.

"She is as beautiful as a parrot. The old man is fortunate," many said, especially young men.

Selli did not remain a parrot for long. She became a tigress. What should never have happened had happened. What could tears do now?

"They sacrificed me without moisture in their hearts. Why should I remain sweet to anyone? I have been made a great lady. I must therefore roar at the poor, servants, and dependants. This man gave me this wealth in exchange for bearing the name of my husband. I have every right to spend it as I please."

That thought turned the parrot into a tigress. The stag, meanwhile, rapidly became a philosopher.

Rangan imagined that the mismatched marriage would have wounded Selli's heart, hollowed her glass-like cheeks, extinguished her eyes or filled them with fire, darkened her body, and burdened her with sighs. He yearned to see her but went only after several months.

For one instant confusion appeared in her eyes, then disappeared. She laughed loudly, clapped for servants, and ordered the Packard car. "Why, brother, shall we go to the beach?" she asked. She identified the origins of the objects around her: "This is from Jaipur, this from Delhi, this from Kashi, this from Japan."

People had formerly called Selli a silent maiden. Now she allowed nobody else to speak. Her change greatly perplexed Rangan.

Only after many days did he understand the special ability of womankind to endure the cruelties inflicted upon it.

Fragrance and stench are different. Fragrance rises slowly, spreads without haste, lasts long, and gives peace to the mind. Stench rises suddenly, pierces and spreads, then soon dies. Anger resembles stench: it bubbles and pierces but eventually dies, whether quickly or after a long time according to one's nature. The capacity to endure resembles fragrance. It is not easily acquired, and once acquired it does not quickly perish.

The tigress Selli poured out the stench of anger and then changed.

"I never imagined my Selli would change this way. I expected her to roll upon the ground, weep, and curse us all. Today she shows a pleasant face as though she had encountered no hardship. Has my Selli changed too?" Rangan wondered without understanding.

"Selli, I do not know what to make of it. If you are happy, that is enough for me. When you left home you sobbed. I grieved, thinking, 'Her life is being ruined, and sinner that I am, though I know it, I lie powerless to stop it.' Today I see you here laughing and rejoicing without any want."

"Brother, you say I have changed. Is that not it? Does the change not make you happy?"

"Immeasurably happy, Selli."

"Change is unavoidable, brother. It is nature."

"Good heavens, you speak like a great researcher."

"What is there in my speech? Speak to him and you will see."

Selli's husband arrived. Anger and hatred flashed through Rangan, but the sight of Somasundaram's peaceful face made them refuse to remain. Somasundaram greeted him respectfully.

Only afterward did Rangan remember he had forgotten to return the greeting.

"Brother says I have changed," Selli told her husband.

"My boy, everyone has. My speech no longer has its former harshness. It is unnecessary and has fallen out of use. Formerly I was possessed by the frenzy of accumulating money. I had to squeeze the workers in the mill without letting them realize it. Every day I needed rolling eyes and threatening words. I no longer do. I have converted the mill into a cooperative. The workers manage it themselves, and so angry speech, glaring, and threats have left me."

"Yes, brother. They even say tenderness now rests in his eyes. Selli, you said so yourself one day," Somasundaram added playfully.

"Only one day?" she replied.

Somasundaram continued:

"A tiger's work is destruction. It must watch sharply for prey and choose where to leap. On finding prey, it must attack; attack requires speed, and speed requires anger. That anger bubbles like fire in its eyes. Look at the rabbit's eyes, my boy. It harms nobody, so its eyes contain no cruelty. Yet the thought that many creatures here may harm it places fear in those beautiful eyes, giving them a peculiar beauty. I was once a tiger; now I am a rabbit."

"You speak delightfully."

"I think a great deal, my boy. I examine everything: my former thoughts and deeds, victories and disappointments, and the opposition that arose. By thinking repeatedly I find an explanation for each."

"Nobody would have expected a man who hunted profit to become such a thinker."

"What does change mean, my boy? That my position, thought, and conduct are no longer what they were. Not merely that, but that they appear different to you. That is important. I have changed, and a mature change has also occurred in you that enables you to recognize it.

"Some changes are obvious to everyone; others can be understood only by close observation. A change in appearance is readily known. A change of heart will not reveal itself without maturity, concern, and effort. Only if the change you see in my appearance is attractive will you try to discover whether my heart has changed too. If I still bore a bristling moustache and blood-red eyes, you would feel disgust. Would you approach and speak to me, much less perceive the change within? One cannot say the heart always matches the exterior, but the exterior must invite a desire to engage with the heart. Think of it as a window into a house."

"Brother, once he starts speaking, there is no end," Selli said.

"My boy, there may be two, no, three reasons for Selli to say that. First, she may know my heart and feel no need to hear it discussed again. Or she may be indifferent to what my heart is like. Or she may speak this way to see whether you care."

"Selli, I now understand what caused you to change," said Rangan.

"Why? Do you think I seated Selli before me, taught her lessons, and changed her? My boy, she changed me. Because I changed, she changed. Change is richly fascinating. One thing grows through another and assumes a new form. Change may arise through growth, mixture, or removal. It may be genuine or false."

"Brother, the change you see in him..."

"I nearly forgot. Change is seen and understood according to the observer's vision. If a small alteration occurs in a machine at the mill, I detect it from the sound and say, 'The roller here has worn down.' Habit gave me skill and practice gave maturity. When Selli plucks her instrument, she knows at once which string needs tightening."

"Is it not harder to determine whether an apparent change is true or false than merely to notice that it occurred?" Rangan asked.

"Do not ask! It is extremely hard. If only we possessed a perfect capacity to distinguish truth from falsehood, half the troubles in today's world would vanish. Science has given us instruments for detecting adulteration in oil. They say spiritual wisdom is the instrument for finding truth and falsehood, but that instrument itself has not yet been perfected. Still, we must not remain idle merely because we cannot do it. We must try very hard.

"Some changes endure and others do not. A mass of clouds assumes many forms before our eyes. Alteration of form is one thing, alteration of character another. Do people not speak of a fraudulent ascetic?"

"Some people say that even of you."

"Why would they not? They have read what happened to men who performed severe penance in forests, so they fear and doubt. Only yesterday an orator is said to have shouted to the mill workers, 'The capitalist has changed his form. Now is precisely when you must be vigilant.' A change in form need not produce a change in character."

"Only a change in character brings benefit. What use is a mere alteration of form?"

"Yes. Thinking further, we can classify change as beneficial or meaningless. We see vehicles with three wheels, two wheels, and four wheels. In each case one might say a wheel was added or removed, yet the change serves a purpose. If we decide to make some change of our own and remove one of a motorcar's four wheels, the utility is destroyed. I do not speak of meaningless change. Observe only the changes Selli makes to her braid during the four days you are here, and you will understand the painful search for changes without purpose."

"If you dislike it, say so. Why jab at me?" Selli asked.

"Her words point to another classification: changes made for oneself and changes made for others; unavoidable and avoidable; expected and unexpected.

"The mill now has two entrance gates; formerly it had one. The auditor argued it was wasteful expense. I showed that the volume of carts required two gates and how much time they saved, and he agreed. As the mill grows, the surroundings predictably change. Unexpected change also occurs. The well water here became polluted. We did not foresee it. Had we done so, we would have sought a method by which the waste caused no harm. Come next year and you will see a fine vegetable garden where waste now accumulates. Waste itself results from transformation of materials, and now that waste will enable other materials to arise."

"Whatever he finds, he can extract profit from it," Selli said. "Look at him, brother. Even worthless waste becomes a source of profit once he discovers it can feed a vegetable garden."

"Selli, is it enough to use a material for only one benefit? Iron is one material, yet we forge swords, spears, locks, keys, machines, and agricultural tools from it. How many uses it has!"

"They say the hearts of capitalists are iron too."

"Mischievous girl! Iron becomes so many useful implements because people heat and hammer it. What benefit would it yield if its shape were never changed? Consider this amusement: heat turns ice into water, a useful substance. Greater heat turns the same water into vapour."

"Then it becomes useless."

"Only for the moment. Vapour becomes cloud, rain, and water again."

"My boy is right, Selli. Nature performs strange transformations. It is a magician, even a scientist. Heat turns ice into water. Things placed in heat lose their inner juice. A calf becomes a bull, a bud a flower, a child a youth, a green leaf a dry one, and a plant a tree. A tree becomes firewood and a dry leaf fuel. Sugarcane becomes juice, syrup, a solid cake, powder, dissolves again, and combines with food for use. River water mixed with mud cannot be drunk; its usefulness falls."

"It can irrigate."

"True. It cannot be drunk but serves irrigation. Yet if river water mixes with the sea, it is useful neither for drinking nor irrigation. Bad company ends in harm. In the river it could be drunk; joining the sea destroyed that use. People are like that too. 'He went to the city and changed. He acquired money and changed. He studied and changed accordingly. He joined that man and therefore changed.' Do we not speak so? We see a man's very nature change while gambling. After four days of hunger he grows as thin as a splinter, beyond recognition."

"Sometimes the object does not change; only the mind does," said Selli.

"Yes. When one's mouth is ulcerated, food is distasteful, though the change lies not in the food but in the person. Can the breeze burn or moonlight scorch? No. Yet to those tormented by separation, breeze and moon seem changed: the breeze lashes and the moon burns.

"A sculptor turns stone into a statue. A priest turns the statue into a god. A fraud becomes a priest, and people call the leaf a stone again. Did you notice?"

"You said a statue becomes a god through the priest. After all this talk, let me ask: how did you become a god to Selli? She regarded you not even as a statue but as stone. She grieved, and I was furious."

"My boy, if stone softens like fruit when touched, what can one think? Selli indeed regarded me as stone, and she was not wrong. She believed her life would strike that stone and shatter into a hundred pieces. But I was the stone form that melted and stood with overflowing tears.

"Selli stood weeping. At the sight of me her body trembled with fear and her face went pale. I melted inwardly. I remained in the form of stone, but the change produced in me by Selli's condition was something I had never expected. She trembled because she thought the man who had tied the tali possessed the right to do anything to her. Then she saw tears stream from the stone form, and her trembling diminished. She worshipped me. Do you know what she called me? 'Father.' Is there a word more sacred and worthy of worship?

"The arrogance of wealth may drag many women to one's bed. But is receiving a woman like Selli as a daughter ordinary or commonplace? She worshipped me, yet she was the one who granted me a boon. She became my daughter.

"Do not be startled and ask whether her life was not being ruined. I imposed no barrier. I gave her the right to choose a husband suited to her heart. But she said, 'This is a sacred place of sacrifice. Present society will not accept it. Your high standing will fall and the town's blame will gather around you. I am your daughter and shall pass my life as a virgin.' She has remained a virgin. Thinking repeatedly of the great cruelty done to her, I melted. My heart acquired motherhood. A great change occurred. I became human and gained a daughter."

"My boy, I was only a form of stone. Selli made me..."

"Selli, you have not opened your mouth and said one word all these days," Rangan said.

"Nor shall I now, brother. He is my father. Did I become his daughter in order to bring him dishonour?"

"Selli, this is a sacred place of sacrifice, a place of worship."

Somasundaram then said, "My boy, ask why your father did this. I shall tell you. Among my friends there was a fierce debate over the power of money. I wagered that even the finest father, if trapped in difficulty, would give his daughter in marriage to an old or cruel man for money.

"I worked according to a plan. Your father acquired the right to sell the mill's products. That was my arrangement. He purchased stock beyond his means and filled his warehouse. That was my urging. I tempted him with the promise that if he gritted his teeth for three months his profit would triple. In the third month new goods rose like a mountain in my mill, at a lower price. Your father was about to become destitute and unable to repay his debt.

"Do you remember that arrangements were underway to marry you to the son of Manikka Bhoopathi, the drugstore owner? That too was my arrangement. Bhoopathi's very shop had been mortgaged to me, and so he consented to give his son in marriage. If that wedding occurred, your father's shortage of money would end and danger would pass. But for the wedding to occur, your father's debt could not be exposed. My mouth had to be closed.

"My condition was Selli. Your father shed tears. I turned my heart to stone. I saw only the one lakh rupees before my mind's eye, and no other morality or compassion. Your father floundered and consented to give Selli. I took back the goods and arranged your marriage. You alone refused it."

"Sir! I must run at once and ask my father's forgiveness. I have committed many cruelties against him."

"He will come here himself, my boy. We can telephone him."

"Because I became angry with him, he was reduced to a wretched state."

"Do not worry. When he learns that Selli has become my daughter, he too will regain his former self."

Kuthalalingam was away from town and returned only four days later. He did not go directly home. He came to Somasundaram's house. Somasundaram welcomed him, asked him to sit, took up the telephone, and began calling Rangan.

With a pistol he had concealed, Kuthalalingam shot Somasundaram, not once but repeatedly.

"Perish, destroyer of my family! Perish, outcaste who conspired and made me your slave! Through you I lost the love of the son I fathered," Kuthalalingam shouted like a madman.

Even as blood gushed out, a smile played over Somasundaram's face.

Selli came running and saw him fall, barely alive.

"Father!" she screamed, fell upon him, and wept.

"Selli..." Kuthalalingam called in a tearful voice.

Seeing Selli cry "Father! Father!" over Somasundaram, Kuthalalingam stood stunned.

"Selli, quickly, paper for writing. Kuthalam, do not fear. My work is done. Give me the pistol," Somasundaram said while life barely remained.

He wrote and signed:

> Broken-hearted suicide.
>
> Somasundaram

"Father!" Selli sobbed, clutching at Somasundaram's departing life.

"Daughter!" cried Kuthalalingam, falling at Selli's feet.

Many days passed before Kuthalalingam learned the complete truth, but by then his mind had broken.

"He was a good man, but he married his daughter into a great household out of greed. The old man committed suicide, and this one went mad. How could he remain the same? Poor fellow, he has changed like this."

People around town spoke so.

The virgin Selli assumed the garb of widowhood.

14 January 1961

## Translator's Notes

The canonical source combines two OCR parts into one story. The title's `கன்னி விதவை` is deliberately paradoxical: Selli remains sexually untouched because Somasundaram treats her as his daughter, yet society and his death impose widowhood upon her. The long discussion of change is translated in full because its examples prepare the revelation of genuine and false moral transformation. The final scan confirms that the dying Somasundaram writes a suicide note to shield Kuthalalingam from prosecution. Apparent OCR errors and difficult terms are documented separately.

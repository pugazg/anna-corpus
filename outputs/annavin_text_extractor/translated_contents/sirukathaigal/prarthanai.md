## English Translation

“Annam! Come here, girl. Must it always be play? Come and worship the deity. Open your eyes, look, and pray, ‘Mother Kamakshi.’ From the moment you rise in the morning, you are absorbed in kanakambaram flowers. In the evening, you delight in jasmine. Can time pass forever in this fashion? Come, Annam, come.”

Annam had a slender waist, fish-like eyes, a reddish complexion, and a ready smile. She knew nothing of the world.

Thayammal, Annam’s mother, was old. She had witnessed changes in the world. She had seen good men become wastrels and paupers become Kuberas. She rejoiced at the beauty of her only daughter. Yet she worried that the girl knew nothing of the world and passed her days eating, sleeping, dressing, exchanging village gossip, stringing flowers, and reading books.

Your mind may ask why this should cause worry. Was it not Thayammal who knew the hardship of that family? The man who tills the land and waters it rejoices when the crop grows, worries when weeds sprout, withers when the lake dries, and looks up at the sky in distress: “Mahamayi! If there is still no rain, the field will dry and so will my stomach.” What anxiety does a mere passer-by have? He will walk on, trampling and crushing the crop in his path.

Thayammal did not intend to attain the eight forms of wealth through Annam. That was the only path she saw for the girl to live. She could find peace only if she entrusted Annam to a good man while still alive. Otherwise, would Annam too not have to fly about without refuge?

All the sufferings Thayammal had endured came back to her. How many quarrels, how much starvation and abuse! Good heavens! She thought that not even an enemy should undergo such misery.

Men look at a woman as though they would swallow her. While wooing her, they swear: “You alone are the celestial Rambha upon earth. I shall touch no other woman.” But when marriage is mentioned, they speak of a different place, status, lineage, caste restrictions, household discipline, and all manner of things.

“Why marry, my darling? Will the affection between us disappear?” they ask. When she sobs, they wipe her eyes. At that moment the heart melts. Later, he belongs to one world and she to another. The division keeps widening. One maiden is chosen for marriage, another for love.

This disorder made Thayammal a sex worker. Annam was its consequence. What fault was there, poor woman, in Thayammal’s desire to secure at least a good fate for Annam?

Malaiyappan, the landlord’s son, came freely to Thayammal’s house. Thayammal’s father was delighted. The villagers even began to fear him a little. Thayammal belonged to a poor household, but her beauty drew Malaiyappan to that hut.

Malaiyappan’s parents decided that they must tie him in marriage before the boy became thoroughly corrupted. Before they could do so, Malaiyappan had seduced Thayammal in the hut. It did not require much effort. He touched her, and the tender shoot bent.

“I am going to marry only you. I swear upon my father,” Malaiyappan said.

The maiden was filled with joy. In her lower belly, a witness to that joy slowly began to move.

As soon as he learned the news, Malaiyappan slipped away from the village. Thayammal’s father washed away his grief and anger with tears.

“The rogue is a great deceiver. Somehow that woman tried to enchant and trap him. He is only a young fellow. She flashed her eyes, and he was bewitched. Something has happened. He has now written from Kodaikanal asking us to give that family some money and send them out of the village.”

With satisfaction that his son had reformed, the landlord read the letter to the boy’s mother. “Akhilandeswari, you have given Malaiyappan this good sense!” the lady prayed joyfully.

What did that woman know of Thayammal’s prayer?

The pregnant young woman grew thin, lost food and sleep, and tossed upon her bed stricken by anxiety. Terrified by the village’s abuse, not knowing what to do, she wept and prayed to Kali:

“Mari Mother, what sin did I commit that you brought me to this state? The affection he showed made me consent. How lovingly he swore that he would tie the marriage pendant around my neck. He swore upon his father, and I believed him. He has abandoned me in midstream. Should this poor woman be made to suffer so? What wrong did I do you? I never failed in worship, fasting, or devotion. Should this stone have fallen upon my head?”

Was this alone Kali’s work? If she undertook to show compassion to every woman shedding tears, she would never find rest. Kali’s grace did not arrive, nor did the foetus perish.

“Kali! Could I not contract the cholera, plague, or severe fever coming through the village? Take me to your feet,” the foolish woman prayed. The heap of corpses at Kali’s feet was already enormous. Where was there room for her?

Inside her belly, Annam gently kicked Thayammal with her foot. For others, a child moving in the womb brings a smile to the face. For Thayammal, it brought terror.

At first, the landlord thought he should give them a little money and drive “these creatures” from the village. Then he reconsidered: why give money when a threat would make them run? He did exactly that.

In a government hospital in another town, the unmarried Thayammal gave birth to a beautiful girl.

The suffering that followed was beyond measure. There were countless ordeals and any number of “protectors.” With every man she knew, there came a different situation, intention, and nature. She became a sex worker.

“That terrible sinner abandoned me to this fate. Will Kali fail to pay him his wages by sending a thunderbolt upon his head and destroying his family?” Thayammal would curse the landlord’s son and pray to Kali.

Did a thunderbolt strike his head? He married the only daughter of a landlord in a neighbouring village, and that estate fell into his hands. The government placed a “Rao Bahadur” title upon his head as well.

The temple priest spread word throughout the district: “Is not Malaiyappan a great devotee?” Malaiyappan appeared at every temple festival without fail.

There were many other women who had stumbled like Thayammal. They received the affection of this “lord of charity.” At such times, the temple priests provided intelligence and assistance. Was that something disgraceful? Had not Lord Siva himself supposedly served as messenger between Sundarar and Paravai?

The pain of Thayammal’s life in prostitution cannot be described. What if it were? Who would feel pity? One or two might ask for her address. What else would happen?

Annam grew and reached womanhood. Thayammal became exhausted from struggling through the world. She had a dilapidated house, jewellery worth about one thousand rupees, many diseases in her body, and a volcano in her heart.

In this condition, Thayammal told marriage brokers that she wished to marry Annam to someone. As usual, she appealed to Kali.

Who would readily agree to marry a sex worker’s daughter? Yet Annam’s beauty did not let men say “I refuse” so easily.

“What does it matter to you that her mother is a sex worker? Look at Annam’s grace. She is like a creeper. Her gaze is cool; her lips stir desire. Her speech can give even a brute the heart of a child, and her laughter is music. What stain does Annam bear? If Thayammal is a sex worker, that merely bears witness to her suffering. Marry Annam and prevent the same suffering from coming to her.”

Annam’s eyes spoke thus to the hearts of young men. Some therefore began to say, “Yes, why should I not marry her?”

“Whatever his caste, it is enough if he earns some twenty rupees a month and protects my daughter,” said Thayammal.

The brokers did everything they could. Some men came to see the prospective bride and left. Nothing was settled. This was the source of Thayammal’s worry.

At that time, the man keeping her as his mistress was a toddy-shop contractor. Whenever Thayammal became too worried, he told her to “take a little.” Thayammal had acquired that habit too.

Annam’s own heart was not unmoved. She longed for a young man named Arumugam, who frequently passed through that street expressly to see her. Their meeting became a daily event.

Inside the front entrance of the house, Annam would sit stringing flowers. Arumugam would come to the betel-and-areca shop opposite, chew betel, and stand there. The two would meet. They smiled, and their hearts slowly became one.

They did not speak. Why should they? Was the great poet’s statement false? “When eye meets answering eye, what use are words spoken by the mouth?”

Thayammal had witnessed this scene once or twice. Had she not reached her present fate by being deceived by a false smile and sweet words? Thinking that she must prevent anyone from ruining Annam in the same way, she rebuked her several times.

“If you sit at the entrance, should you not close the street door? Every kind of shaven head and block of wood is standing at the shop,” Thayammal would say.

Annam’s face would tighten.

“Foolish girl! I say it only for your good. Did I not come to this fate because, when I was young, that destroyer wooed and ruined me? The world is very wicked. You must live carefully,” she advised.

“He is neither a shaven head nor a block of wood,” Annam’s inner mind said, but she did not dare open her mouth.

A few days later, the contractor came to Thayammal’s house with a friend. He had already drunk a little while coming and had brought some more in his hand.

Could Thayammal behave in a way that displeased him? If she did, her livelihood would be twisted out of shape. Therefore, when the visitor drank and slurred, she did the same.

Annam was a young woman. The grandfather who had served as a companion had died. Thayammal therefore told Annam to eat early, bolt her room, lie down, and sleep. Then she devoted herself to drinking the liquors brought by the contractor.

All three became intoxicated. Their talk and laughter grew loud. The speech of the newcomer showed him to be a practised operator. The young man was the contractor’s friend, and that evening’s expense was his. The lustful rogue had a reason for spending it.

Thayammal and the contractor managed to remain upright, though babbling, only until midnight. After that, they fell like trees.

The visiting rogue merely appeared heavily intoxicated. In truth, he had drunk only enough to make himself a little livelier. Once the other two had collapsed, he rose, saying to himself, “Let us attend to the work for which we came,” and went towards Annam’s room.

Annam was asleep. The lustful man knocked on the door. Still heavy with sleep, Annam asked who it was and opened it. Seeing an unfamiliar face at the threshold, she was briefly afraid. Then she recognised him as the man who had arrived with the contractor and asked, “What is it? What do you want?”

He was thoroughly experienced in the world of pleasure.

“What do I want? Do you not know?” he asked.

Annam understood and was terrified. By then he had seized her hand. She struggled and cried out. Her strength was a mere berry to him. He pressed his drink-stinking mouth upon the mouth that screamed.

There was a struggle upon the cot.

“Help! I shall wake my mother. Who do you think I am? They are arranging my marriage.”

“What do you think is about to happen now?”

“I do not consent to this sort of trade, do you understand? If my mother learns of it, she will not spare me.”

“That is all right, girl. I told him, and he agreed before bringing me here.”

“Really? Were they not arranging my marriage?”

“Is it a marriage only if someone leaps at your neck and ties a pendant? Do men not marry and then abandon women in the middle of the street? I shall protect you until the end.”

“No. I shall not agree.”

In the end, she agreed. To what?

To open the door at five in the morning and send him out. He was exceptionally cunning and had made her submit. He convinced Annam that Thayammal had consented.

Dawn came. Thayammal’s intoxication cleared, and she looked for the newcomer.

With a smile Annam said, “He left only half an hour ago, mother. He said he would return tonight.”

“What business did you have talking to him?” Thayammal asked angrily.

She went to Annam’s room, beat her lower belly, and wailed, “Have you too come to this path, sinner?” She had seen his handkerchief lying upon the cot.

“He said you had told him. I thought of waking you and asking, but you were completely unconscious,” said Annam.

Thayammal said nothing more. She sat with her hand against her cheek, tears brimming in her eyes.

Again and again, Thayammal asked the contractor where the man who had come that day was.

Three months later, the contractor handed her a wedding invitation announcing the auspicious marriage of long-lived Sivanandan and fortunate Sundari. The man who had visited that day, he said, was the groom.

The invitation itself asked readers to bless the couple.

“Sinner! He has destroyed my household,” Thayammal cursed.

“Why are you scolding him, you stout donkey?” asked the contractor.

“It has been three months since Annam last menstruated.”

“Is that so? Was it him?”

“Yes! The man you brought that day. He seduced Annam that very night. This foolish girl allowed it and now stands blinking helplessly.”

“I intended to find a good match and settle her. Three months?”

“Can you not hear her vomiting over there?”

Yes. Annam was vomiting because of that condition.

A daughter following her mother’s path, men’s lust, and hearts that scorch love for the sake of social restrictions create sex workers. Thayammal and Annam are witnesses to it. But in this world inhabited by “virtuous” men, will anyone see the tears of those silenced women?

Thayammal’s prayer now was: “Kali, let my daughter give birth safely, without any harm.”

She did not appeal to Kali, “Send a thunderbolt upon the head of the sinner who ruined my daughter.” What benefit had the poor woman obtained by making that appeal when the same thing happened to her?

*Dravida Nadu*, 14 March 1943

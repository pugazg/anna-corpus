ஏழை

அவன் ஒர் ஏழை - ஏளனத்துக்கு ஆளானவன். “தொட்
டது துலங்காது, கெட்டது நல்லது புரியாது, மாடுபோல் பாடு
படுவான், புத்தி கிடையாது” என்று நண்பர்களே நையாண்டி
செய்வார்கள். சில வேளைகளில்தான் அவனுக்குக் கோபமும்
துக்கமும் பீறிட்டுக் கொண்டு வரும். பொதுவாகச் சிரித்தபடிதான்
இருப்பான் - உள்ளூர அவனுக்கே, தனக்குப் போதுமான அளவு
அறிவுக்கூர்மை இல்லை என்ற எண்ணம்போலும். ஆகவே
உள்ளதைத்தானே சொல்கிறார்கள், இதற்காகக் கோபித்துக்
கொள்ளலாமா என்று எண்ணித் தனக்குத்தானே சமாதானம்
தேடிக் கொள்வான்.

அவன் ஏழை, ஆனால் ஏழை என்ற நிலையிலிருந்து விடுபட்
டாக வேண்டும் என்ற எண்ணம் எப்போதும், குடைந்து
கொண்டிருக்கும் மனம் கொண்டவன். எதையாவது செய்து எந்தப்
பாடாவது பட்டுப் பொருள் பெற வேண்டும்; ஏழ்மையை
விரட்டிட வேண்டும் - செல்வவான் ஆகவேண்டும் என்று
எண்ணுவான்; கூறுவான்.

(வேலையில் சுறுசுறுப்பு இல்லை, செய்வதிலே தவறுகள்
ஏற்பட்டுவிடுகின்றன. சுட்டிக் காட்டினால் இளிக்கிறான் அல்லது
கெஞ்சுகிறான். தொலைத்து விடலாம் என்றாலோ பார்க்கப்
பரிதாபமாக இருக்கிறது ' என்று முதலாளி கூறுவதுண்டு பலமுறை.
அப்போதெல்லாம், ஏழை எல்லப்பன், எப்படிப் பணக்காரனாவதுி
என்று திட்டமிட்டுக் கொண்டு இருந்தான் என்பது பொருள். அதை
எப்படி அவன் கூச்சத்தை விட்டு வெளியே கூறமுடியும்? முட்
டாள் என்றுகூட ஏசுவார்கள் - கோபித்துக் கொள்வதிலே பயன்
இல்லை என்று மனதுக்கு ஆறுதல் கூறிக் கொள்வான்.

முயற்சியைக் கைவிடக்கூடாது என்று ஒவ்வோர் தோல்வி
யின் போதும் எண்ணிக் கொள்வான். ஏனெனில் அவன்
பார்த்துமிருக்கிறான்; கேள்விப்பட்டும் இருக்கிறான். பணக்காரர்
ஆன பலர், ஆவதற்கு முன்பு அறிவுத் திறமையுள்ளவர்கள் என்று
புகழ் பெற்றவர்கள் அல்ல என்பதனை.

“மாடு மேய்க்கக்கூடப் பயன்படமாட்டாய். மண்டை
முழுவதும் களிமண்தான் உனக்கு' என்று கணக்கு ஆசிரியர்,
ஒவ்வொரு நாளும் சொல்லிச் சொல்லித் தலையிலே குட்டுவார்,
செல்லப்பன் என்பவனை. எல்லப்பனுடன் படித்தவன். இன்று
செல்லப்பன் பெரிய புள்ளி. பணத்திலே புரளுகிறான். சேமியா
வியாபாரத்தில் ஆரம்பித்து, செயற்கை வைர வியாபரம் நடத்தி,
இப்போது, செல்லப்பா கம்பெனி ஏற்றுமதி இறக்குமதி ஏஜெண்டு
என்ற பட்டம் பெற்று, பத்து இலட்சத்துக்கு அதிபதியாகிவிட்டான்.
வீட்டுக் கணக்குக்கூட அவனுக்கு, எல்லப்பன்தான் போட்டுக்
கொடுப்பான், இப்போது செல்லப்பனுக்கு, அடுத்த வருஷம்
அமெரிக்காவில் எத்தனை லட்சம் பன்றிகள் இருக்கும் என்ற
கணக்கிலிருந்து அவைகளுக்கு எத்தனை டன் சோனமும் உருளைக்
கிழங்கும் தேவை என்பது வரையில் கணக்குத் தெரியும்
என்கிறார்கள்.

“எப்படித்தான் இவ்வளவு கணக்கையும் கற்றுக்
கொண்டானோ, :மக்கு” என்று பெயர் வாங்கிய செல்லப்பன்:
என்று எல்லப்பன் வியப்படைகிறான்; விளங்கவே இல்லை.

“எல்லாம் நான் கொடுத்த தகவல். அதைப் பயன்படுத்திக்
கொண்டு எனக்கு உரிய பங்கு கொடுப்பதாகச் சொல்லி நம்ப
வைத்து, முறைகளை எல்லாம் கேட்டுத் தெரிந்து கொண்டு,
கடைசியில் எல்லாவற்றையும் அவனே சுருட்டிக் கொண்டு
என்னை விரட்டியே விட்டான்; ஈவு இரக்கமற்ற பாவி” என்று
முணுமுணுத்தபடி முடங்கிக் கிடக்கிறான் கந்தப்பன். அவனுடைய
கந்தலாடையையும் தள்ளாடும் நடையையும் காண்பவர்கள்,
என்னமோ பிதற்றுகிறான் என்று எண்ணிக் கொள்கிறார்கள் -
நம்ப மறுக்கிறார்கள்.

கந்தப்பன், தகவல்களைத் துருவித் துருவிக் கண்டறிபவன்,
கணக்குப் போடுபவன், புதுப் புதுத் தொழில்கள் குறித்த திட்ட
மெல்லாம் போடுவதில் வல்லவன். அதை விளக்குவதிலும் திறமை
மிக்கவன். ஆனால் தானாகக் காரியமாற்றுவதற்குத் துளியும்
வசதியற்றவன். எனவே செல்லப்பன் போன்றவர்களிடம்

“வசதி என்றால் எவ்வளவு தேவைப்படும், சொல்லேன்."

“நம்பமாட்டான், உன்னைப் போன்றவன். செல்லப்பனிடம்
ஒரு பேச்சு சொல்லு, உடனே விவரம் கேட்டுக் கொண்டு
காலத்தை ஒட்டமாட்டான். பணத்தை எடுத்துக் கொடுப்பான்;
வேலையை மளமளவென்று ஆரம்பிக்கச் சொல்லுவான்
தெரியுமா?”

“உன்னைத்தான், உபயோகமற்றவன் என்று ஏசி விரட்டி
விட்டானே! இன்னும் எதற்காக அவனுடைய பேச்சு? விட்டுத்
தள்ளு. மூன்றாயிரம் வேண்டும். அவ்வளவுதானே. நாலு நாள்
பொறுத்துக்கொள்.”

“சரி! உன் பேச்சிலே எனக்கென்னவோ, பலமான
நம்பிக்கை ஏற்படுகிறது. இதோ பார், இது ஒரு பிரபலமான
அமெரிக்க திபுணர் தயாரித்த முறை. ஒருமுறை, மகாபலிபுரத்துச்
சிற்பங்களைப் பார்க்க அந்த அமெரிக்கர் வந்தபோது, தற்செயலாக
அங்குப் போயிருந்த நான், அவருக்கு மெத்த உதவியாக இருந்தேன்.
அப்போது அவர் கொடுத்த முறை இது.'*

“அமெரிக்காக்காரன், ஏன் அந்த முறையைப் பயன் படுத்தித்
தானே அந்தத் தொழிலை நடத்த இலாபம் தேடிக் கொள்ளாமல்
உன்னிடம் கொடுத்தான்? காரணம்?”

## English Translation

He was a poor man, and an object of ridicule.

“Nothing he touches prospers. He cannot tell good from bad. He toils like an ox but has no brains,” even his friends would mock. Only occasionally did anger and grief burst forth in him. Usually he kept smiling. Deep down, perhaps he too thought that he did not possess enough sharpness of mind. “They are only saying what is true; why become angry over it?” he would think, consoling himself.

He was poor, but his mind was forever tormented by the thought that he must escape poverty. He must do something, endure any hardship, acquire money, drive poverty away, and become rich. He both thought and said so.

“There is no briskness in his work, and whatever he does contains mistakes. If they are pointed out, he grins or pleads. One thinks of dismissing him, but he looks so pitiable,” his employer often said.

At each such moment, poor Ellappan was in fact planning how to become rich. How could he cast off his shyness and announce that? They would abuse him as a fool too. There was no use growing angry, he told his heart.

After every failure he resolved not to abandon his effort. He had both seen and heard that many who became rich had not previously been renowned for intellect or talent.

Every day the arithmetic teacher used to knock Sellappan on the head and say, “You would be useless even for grazing cattle. Your whole skull is filled with clay.” Sellappan had studied with Ellappan. Today he was a great man rolling in money. Beginning with vermicelli, moving into imitation diamonds, and now bearing the title “Sellappa Company, Import and Export Agents,” he had become master of a million rupees.

Ellappan once had to do even Sellappan's household accounts. Now people said Sellappan could calculate how many million pigs America would have next year and how many tons of maize and potatoes they would require.

“How did the Sellappan once known as a blockhead learn all these calculations?” Ellappan wondered. He could not understand it.

Kandappan lay huddled and muttering: “Every bit of information came from me. He promised me my proper share, gained my confidence, learned all the methods, then seized everything and drove me out. The merciless sinner!”

Those who saw Kandappan's rags and staggering gait assumed that he was babbling and refused to believe him.

Kandappan was skilled at digging out information, making calculations, devising plans for new industries, and explaining them. But he possessed not the slightest means of putting a plan into operation by himself. He therefore had to take his schemes to men like Sellappan. At first a strong rumour circulated that one such scheme of Kandappan's had made Sellappan wealthy. Sellappan neither denied nor admitted it. “Take it as true if you like,” he would say with a laugh. That casual manner alone made Kandappan's accusation unbelievable.

Kandappan complained to Ellappan once or twice. Ellappan not only disbelieved him but rebuked and drove him away.

Ellappan had a habit of listening with relish to accounts of how newly rich men had made their fortunes. Only while Kandappan told such tales did Ellappan show interest; he might even give him a quarter or half an anna.

“Why speak of distant matters?” Kandappan said. “There is a new method for making ginger candy. People call Irulappan Company's product mere rubbish. I know how to produce goods of exceptional quality.”

“If you know, why not do it?”

“One requires capital.”

“How much? Tell me.”

“Would you give it to me if I told you? What do you possess?”

“Why worry about what I possess or how? Tell me how much is needed to begin. Somehow I shall find the money.”

“That is precisely what I ask: how? If I reveal the new method after trusting the hope you offer, what use is it if you then spread your empty hands?”

“What profit comes from locking the method inside your mind without telling anyone?”

“Why argue needlessly? Can you invest three thousand rupees? Tell me that.”

“What profit if I do?”

“Ten thousand in the very first year.”

“A three-thousand-rupee investment and a ten-thousand-rupee profit? What fool would believe that?”

“A man like you would not. Say one word to Sellappan: he will not waste time asking for detail. He will hand over the money and order the work begun at once. Do you know that?”

“Did he not abuse you as useless and drive you away? Why still speak of him? Leave it. You need three thousand, that is all. Wait four days.”

“Very well. Somehow your words inspire strong confidence in me. Look here. This is a method devised by a renowned American expert. Once, when the American came to see the sculptures at Mamallapuram, I happened to be there and gave him considerable help. He gave me this method then.”

“Why did the American give it to you instead of using it himself to run the industry and make a profit? What was his reason?”

“Reason? He and I were brothers born of one womb in a former life. Go away! You habitually probe only into irrelevant things. Sellappan knows how to prosper. You have doubt and confusion about everything. Tell Sellappan merely that it is the Anderson method, an American method. He will ask no question and will urge that the enterprise begin. That is Sellappan! Is he like you? He knows how to live.”

After this conversation, Ellappan dismissed Kandappan's plan as worthless. Later he went to Sellappan, not on business, but to invite him to preside over the old students' association anniversary. During their talk he mentioned what Kandappan had said.

“The Anderson method? The American method? Yes, that method has become our company's exclusive property. It must be six months since the agreement was made. How can Kandappan claim ownership? Poor fellow, he will become trapped in trouble if he files a suit. This is his habit: without knowing the facts he gets entangled in something and then stares helplessly.”

Ellappan grew angry with Kandappan, went to his house, and abused him. Doubt entered Kandappan's own mind. Perhaps in his enthusiasm he had revealed the method earlier and Sellappan, master of every stratagem, had written directly to Anderson and obtained an agreement. He became despondent.

The very next day a warning advertisement appeared in the newspapers:

> The rare American Anderson method is our exclusive property. Legal action will be taken against anyone claiming any right in it. Be warned.

Kandappan himself was shaken.

“What would have become of me if I had trusted you and invested three thousand?” Ellappan scolded. “Fortunately Sellappan is my friend and remains friendly even after rising in the world. By warning me at the right moment, he saved me. Otherwise I would have faced disaster.”

Kandappan's anger did not even turn against Sellappan. “Anderson deceived us after winning our trust. He gave me the rights and then gave Sellappan the same rights. Greed caused it! He looked like a great gentleman, but his conduct is disgraceful. Fortunately Sellappan warned me early. Had I invested capital and begun production before the suit came, I would have suffered all the trouble and fever.”

Meanwhile Sellappan was investigating the possible profit. To prevent anyone else from obtaining Kandappan's method, he had seated another man called Anderson in his office and obtained a document saying that this “American” possessed such a method.

Sellappan occupied a lofty station, so no one dared collide with him. How could rag-clad Kandappan find such courage?

Sellappan's many triumphs stood as proof of what a “great station” meant. To describe a man as one who makes a dead tree sprout and turns wasteland into an orchard is to call him a magician. Whenever someone said of an enterprise, “It is ruined; I have abandoned it,” Sellappan would appear and say, “I shall run it.” He would do so, and by one means or another it always ended in profit for him.

No one knew what he did. The accounts showed profit. No one understood whom he approached or how he planned, but in the end he demonstrated success. The whole town might say, “This time he is finished! He has become properly trapped and cannot escape.” Friends might warn him. Yet he would win in a manner that left every one of them hanging his head.

Arumugam had consulted his horoscope, laid the ironworks foundation at an auspicious hour, and begun it with ministers' blessings. Within six months the works closed. The loss was unbearable, and opposition from the town had grown overwhelming.

The village council passed unanimous resolutions. The iron-rich earth found there would yield good profit only if exported abroad, it declared; establishing an ironworks would merely enable one private face to reap profit, and the council therefore strongly condemned the scheme. Because the factory drew people away from agriculture, farm labour had become scarce. Agriculture itself would be destroyed and the townspeople left struggling for food, another resolution warned, urging Arumugam to close the factory in the public interest.

“He is looting us! Seizing everything he can! Are we simpletons? The iron earth lies in our own land. Only if we give it can he dig it out. Do not sell your land!”

There were speeches, slogans, and posters everywhere. The factory building alone had swallowed two hundred thousand rupees. Arumugam could not manage the opposition. Further agitation was launched over the labourers who dug the iron-bearing soil and their working conditions. Unable to bear the trouble, Arumugam sold the factory to Sellappan.

Sellappan first changed the building's colour to a soothing green.

“How is it? Cool and pleasant to look at, is it not?” he asked.

“Yes,” people said. The town began talking about it.

“Whatever else one says, Sellappan is clever. Previously that building looked like a prison, painted ochre. A worker entering would feel afraid. Only now is it attractive: parrot green!”

“Is that all? Previously only one person at a time could enter because the doorway was so narrow. Look now: an elephant can enter comfortably. He has remodelled it so.”

“Arumugam is a miser. Sellappan is not. He spends freely and is willing to let profit come slowly. Arumugam would milk a cow in one rush until he reduced it to a skeleton.”

Thus talk spread that the factory's transfer to Sellappan had brought a new change and better conditions. Enlarging the doorway and repainting had cost little. Labour for painting cost only one hundred rupees, and the paint cost nothing.

A newly established paint company had sought Sellappan's favour and asked him to serve as its agent. “We shall see,” he said. “First both I and the town must know the quality of your paint. Paint some large building as a demonstration.” The company pleaded with him to arrange such a building. Magnanimously, Sellappan showed them his factory. That was how he obtained the paint without expense.

When Sellappan decided to buy the factory, he approached one of his more capable relatives.

“You have left the village council to all sorts of fellows. That will end in great danger. Anyone enters the council and taxes everything. They will call common land council property. It has remained under your supervision for generations, and you obtain something by dry cultivation there. All that will be lost. Do not let the council go. Put our own men in and capture it. When a Collector or Governor visits, will he summon and speak to the council president, or to you? You must stand aside while a man indebted to you shakes the Governor's hand. Shame! Can this be allowed? What great expense will it involve? If you have no heart for it, leave it to me; I shall meet every expense.”

Thus the council came under Sellappan's control. An accusation was spread that those who had passed the old resolutions had accepted bribes from foreign merchants. This cost only ten rupees. The resolutions were cancelled and the town's opposition to the works disappeared.

Sellappan began visiting the factory frequently with new people. Talk spread that it would expand and provide work for at least ten thousand.

“Dormitories must be built, according to the doctor. A worker's labour is the true capital. What can money do? Can it dig earth, melt iron, strike with a hammer, or turn a wheel? Only labour can do these things. Only if workers are robust can one obtain the full benefit of their labour.”

“Building quarters will cost a great deal.”

“What if it does? Rent repays it. Land has many expenses. What expense does a house have? Once built, rent comes by itself every month.”

“Will the workers pay regularly?”

“Why ask them? I am here. I shall pay the rent, deduct it from their wages, and settle the account.”

“With that arrangement it is not bad.”

“Not bad? What a generous lord you are! Have you no heart even to admit that the capital will yield a proper profit? What a miserly man!”

“Oh, nothing like that. According to your plan...”

“How many houses do you propose to build? Land may be rather difficult to obtain.”

“Where shall I go for land? You must give me ground beside the factory.”

“There it goes! You have placed your hand inside my very garment. If I sell the adjoining land because I desire your money, where shall I go if the factory must expand tomorrow?”

“Do not say that. You have about two hundred acres here. I do not need much; twenty or thirty will suffice.”

This conversation returned to Sellappan half the money he had spent buying the works.

A man came to run a workers' hotel: five thousand from that. Competitors came seeking a site for a cinema hall in the hope of enormous profit: ten thousand from that. By such routes, before the factory even began operating, Sellappan had received twice what he spent upon it.

Vilvam gathered materials to build the dormitories. Batcha Saibu began construction for the cinema. Their money had already reached Sellappan.

Seeing the commotion Sellappan had created, even the paint-company owner became tempted.

“No, you run it yourself,” Sellappan told him. “Pay me what I have spent together with a modest profit. Why should I possess so many industries? Many people must engage in enterprise. I liked you very much the first day I saw you.”

The factory changed hands, and the profit entered Sellappan's strongbox.

The dormitory builder and cinema operator now walked repeatedly to the paint-company owner. “When will you start the factory? Trusting you, we have spent heavily and fallen into debt. You must help us.”

The paint-company owner had no answer. The authorities had refused permission to operate the ironworks. All he could say was, “We shall see; go for now.”

“Had Sellappan retained it, the factory would be running by now. He is clever and would have made it work. This fellow intruded, greedily bought the factory, and Sellappan sold only because a great man asked. One needs ability to run such a thing. We have all lost because of him,” people said.

By merely making preparations as though to run the factory, Sellappan had earned nearly a hundred thousand rupees.

“Ellappa, why involve yourself in one new venture after another?” Sellappan asked kindly. “Running a business alone is difficult. Follow my advice.”

Ellappan felt terror merely hearing the proposal. In a trembling voice he said, “It is dangerous, and sinful too!”

“Fool, do not fear. I shall arrange everything. No danger will arise.”

“It is unfamiliar work. My mind will not permit it. Smuggling is a serious crime...”

“Only if you are caught! Do not panic. What must you do? Go to the place I name. Goods will arrive; receive them and place them where I specify. That is all.”

“I cannot escape the eagle eyes of the officials.”

“Pieces of meat will be thrown to the eagles. Why stare? Arrangements exist for all that. Nothing will go wrong. One needs only a little courage and sharpness. You know Menga, do you not?”

“The woman whom even the police decorated with a special medal for discovering a smuggling clue, identifying the criminal to the authorities, and performing her duty?”

“The very same! Good heavens, what photographs, songs, and praise appeared in the papers!”

“She performed her duty courageously. The smuggler had brought sixty watches, they say.”

“Yes. I had ordered sixty; they were confiscated, costing me three thousand rupees.”

“What are you saying? The smuggling was your arrangement?”

“One should not discuss such matters in detail. Still, I shall tell you so that courage and confidence in me may arise. Menga won a medal and reward by informing against the watch smuggler. His name is Singaram, and he is my man. He brought the watches according to my arrangement, and Menga knew it. She too worked for my organization. You are about to ask why she betrayed Singaram. That too was my arrangement. Surprised? The detail will make it clear.

“I sent both of them. Under my plan, Singaram smuggled the watches and Menga exposed him to the authorities. I knew an officer was following them as they travelled to Ceylon. Precisely for that reason, I told Menga to quarrel with Singaram in a hotel there. Menga normally speaks as though honey flows from her lips; that day her speech was a scorpion. Even Singaram was startled for a moment, and the officer observed it.

“He concluded that they were enemies.”

“Of course. Anyone would. Singaram raised his walking stick as if to strike her, and the bold woman removed her shoe to hit him. What else could the officer conclude but enmity?

“They completed their assigned tasks and embarked. Before they landed here, Menga excitedly approached the officer with a secret: 'Watches are hidden in Singaram's walking stick. I saw the smuggling with my own eyes. The rogue deserved a beating with my shoe! You cannot imagine the insolence of that smuggler. I happened to enter a hotel to eat, and the brute approached to flirt with me. I flared up; he raised his stick. I meant to destroy his dignity, but the fellow escaped. A short while ago, in a secluded part of the ship's deck, I saw what looked like a screw in his stick. He unscrewed it and inserted watches. I had one urge: I must report him. I also feared that he would take revenge, but resolved to perform my duty courageously.'

“You have never spoken with Menga. If she calls yellow black, you will believe her. Such sweetness and grace! A voice like music and arguments like a song that never misses its beat. The officer was delighted. For three months not one bird had fallen into his net, and his superior raged that he did not know how to cast it. He thought himself fortunate to have caught one bird. Singaram was arrested.

“Now see the marvel. Menga received police protection! Senior officers took her in a police vehicle, delivered her safely to her lodging, saluted, and left. Newspaper men photographed her after asking her to smile. Fools! Menga always smiles; does she require a photograph to do so?

“After the officers departed, Menga removed her shoe, placed it in a box, and sent it to me. You are astonished, fool! Did you imagine Menga's shoe was ordinary? A diamond worth a hundred thousand rupees was concealed inside. Smuggling! That was why I sent her.

“By exposing Singaram she won the officer's goodwill and affection. The authorities trusted her. The very Menga whom they ought to have arrested and searched for the smuggled diamond received their protection and hospitality! A hundred-thousand-rupee diamond in the shoe. Three thousand lost through confiscated watches; a hundred thousand profit for me. Praise, medal, and newspaper photograph for Menga. Do you see how flawless our arrangement is? What fear remains?

“Here is the address and this is the code. The goods will come; receive them. Vegetables are needed at a religious feast, are they not? Diamonds will be sent inside pumpkins and bottle gourds. The feast is for ascetics of the Sankara monastery: my charity! Once the vegetables arrive, my man in the kitchen will secure the diamonds while the devotional singing continues. I shall come for worship. The head holy man will place rudraksha garlands around my neck. Police records call him Karuppaiyan; dressed in ochre, he is known as Periyasami.

“I will not call it a salary of one thousand a month for doing this work. Regard it as a gift. In four years you have changed jobs eight times. You also bear a bad name for never remaining anywhere.”

“That is not all, Sellappa. I have acquired the bad name that nothing I touch prospers.”

“Here you will certainly succeed. Success in this trade is assured.”

“You cannot say so. It has already failed.”

“Failed? What has? Why? What are you saying?”

“I mean the arrangement to send me to receive smuggled diamonds has failed. You wish only to give me work, and I too want to enter some profitable occupation. I was delighted that you offered such an opportunity. But...”

“But what? But...”

“Different work has arrived for both of us: for me, the work of arresting you; for you, the work of stating the truth and surrendering to the police. Do not be startled, Sellappa. I am the senior detective officer working in this region. That is my permanent employment. To assist it, I occasionally take other jobs. You mentioned eight workplaces: true! I remained in each only until I obtained the information required there. Why stay afterward?

“You said nothing I touch prospers. Do you know the truth? My permanent work is investigation and catching criminals red-handed. That was my work in all eight places. Rise without creating trouble. The police vehicle is ready. Menga is inside it too.”

Sellappan was stunned. Ellappan, who had behaved like an innocent, had trapped the man who had escaped every other net.

“He has lost this job too! He has gone to another town. Who would give work to that sleepy-faced fellow? We pitied him because he was poor and employed him, but a man needs at least a little briskness and intelligence!” people at the workplace said of Ellappan as he departed for another town with his bundles.

“Poor” Ellappan went to take another job elsewhere, this time as advertising officer for a chewing-tobacco company.

There was information that Sivalingam of the chewing-tobacco company conducted large-scale opium smuggling. Ellappan was going to investigate, wearing the guise of a poor man.

14 January 1962

## Translator's Notes

The title “ஏழை” means a poor man and also carries the patronizing sense in which others dismiss Ellappan as pitiable or incapable; the ending exposes poverty and dullness as his investigative disguise. The source spells the celebrated informer as both மேங்கா and damaged variants; “Menga” is used consistently. “சீவல் பாக்கு” denotes prepared sliced areca or chewing mixture and is rendered descriptively. Business, fraud, bribery, smuggling, police, and criminal claims belong to the fictional narrative. Several heavily damaged lines in the ironworks agitation are translated conservatively from their surrounding argument and are logged separately.

"You went about town dressed so grandly that one would think you were taking up a Collector's post. And in the end, brother, they say all you have got is a clerk's job!" Kamini teased as she adjusted her curls.

"You monkey! It is enough that I have found a clerk's job. Just wait and see what sort of ladle comes your way. Are you making fun of me?" Kachabakesan retorted.

"Ladle or funeral pot, indeed! With my daughter's beauty, why, she might get even a Governor," said Perundevi, mother of the young pair Kamini and Kachabakesan.

"Who knows? Go on," said Paralinga Gurukkal as he adjusted his ritual bag. He was the priest of the local temple.

"Father, have they asked me to report at the office today itself?" Kachabakesan asked.

"See whether the day is auspicious," said Perundevi.

"If the mind is good, every day is a good day. Besides, after the office has issued its order, can he sit looking for an auspicious day and star? Look for a good omen and send him," said Gurukkal, and left for the temple to bring back a bunch of fruit hanging in its garden.

"Brother, tell me without getting angry. What work will you have there?" Kamini asked.

"There will probably be a clerk's job in some section," Kachabakesan replied thoughtfully.

"Whatever work they give you, what does it matter? Behave cleverly and earn the goodwill of your superiors; then your position will rise of itself, step by step. Ananthachari's elder son-in-law, whom I know, entered service merely as an attendant. Now he is said to be a manager. There is such a thing as luck, you know," Perundevi said.

"It was not luck, Mother. It was the appam-vadai and buttermilk curry made in their house that turned an attendant into a manager," Kamini said.

"What nonsense are you babbling, you silly girl?" Kachabakesan rebuked her.

But Perundevi said, "Hush. What she says is true. A certain Englishman who was General Manager in that office was apparently very fond of appam-vadai and buttermilk curry. Ananthachari and his people learned this and supplied the Englishman with them every week without fail. The man's position rose rapidly."

"Find out what your superior likes. We too shall do what was done in Ananthachari's house," Kamini teased.

"They say my superior longs to bite off and eat your nose. Shall I make the arrangements?" Kachabakesan returned.

"Enough, enough play. Be off," Perundevi said. She watched for a good omen and sent her son to the office.

Govinda!'" his wife Umayal consoled him.

Their son, Kodandapani, B.A., was adjusting the buttons of his coat.

Kodandapani had then been the son of a wealthy household and had sometimes freely paid the temple priest's son's expenses at the cinema, theatre, and hotel.

They had time to say no more. Karan Singh, a refugee who had become an attendant, seated them in the office corridor and ordered them not to talk.

Inside, Ulaganatha Mudaliar was in torment. The responsibility of deciding in which section each new clerk should be placed had fallen upon him.

There were two important sections. He managed one; Mathurbhootham managed the other.

Kachabakesan, B.A., was a Brahmin boy. For one moment Ulaganathar thought it best to send him to Mathurbhootham. The next moment he imagined Mathurbhootham asking, "Why, Mr Mudaliar, do you dislike even the very scent of a Brahmin?"

Mathurbhootham had a habit of pouring out dangerous implications under the guise of playful talk. Very well, Ulaganathar thought, I shall keep the boy in my own section. The next instant he imagined Mathurbhootham saying, "The Mudaliar found himself a fine, sturdy Brahmin and kept him there, it seems." The man was a born troublemaker. Ulaganathar was bewildered.

At last he retained Kodandapani in his own section and sent Kachabakesan to Mathurbhootham.

For ten days neither superior had reason to take particular notice of the new clerks.

Mathurbhootham often went into Ulaganathar's room to discuss the affairs of the world.

"Why do you say that? I have neither hatred nor anger towards The Hindu. I do not have the time to read newspapers at leisure as you do."

"See, see! There is a barb in the way you say it. You mean that you attend earnestly to office work while I read the paper, pass the time, and draw an idle salary. Is that what you think, Mr Mudaliar? Mudaliar-sir!"

"You ought to have become a lawyer. Say one thing and you find eighteen meanings in it and cross-examine a man."

"Let that be. Your leader's speech appears in The Hindu today. That was why I asked whether you had seen it."

"What new bomb are you throwing now with 'my leader' and 'your leader'? Who is my leader?"

"Sir A. Ramaswami Mudaliar."

"Why should he be my leader? Those men are politicians. What connection have we with them?"

"Whether or not you claim one, Sir Ramaswami says there is a connection."

"What? Does he say, 'I am Ulaganathan's leader'?"

"Not Ulaganathan's alone. Uthandan, Kodandan, Kandan, Kathan, Katteri, Kaveri, and all such people - that is, every non-Brahmin - are said to have him as their leader."

"What a curse! Why do we need this needless trouble?"

"Mudaliar, you are hiding what is in your heart. Tell me honestly. Your opinion is that Sir Ramaswami is a great genius, is it not? You yourself have spoken so. I heard it with my own ears."

"Did I praise only him? I have praised Sir C. P. Ramaswami Iyer too."

"There you are, Mudaliar-sir! What did you say? You praise Ramaswami Iyer 'too'? 'Too'! The very word makes the hatred, hostility, and loathing you bear towards Brahmins perfectly clear."

"Do not pry my mouth open, invent quarrelsome meanings, and earn my curse. Speak of office business, or pleasantly tell me of the welfare and prosperity of your household. Why must we discuss politics?"

"My principle is that it is better to speak openly than to keep things inside and cut a man's throat. People call Mathurbhootham quarrelsome. I hear even you say so. I do not care. It is my habit to speak whatever strikes my mind."

"That is good. But I do not conceal anything in my heart. I make no distinction between Brahmins and non-Brahmins. It has never even occurred to me that this is a problem."

"Then the two are equal to you? You regard Brahmins and non-Brahmins in precisely the same way?"

"Yes. Why should there be difference, enmity, or rancour?"

"To put it briefly, you are asking, 'Hey, you Brahmin boys! Do you think you are superior? Are we not all the same?' Is that not what it means when you say you regard both equally?"

"Siva, Siva! What a mind you have! You turn meaning into disaster. Look here, why do we need idle chatter? Let any leader say whatever he likes. Our leader upstairs has ordered me to put six months' accounts in order within twenty-four hours. I am dying to finish it, and now you trouble me besides."

"You mean, in veiled language, that I am a talkative Brahmin, a quarrelsome Brahmin, a Brahmin who obstructs work. Is that it?"

"Rama, Rama! Can you never find a straightforward meaning in anything?"

Their conversations went on in this fashion.

Mathurbhootham freely and fiercely analysed political questions. He drew Mudaliar into debate and fearlessly condemned the faults of great leaders. Condemning the Non-Brahmin Movement gave him as much delight as sitting on the beautiful river sand on the night of Chitra Pournami and eating mixed rice. What flavour it had for him!

Mudaliar was forever afraid of saying something that might trap him. The two held posts of equal rank, yet Ulaganathar trembled. Mathurbhootham did not.

"He is an idiot!"

"This is nonsense!"

"He is a fool!"

"Men like him will die with worms in their tongues!"

In this reckless fashion Mathurbhootham spoke of members of the Non-Brahmin Movement, however exalted their position.

Ulaganathar acquired the name "Scowling Face." Mathurbhootham acquired the name "the clever man."

"Ulaganathar is terribly slow. If he begins a task at ten in the morning, it will not be finished by six in the evening. It may take until eight. He is that sluggish," people said.

"Do you know how brisk Mathurbhootham is? He rattles through office work in half an hour and never misses a music or dance recital in the evening. He is an expert at his job. One glance at a file and he understands it. However large the matter, he never worries his mind over it. He has tremendous subtlety," they praised him.

"You boast that you know everything and that nothing lies outside your knowledge, yet you are a perfect cipher even about your own office. Shame! What kind of man are you?" Bhagavathi, Mathurbhootham's second wife, rattled at him.

"Why, dear, in what matter am I a perfect cipher?" Mathurbhootham coaxed.

"Enough. Is childhood returning?" she asked, allowing his hand to rest upon her cheek. "Do you not even know who works in your office? What work can you be doing without knowing even that?"

"Why, I do the General Manager's work. Even he would not dare question me so boldly."

"Let that pass. A young fellow joined your section recently, did he not?"

"Yes. Ten days ago. Like a frog in a well."

"Let him be a frog in a well or a crocodile in a pond. Do you know who he is? Do you at least know he is a Brahmin boy?"

"Would I not know even that? It is because I knew that I corrected all his bad English myself without humiliating him."

"Do you not know whose son he is?"

"Whose household?"

"During Navaratri kolu at our house last year, a girl came and sang - rather tall, slender..."

"So many young girls came to sing and dance. Who can remember?"

"This one had curly hair and spoke very spiritedly. She must have called you 'Uncle! Uncle!' a hundred times."

"Yes, as if my heart would rejoice merely because these girls call me uncle!"

"Is that alone enough to delight your heart? I know what it takes. Never mind. The girl's name is Kamini. They are very close to us. This boy is her elder brother, the B.A."

"Is that so? The silly fellow does not even stand straight before me and speak. Can shyness go so far?"

At another house Ulaganathar was saying, "You should not come here like this, Naidu-sir. You once held a high position. Do not imagine I speak to wound your feelings.

"Times are generally very bad now. Since your son is in my section, if you come to see me the world will talk wrongly. People will say, 'Naidu and Mudaliar are friends; Mudaliar must have held out his hand for something, for the boy works under him.' Why invite that trouble? It might even become dangerous. So please stop coming to see me here. I survive at the office as though each day threatens death while life somehow continues. Five years remain before my pension. Do not throw dust into its mouth. Besides, what is there to discuss? Do I have the authority to add five or ten rupees to your son Kodandapani's salary?"

"Mudaliar-sir, forgive me. I said this was what would happen. My wife pestered me into coming. I will not come again. Things will proceed as God permits."

"God will leave you with no want. The young man is orderly too, which gives me the greatest satisfaction. But if I say even that openly, people will give it some dangerous interpretation. They will say, 'These men are all of the non-Brahmin community, one race; like joins like,' and bring calamity upon my head."

"Rama, Rama! Let no such trouble come to you through me. I did not come because I wanted my son's position raised. In these times it takes a Brahma's effort for each person merely to look after his own affairs.

"Though we have known each other many years, I came only so you could never one day say, 'Did Naidu ever visit me and ask me to look after his son? Perhaps he thought it beneath his status.' There is no other reason."

"Do I not know your dignity, honour, and former position, Naidu? Could I lack regard for you? It is all in my heart. Must one pour it outside?"

"That is enough, Mudaliar. Even if my son were given a salary of a thousand rupees, I would not feel this happiness. I take my leave."

Thus Bhagavathi warmly recommended Kachabakesan to Mathurbhootham, while Ulaganatha Mudaliar fearfully told Sakalaguna Naidu that it was improper even to visit him at home about office affairs and sent him away. He trembled lest showing concern or goodwill endanger his own post. But Mathurbhootham - what had he to fear? The next day Bhagavathi's sweet words began to bear fruit.

"Hey, fool! Come here."

"What is it, sir?"

"You are a Brahmin, are you not? You idiot, are you not a Brahmin boy?"

"Why do you ask like this, sir? What doubt can you have? I am a Brahmin."

"Though you are a Brahmin, why is your intelligence so wooden? You have worked here ten days. Could you not have told me you were a Brahmin and come and spoken to me? How are you going to prosper?"

"I felt a little shy."

"Shy? Why, blockhead? What does your father do?"

"Father... he..."

"Why hesitate? What is his occupation?"

"He is a gurukkal at the temple."

"You simpleton! Why be ashamed to say it? Would an Englishman be ashamed to call himself a bishop or priest? Why should we be ashamed to say that one performs a priest's service?"

"There is no respect for it these days."

"We must create respect, blockhead! Create it. Whether a rich Chettiar earning lakhs or a man commanding hundreds and drawing thousands in salary, must he not seek your father's favour before he can obtain darshan? Your father performs such noble service. Should you announce it proudly or mutter it in shame? It is on seeing people like you that fellows mock us with talk of ritual bags, mendicancy, and topknots. Never again go about thinking your father's work lowly."

"Many people do not possess such breadth of understanding, sir. They speak contemptuously."

"They will. If only Rajaji had not needlessly introduced an education scheme and bought trouble, and had ruled the State another ten years, the Brahmin-haters would have learned their lesson. What can one do? Rajaji is a very wise man, but he acted without foresight. Creatures that ought to have remained in a corner have gathered up their hair and emerged."

"Yes, sir. Many of our people were very sad when Rajaji stepped down."

"Yes, yes. Now what can I do for you?"

"I have no grievance, sir."

"Is it enough that you have none? The supreme goddess at home is cracking her whip: 'Our Kamu's brother works there; look after him,' she says. The order has been issued."

"I am very happy, sir. I have not even seen her."

"She says she must be about my age. It seems she has seen you. Very well. If I am to do anything for you, I must first give you the boot from my section."

"Oh! What is this, sir? You speak of helping and then say you must give me the boot."

"Yes. If I keep you here and favour you, people will accuse one Brahmin of helping another. These sharp-toothed fellows are now conducting their campaign to annihilate Brahmins. So from tomorrow you must work in Ulaganatha Mudaliar's section. I shall arrange it."

"Everybody says he has a scowling face."

"Why should you worry about his face? He is a very frightened man, terribly afraid of Brahmins. He will not dare wag his tail at you. While you work in his section, I shall choose the moment and make him recommend you himself."

"Who is this 'sir' you insert after every word? Do you know how fully Kamini calls me 'Uncle, Uncle'?"

"Very well, Uncle."

"Bravo, nephew! But though I have married twice I have no child. You are merely a nephew in name. Do not call me uncle imagining that you will obtain a girl in marriage."

Ulaganatha Mudaliar neither spoke as sweetly nor delighted Kodandapani. Simply because Kodandan was Sakalaguna Naidu's son, he feared that even speaking to him would violate office propriety and provoke danger, rumour, and a bad name. He therefore did not confer with Kodandan at all. The very next day he ordered him to work in Mathurbhootham's section and thought he had escaped a danger.

The following day Kodandan came under Mathurbhootham's supervision, while Kachabakesan began to keep watch over Ulaganatha Mudaliar.

"Mr Kacha..."

"Sir, I dislike being addressed in that vulgar fashion. Please call me Kachabakesan."

"Very well. Have you become familiar with the ledger?"

"No. I have no taste at all for it. Mathurbhootham himself said that correspondence was the work suited to me."

"Is that so? He can tell in an instant what work should be given to whom. He possesses an extraordinarily subtle mind."

"Mathurbhootham often says that the standard of the office would rise if you too worked a little more briskly and boldly, sir."

"Does he? Very well, very well. Attend to your work."

This was how the association between Ulaganathar and Kachabakesan began.

"Megathandan... Who in the world is that? You? Oh, Kodandan! Does 'Kodandan' mean you are a Konar, Chettiar, Mudaliar, or Reddiar? I cannot find a single wretched suffix attached to your name."

"Naidu, sir."

"Oh! A Naidugaru! Dear me, you come of a line that ruled kingdoms. How will you work submissively under me?"

"It is nothing like that, sir. You may ask in Ulaganathar's section."

"Ulaganathar is one of your own people, my boy. I am a Brahmin - a 'Pappan.' If you work meekly under me, your self-respect will be ruined."

"I have no such thought."

"Very well. But you know such ideas are being discussed in the country now, do you not?"

"I do, sir."

"You do? How? Kodandam, are you connected with that crowd?"

"No, sir. I do not know any of them."

"You are safe so far. Look here. I always speak with a smile. But if I detect even a drop of this thing called self-respect in you, I shall finish you - finish you completely. Yes. What newspaper do you read?"

"Who, sir? Me?"

"Your father? What an utter fool you are! Of course I am asking you. What newspaper do you read?"

"None, sir."

"Did you acquire all wisdom with your B.A.? Do you not read even a newspaper?"

"The Hindu comes to the house opposite. I read it on Saturdays."

"Who lives opposite?"

"A man named N. V. Natarajan."

"Who, that chatterbox who talks of Aryan and Dravidian?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then say that the Aryan-Dravidian poison has entered you too."

"No, sir. I never discuss any of that with him."

"Why not?"

"Those people know a great many things. What can I say to them?"

"Those people know a great many things? Is he an Alladi or a T.T.K.? Look at the conceit! 'They know many things,' indeed. What do those fellows know? They know how to raise barren cries about Aryan and Dravidian, Brahmin conspiracy, and northern domination. What else? What do they know of history, geography, learning, or science?"

"Yes, sir."

"You know they know none of these things. Have you ever condemned them?"

"No, sir."

"Why would you? Blood is thicker than water!"

"It is nothing like that, sir. I know nothing about politics and have no desire to learn."

"Tell me: are you Aryan or Dravidian?"

"Sir..."

"Why do you gape? Are you Aryan or Dravidian?"

"I am neither Aryan nor Dravidian."

"Neither? Then what are you, a lump of mud? Fool! Aryan or Dravidian? Answer me."

"I do not know, sir."

"You do not know? Do you not know enough to say that all this talk of Aryan and Dravidian is meaningless and malicious? Or are you unwilling? You have no mind to say it is wrong to speak of a division between Aryan and Dravidian. Let Aryans remain Aryans and Dravidians remain Dravidians, or let the wretched business be whatever it may: we are all human beings, human beings first and last. Do you not know how to say that?"

"How would I know all this, sir?"

"Bah! You know nothing. Go, go. Talk to anyone you please and ruin yourself; I do not care. But if you wag your tail in the office, you are finished. I shall destroy you. Yes."

The first assault itself was fierce. Thereafter, whenever Mathurbhootham called Kodandan, he opened a path to some quarrel.

"Hey, my Dravidian! Heroic Dravidian, come here! There is a Rangoon broom in the car on the street. Bring it to me. Ah, wait, wait. You may raise the cry, 'I shall not be an Aryan's slave.' There will be a Dravidian servant at the door; tell him to bring it.

"What is this, Kodandapani Naidugaru? Red and black cloth! Oh, the Movement? Hey, the Movement! Your serving this Aryan with folded hands will make all your leaders hang their heads. Why are you still here?"

Thus Mathurbhootham taunted him every day. There was no scowl upon his face, but every word was a barbed shaft. And all the work rested on Kodandan's head.

"Sir, Lakshmanan said he would look after the ledger."

"Why? What does Naidugaru propose to do, hold a royal durbar? You will attend to the ledger."

"And the letters?"

"You will do those too. Do not whine that there is no time. At your age I did five times as much work. Do you know that? It is not enough merely to wail, 'Aryan, Aryan.' One must have brains, boy, brains. Go. If necessary, take these files home and finish the work there.

"What are you thinking? That the Brahmin is loading work upon you as one loads a donkey? Only by enduring such hardship can you advance tomorrow. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

It was a severe trial for Kodandan's youth. His patience broke a little more every day. His parents counselled him: "Those who make you weep as they advise you are the ones who truly wish you well. The Iyer is making you work this way only to turn you into a capable man. Do not misunderstand him." His heart seethed, but his circumstances compelled him to bear even fire.

Meanwhile, Ulaganathar performed Kachabakesan's work in addition to his own. He feared that Kachabakesan might complain somewhere that too much work had been imposed upon him, bringing danger. The young man would sometimes ask on his own, "Shall I do that? Shall I attend to this?" Ulaganathar would say, "No, Mr Kachabakesan. I shall look after it," lest Kachabakesan's feelings be hurt.

Kachabakesan's family did not bless Mudaliar for this. Instead they cursed him: "What an evil mind that Mudaliar has! He is afraid that if he gives Kachabakesan work, the boy will acquire skill. He is plotting so that one day he can accuse him of being fit for nothing and say, 'I did everything myself.'"

The General Manager asked for a secretary who was young, a graduate, and brisk.

"What is wrong with Damodaran?"

"Nothing at all. But the General Manager has not the strength to converse with him. Damodaran is so deaf."

"Whom, then, did you recommend?"

"I? I recommended nobody. But I recommended that the responsibility of deciding the matter be left to you, and he agreed."

"Why give me that trouble? Whom am I to recommend? Why needlessly earn the ill will of so many people?"

"I have always liked recommending you as a man of great responsibility. Why struggle? You are like one who holds butter in his hand and cries out for ghee. You have spent these six months preparing that fellow Kachabakesan. Recommend him."

"Kachabakesan?"

"Yes. What is wrong with him? Oh, I forgot: he is a Brahmin! So even if he has merit and ability, you must not recommend him. Is that it?"

"What malicious talk is this? Have I ever said such a thing? A thousand people in town may talk; am I responsible for all of them?"

"We shall see. This will reveal whether you too belong to that destructive crowd or whether you are an honourable man bound by justice and integrity. We shall see."

Kachabakesan, B.A., was appointed secretary to the General Manager. Kamini went to "Uncle" and offered him a thousand crore prostrations.

"I have grown old. Otherwise I would have said I did not need these thousand crore prostrations and asked you for a single kiss," Mathurbhootham said.

"Sister, look at his desire!" Kamini said. She bestowed a meaningful smile upon Mathurbhootham and went away. That wicked smile tormented him throughout the week.

"Uncle, have you seen this?" K. Kesavan, B.A. - Kachabakesan - asked familiarly, handing Mathurbhootham an English journal. In it Kachabakesan had written an article entitled "Casteism and Indianism."

It is wrong to speak forever of caste; it is also wrong to speak in support of caste, and foolish to say caste must continue to exist. Yet it is not wise merely to condemn caste as folly and cruelty. Great men, the noblest of Bharata, created caste for proper reasons, and it yielded many good results. The finest of these results is Indianism. The cultured course is therefore to remove casteism without ruining Indianism.

Thus the article twisted, wandered, stuck, and muddled its way forward.

Mathurbhootham was transported with delight when he read it.

"Bravo, bravo! What a great intellect you are! Though you work in an office, you have not become a frog in a well; you have been conducting research into the country's problems. Caste abolition is our General Manager's very life. He is a great reformer. He has married a Parsi; his eldest daughter married an Indo-Chinese man; and he himself is a Brahmin from Kannauj. He will experience divine bliss when he sees your article," Mathurbhootham praised him lavishly.

"He saw it, read it, and praised it! Besides that, he gave me permission to attend the Conference for Research into the Culture of Humankind, with a cheque for six hundred rupees," Kachabakesan said.

"What I said earlier I say again: I was not fortunate enough to make you my nephew in truth. May you live a long life," Mathurbhootham blessed him.

"A short life! A short life! Caste is apparently an Aryan conspiracy, a scheme to destroy Dravidian society! A great researcher, indeed! Fool! A conceited idiot who does not know how to survive!" Mathurbhootham cried angrily three months later, on seeing an article Kodandan had written in a weekly.

He read its conclusion: "Down with Brahmin conspiracy! Down with Aryan domination! Down with caste! Long live social equality!"

He picked up his pen and rapidly wrote a report to the General Manager:

1. The clerk named Kodandan is irregular in his work.
2. He always arrives late.
3. He talks back to his superiors.
4. He takes office files home and misuses them.
5. Contrary to office rules, he has joined a communal political party.
6. He raises slogans demanding social equality and, under that pretext, supports the Communist Party.

Mathurbhootham prepared such a list and manufactured and attached evidence for every charge.

The journal that published the article had a red cover. Inside it was a picture of Lenin. "Those two facts are sufficient," said the General Manager. He issued an order dismissing Kodandan from his post.

Kodandan grieved and grew angry, but within a moment shame drove away even his anger.

"Shame! How cowardly I have behaved merely to preserve a wretched clerk's job! How utterly without pride I have been!"

The secretary's post carried a salary of one hundred and fifty rupees, a separate allowance when travelling out of town with the General Manager, and many other privileges. Who would obtain it? Kodandan? Mathurbhootham met Ulaganathar and spoke to him.

"Mudaliar, do you know the General Manager has set an examination to determine whether all your long-standing talk comes merely from your lips or from the fullness of your heart?"

"What new danger is this?"

"He asked me to find him a secretary. Guess whom I recommended."

"Sundarathaiyar?"

"Is he some great prodigy? Why do you ask merely because he is a B.L.?"

Kodandan continued his reflections:

"Afraid and ever more afraid, we kept our mouths shut, unable to say even what our minds knew to be right.

"We thought we must deal carefully with a Brahmin officer and never let him detect the slightest Dravidian community feeling in us, for if he learned of it he would destroy us. So we remained submissive.

"How many times did he speak contemptuously of and abuse the Dravidian Movement? Did we answer with even one word?

"To protect a job we degraded ourselves and lived as a Brahmin's slave. What benefit did we finally obtain? The sinner has ruined us!

"The job is gone. Never mind. At least now we can find a way to live with honour and pride and do what we can with a consciousness of our people.

"This is merely the punishment for having lived all this time without that consciousness. That is all!"

Kodandan did not merely think these things. He explained to Ulaganatha Mudaliar why a consciousness of their people had to awaken in him too.

"Sir, as long as we have the mentality of washing these Brahmins' feet and drinking the water, our people will never advance," Kodandan said. Ulaganathar was terrified.

"What if one holds a job, sir? One must work properly and efficiently, without seeking bribes or illicit favours. Is that not enough? Must our people become the shoes on a Brahmin officer's feet? Why live so degradingly, sir? Are there no other ways to make a living? Do we lack the strength to labour?" Kodandan spoke courageously, in the new state in which understanding had blossomed.

The order dismissing Kodandan was typed by Kachabakesan himself.

To demonstrate that he was a friend, Kachabakesan gave Kodandan a copy even before the office copy arrived.

"Uncle, who is to get that place?" Kamini asked.

"Who? You tell me," Mathurbhootham said, catching her hands and playfully pulling her towards him.

"Is youth returning? Come and have coffee," Bhagavathi called, preventing any mischief.

14 January 1956

## Translator's Notes

The printed title is read as “மேலதிகாரி!” despite severe OCR damage in the title line.

The story uses caste-marked colloquial Tamil and intentionally offensive forms such as “பாப்பான்” in dialogue. They are retained in translation as historical characterization, not endorsed terminology.

“இன உணர்ச்சி” is rendered contextually as “consciousness of our people” rather than with the modern biological sense of “race.” In this story it denotes Dravidian communal or collective political consciousness.

The apparent name “K. Kesavan, B.A.” in the OCR occurs where the narrative clearly continues with Kachabakesan and is treated as an OCR-damaged or abbreviated reference to him.

The source sequence deliberately interrupts the discussion of the secretary’s promotion with Kodandan’s reflections after dismissal; that printed order has been preserved.

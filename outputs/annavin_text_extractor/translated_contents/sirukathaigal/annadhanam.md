## English Translation

“It is his way of making a living, sir! That is all.” This remark was forever springing from my friend. Temple, tank, consecration, festival, ritual, spirit-medium performance: whatever the undertaking, he applied one common name to it, “a livelihood.” To use his own harsh language, a shameless, exploitative livelihood!

He imagined that his speech possessed substance because it contained so much spice. I did not agree. Was I an atheist like him? I was a Saivite, born into a noble family that had always made room for religious fellowship.

Once my friend Nagarajan became an atheist, he formed the habit of asking the reason for everything and condemning everything. He never failed to supply reasons for his condemnation, but I did not listen very closely. You may ask, “What oddity is this? You offer no reasons yourself, yet do not attend to the reasons that atheist gives.” What could I do? Compared with my faith, my friend's argument never seemed so important. Sometimes, when I had decided that something was excellent and worthy, Nagan would say that the very thing was unnecessary. I would even become angry. What use came of it?

A poor ascetic came to our village, walking unsteadily, with greying hair, modest speech, and a tranquil nature. As a village notable I received him. Perhaps he ate something in our house once. That was all. He asked neither for money nor for silk vestments and caused no trouble.

And how lovingly he behaved! The gardener's daughter lay bedridden. Her father had given her every medicine, but she only grew exhausted and the illness did not diminish. On the very day the “holy man” stayed at our house, the girl became dangerously ill.

“Her life is leaving her!” the gardener cried. My own heart trembled. The ascetic ran to his house, chanted over some sacred ash, and gave it to the girl. She regained consciousness. The gardener was about to fall at the ascetic's feet, but he lifted the man and pointed skyward: “Worship Him.”

Is it surprising that I gained some respect for the ascetic? I spoke with him, and he described his ideal: to establish an ashram in the village, cure the illnesses of poor people, and remove harms caused by spirits and demons. What fraud was there in that? When Nagarajan heard, he roared as usual.

“Welcome, holy soul!” he mocked me.

“Go away, wicked soul!” I returned with interest.

“Yes, they say some great dull-witted saint is staying in your house,” Nagan said.

“Do not speak impudently. That ascetic is not one who interferes in anyone's affairs.”

“My lord, forgive me,” he replied, again in mockery.

“Enough jesting. Do not needlessly call that man a great saint and hurt his feelings. The ascetic plans to establish a service ashram in our village,” I announced.

“Say that you plan to establish a service ashram for him,” my friend corrected.

“Yes, that is so.”

“That is why I called you a holy soul. How could a title fail to reach you now? The man who betrayed his elder brother became an Alvar; here you are, surrendering your reason and preparing to become a saint.”

My friend continued rebuking me. I did not prolong the conversation, for I knew the atheist's nature.

“Why waste the property your elders accumulated?” he advised. “The wealth of rich men like you is squandered upon idle ascetics, renunciants, and spirit-mediums, while the labourer is worn out. This fellow is some impostor. He must have committed an outrage somewhere and been beaten and driven away. He has come here to find fresh pasture. Do not waste your money.”

“Nagaraj, do not regard this ascetic as ordinary. They say he knows rites for longevity, prayers to the nine planets, sesame-fire offerings, prayers to Markandeya and Karkandeya, exorcism through Kala Bhairava, offerings to Ganapati, rites to pacify Time, and everything else.”

“Say what you will. He is a fraud, without doubt. If it is not known today, it will be tomorrow. What is that to me?”

He left.

Having decided to establish the ashram, I went to the nearby town to print a notice and entered the Vetrivel Press. Its manager began extolling the merits of his establishment. I listened patiently. He showed books, theatre advertisements, wedding invitations, and many other products printed there. He had stitched the samples into a volume as large as the Ramayana.

As I leafed through it, four notices caught my attention. They brought my friend Nagarajan before my eyes, opened those eyes, and turned me into an atheist. Here are the notices.

“Is that enough?” the printer asked. “Must I show still more examples of the Vetrivel Press's workmanship? Our notices are neat and delivered on time. We began in 1920, sir. He spoke after displaying the sample book of notices prepared at his establishment. I heard his voice, but my mind was elsewhere, fixed upon the four notices that had seized it.

“What is it, sir? Thinking? Show me the subject of your notice,” he asked and began reading the copy I had brought. I listened in embarrassment.

## My Original Notice

### ASCETIC'S SERVICE ASHRAM

Sadhu Saravanabhavanandar, who performed penance in the Himalayas for twenty years and obtained the boon of fulfilled wishes, has established a service ashram in our village for the public good. Protective charms will be given to those suffering from sorcery, witchcraft, magical attack, or demonic disturbance, and rites will be performed for planetary afflictions. There is no charge; each person may donate as desired. Herbal remedies will be given, and medicine applied for snakebite.

“That is it, is it not? How many thousand notices do you require?” asked the manager.

“None, sir. I shall not print that notice.”

“Our work will be clean, sir! Give us one chance and see,” he pleaded.

I did not know what to do. Then I took another sheet, wrote as follows, and ordered ten thousand copies.

## My Revised Notice

### DO NOT TRUST MASQUERADERS!

People of reason!

Do not trust masqueraders who call themselves ascetics, mendicants, or yogis; dress in ochre; claim knowledge of alchemy and magic; promise universal remedies and all-powerful tonics; profess to conduct food charity, ablution, and worship; or announce that they will establish ashrams and bestow grace. Do not trust them! Do not trust them! Do not trust them! Give them no money and do not fall at their feet.

Such masqueraders' work will be a fraud like that of Singapore Sokkan, who pretended to feed the poor, mixed opium into his Golden Complexion Tonic, fled when pursued by police, and then disguised himself as an ascetic in nearby Sundarapuram to deceive people by announcing a service ashram.

Therefore, intelligent friends, at least hereafter do not trust such hypocrites and be cheated.

Thus,

**One Who Has Awakened**

“What is this, sir?” the Vetrivel manager asked. “You brought an ashram notice, but now you have written another directly opposed to it.”

“Sir, in the sample book you offered as proof of your press's workmanship, I saw four notices about Sokkan's exploits. My eyes were opened. Those four notices printed at Vetrivel Press explained the history clearly: a scoundrel advertised that he fed the poor, deceived the town into granting him a title, used that publicity to make immense profit by selling tonic mixed with opium, and was finally pursued by police.

“The same Singapore Sokkan is now in our village, in my garden, disguised as an ascetic. I am an innocent man who knows no deceit. I believed his speech, agreed to establish a service ashram there, and came to print its notice. Only after arriving here did I discover his fraud. I am therefore printing a new notice advising everyone not to trust hypocrites.”

Before my warning could leave Vetrivel Press, the ascetic somehow learned what had happened and disappeared.

## Translator's Notes

“அன்னதானம்” is the religious gift of prepared food and is rendered “The Gift of Food.” The story exposes how such charity can be used as advertising and a shield for fraud; it does not condemn feeding hungry people itself. “துணாமானா” is a period colloquial rendering of “atheist,” while “ஆஷாடபூதி” denotes a sanctimonious hypocrite or impostor. Religious rites, medicinal claims, opium adulteration, robbery, and police allegations are presented as the fictional notices and narrator's account.

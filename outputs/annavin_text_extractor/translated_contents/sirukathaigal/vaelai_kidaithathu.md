## Wanted

*Our newspaper Sukkiran requires a special correspondent. The applicant must possess at least a B.A. degree and be able to read and write both Hindi and Tamil. Applicants must enclose five rupees with their application...*

"Have you seen this? Instead of sitting there blinking helplessly, look: an advertisement has appeared," Komalam said.

She handed that week's *Sukkiran* to Sadanandam, M.A., B.L., who was reclining in an easy chair with the *Sundara Kandam* resting upon his chest and staring at the Ravi Varma print on the opposite wall. Then, in a sweet voice, she began an alapana in Sankarabharanam.

While studying for his M.A., Sadanandam had taken Komalam's hand in marriage. With the help of her family, he had obtained his B.L. Komalam's father was a celebrated astrologer, yet he had failed to foresee that the son-in-law allotted to him would be a voiceless insect.

In court, Sadanandam could only doze. There was no one to give him a case.

Komalam was a beautiful woman with a fine singing voice. With the help of Radio Director Raghuraman, she received at least three engagements a month and earned one hundred to one hundred and fifty rupees. Listeners sent many letters praising her melodious voice and asking that her concerts be broadcast frequently. Raghuraman arranged them skilfully.

Whatever others may have felt, Raghuraman regarded Komalam's music as life itself. He would spend half an hour before each concert and three-quarters of an hour afterward praising her.

The conversation that began with, "Go away! I don't like such extravagant praise," developed within two months into:

"What can I do? I was not fortunate. A Brahmahatthi has come into my life. I too am afraid someone may see us. But will the heart listen? It has grown very late. Won't they search for me at home? You... do not worry about me."

Astrologer Jagannathachar somehow attended to the household's survival. He was family astrologer to many great households. Komalam's radio income paid for her adornment.

Sadanandam's father died before enjoying the fortune of seeing his son become M.A., B.L. His religious storytelling had been famous throughout the town, but the income was meagre. Sadanandam therefore had to live in Komalam's parental home. Fortunately for Komalam, she had no siblings to create trouble.

"How long can you remain without any work? Your legal practice is not going at all well. You must look for something else. Did the elders say for nothing that employment is the mark of a man?" Komalam often needled him.

Her words hurt Sadanandam. Yet what could he do? There was no falsehood in what she said, he thought, consoling himself.

"The advertisement says they need someone for a correspondent's job?"

"Yes. Would they advertise for someone to do your legal work? If you wish, place your own advertisement: 'Wanted: people to provide cases.'"

"Even if it is a correspondent's job, would you say I should accept it?"

"First go and ask whether they will give it. Only then need you think about accepting. You speak as though you presently hold a district judge's post."

"Why do you strip me of my honour like this? When I think of disappearing to some country where no one knows me, your father says I have 'water trouble.'"

"Apply Amrutanjan and the cold will disappear."

"Mischievous woman! By 'water trouble,' I meant the danger involved in crossing the sea. Fine. Now, even if they ask me to become the peon at the *Sukkiran* office, I will go. Why should you worry?"

"Look, they now offer a correspondent's job. If you work there and gain experience, could you not later be made editor? At first, radio gave me only B-class engagements. Now look, I am in A class."

"Who could possess your good fortune? Raghuraman says bundles of listeners' letters arrive whenever you fail to sing."

"What of him? He always speaks that way. Go directly to the *Sukkiran* office and see them."

"They ask for an application."

"They always write that. Go and meet the editor in person. Isn't he the brother-in-law of our Hemalatha? Don't you know him?"

"How many Hemalathas do you know?"

"I mean the one from the house in Echur."

"Komalam's husband? I am delighted. I will do what I can," the editor of *Sukkiran* told Sadanandam.

"But the proprietor of this paper, Menavana, is a complete fool. He has firmly ordered that anyone appointed as correspondent must first sit a test. You may say you are M.A., B.L., but he will reply, 'Why do we need a lawyer or doctor, sir? We need a correspondent.'

"Look at this. It is a report published in the newspaper *Maruthi*. Rewrite its contents with polish and make it entertaining. If he is satisfied, the job is certain. Otherwise, I can do nothing."

The editor cut out a short item from *Maruthi* and gave it to him. Offering his respects, Sadanandam walked home sadly.

Komalam was memorizing the song "Radha Sametha Krishna." Wearily, Sadanandam gave her the *Maruthi* clipping and explained the matter.

It read:

> Yesterday afternoon, the house-opening ceremony for Timmappa Chettiar's two-storey mansion was conducted splendidly. Among the dignitaries present, Pethanna Chettiar of Big Street was foremost.
>
> Timmappar received Pethanna Chettiar affectionately, seated him in the gathering like the Speaker of an Assembly, remained beside him attending to his needs, and invited him before everyone else to the feast. He stood nearby and personally served him while he ate.
>
> As Pethannar had urgent business, he departed. Timmappar accompanied him as far as the street, waited until he entered the car, shut its door, and saluted him with folded hands. The sight was one of supreme bliss. Only after Pethanna Chettiar had gone did Timmappar formally attend to the other guests.

Komalam read it twice.

"What are you expected to do with this?" she asked.

"Write it differently and make it interesting," Sadanandam said without enthusiasm.

"Go and sit down to write," she said.

Without resisting, he went and began. As time passed, the pile of paper he tore up grew, but the work did not reach completion.

Seeing Sadanandam's struggle, Komalam did not even display her usual anger.

"All right. Do not confuse your head. I will find a way," she said.

Sadanandam's face brightened.

"I was about to tell you the same thing. Ask Raghuraman. People say he is clever at all this," he replied.

"Who can equal Raghuraman's cleverness? A remarkable man!"

Only while she said this did Sadanandam notice the particular beauty that sprang across Komalam's lips. Foolish delight spread across his face, and he moved closer.

"What is this? Is youth returning?" Komalam teased.

Taking the *Maruthi*, she went directly to Raghuraman's garden house. He lived there alone and happened to be on leave from the office that day.

After half an hour of raga alapana, rhythmic sequences, and improvisation, she asked:

"Do you know anyone at the *Sukkiran* newspaper?"

"I know the proprietor himself. Don't I write its music section under the pen name 'Bhringi'?"

"That is excellent. Look at this."

"This is *Maruthi*. Yes, what is in it?"

"It reports how Pethannan was honoured at Timmappan's feast. This must be rewritten differently and made interesting. If it is submitted, they say they will give a reporter's job. I have asked him to apply."

"Is that so? There is some hidden point in selecting this particular news item. What could it be?"

"Will you have something to eat? You look very tired."

"I am not tired. If you need..."

"No, no. These days you become tired."

"Is that what you say? Wait here."

"First find a way to solve this. One cannot spend the time like a playful child."

"Your command, Maharani!"

Raghuraman thought for a long time. A new light appeared in his eyes.

"Ah! I have found it. I understand the hidden point," he said.

Komalam moved closer and eagerly asked, "What? What?"

"Sadanandam's job is certain. Give me five minutes."

He began writing rapidly.

"Excellent! Excellent! Call that man!" the proprietor of *Sukkiran* shouted happily.

Sadanandam came forward shyly. The proprietor patted him on the back.

"Excellent! You have exactly the right brain. This is how one must write to cut a man down. Three hundred rupees a month. From now on, your only job is to watch all of Pethanna Chettiar's activities and write reports. Issue the order and give him an advance!"

The editor danced with joy upon his chair.

The proprietor had just read the report Sadanandam brought. It said:

> Every important person in town attended the opening ceremony for Timmappar's "grand mansion." Like a ward against the evil eye, Pethanna Chettiar too appeared.
>
> On seeing him, Timmappar feared that Pethannan might mingle with the others, start talking, and create some disturbance. To prevent him joining anyone, Timmappar seated him in the hall within his own direct sight.
>
> Knowing that Pethannan was a glutton, Timmappar thought it best to feed him first and be rid of him. He dragged him inside. Afraid that he would confuse the cooks and devour sweets without limit, Timmappar personally served him in measured portions.
>
> Fearing also that Pethannan's continued presence would inconvenience and disgust many people, Timmappar led him out to the street as though asking, "Your purpose here is finished. After eating until your stomach nearly burst, what further business have you?"
>
> Wondering what he might do if delayed any longer, Timmappar himself opened the motor-car door, pushed Pethanna Chettiar inside, signalled the driver to leave, and, when the car departed, folded his hands as though saying, "That affliction is finally gone."
>
> He then invited the dignitaries to relax and attended to them with affection.

"Komu, there has long been hostility between this Pethanna Chettiar and the *Sukkiran* Chettiar. I know it. You may have heard too.

"There is a singer named Malliyur Alli. She was once with this Sukkiran man. Pethannan cast his net and caught her. That is how the feud grew. She comes here to sing and speaks freely with me, so I learned the matter.

"The newspaper *Maruthi* is run by people favourable to Pethannan. Therefore, it glorified his attendance at the feast. The test was to reverse the report. I realized this was the hidden point and rewrote the same news in a new form that humiliated Pethanna Chettiar. It worked. Sadanandam got the job," Raghuraman explained.

Komalam did not rejoice. She frowned.

"Are you still friendly with Malliyur Alli?" she asked.

"Foolish woman! She is old enough to be my grandmother. She must be forty," he said, and began the work of dispelling Komalam's anger.

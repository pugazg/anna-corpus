## English Translation

One evening, I sat before the seashore with my eyes closed, observing silence. Closing one's eyes, becoming silent, and dreaming are all quite natural, are they not?

"Did you see the Viceroy or not?" someone asked, slapping me on the back. I opened my eyes and turned around. My friend Sundaram, whom I had not seen for many months, stood there smiling.

"No, Sundaram. Did you see him?" I asked.

"Would he ever fail to see me? Only last week I wrote a first-rate editorial titled 'Willingdon the Hero.' Don't you know?" he replied.

I was startled. Had Sundaram said he was a Deputy Collector, I would have been ready to believe him. But when he claimed to be a newspaper editor, I could not believe him at all.

"Who wrote the editorial?" I asked.

"Good heavens! Don't you know the news?" Sundaram said by way of preface and began his autobiography. Just then, an acquaintance of mine passed by. I saluted him and displayed a few teeth in greeting.

"Who is that fellow?" Sundaram asked.

"He works in the Currency Office on a salary of six hundred rupees. He is an important man," I said.

"Hush! I cannot stand the sight of such people," Sundaram said, spitting in the direction the official had gone.

"Lucky fellows! They found jobs easily in the old days and now draw fat salaries. What work is available today? What employment? Having surrendered all the money I possessed to education, I began worrying about how to pass my days with barren land and a rough wife. After suffering for a long time, I finally decided to become a newspaper editor."

At this point, Sundaram paused his story to take a pinch of snuff. I wondered how he had suddenly become an intellectual. Then he resumed.

"So many newspapers! Newspapers whichever way one turns. Yet merely hearing the name of my paper would make it impossible for you not to buy it. I began it on a carefully chosen auspicious day. At the top of the page was a rooster. From its beak came the cry 'Kokkarako!' That was also the name of the paper. Leave aside the delight of the sound. I cannot describe the richness of meaning in the matters it published."

"Sundaram, one question. Was it a monthly, a weekly, or a daily?" I asked.

"Why should I tell you? Listen to the whole matter and decide for yourself. Unlike other newspaper fellows, I never wrote about the Reserve Bank Bill, the White Paper scheme, the British Empire, or the Sastri constitution. The important matters published in *Kokkarako* were startling incidents, astonishing mysteries that stirred the blood, and strange curiosities. Occasionally, however, I debated highly complex problems too.

"For example, I wrote an entertaining article under the title, 'Is One Head Enough for a Human Being?' I reported that Reverend Ramsmith had preached that it was a grave error for no one to have considered the question until then, and that intelligent people should discover its truth and explain it to the ignorant. That was enough. Throughout that week, *Kokkarako* published expanded lectures, interpretations, and investigations of the question. Then..."

"Yes, Sundaram, who is Ramsmith?" I asked.

Sundaram was so enthusiastic about telling the story that he did not notice my question. He continued:

"The following week, under the heading 'Our Apologies,' I published a clarification. Ramsmith had never raised the question, 'Is one head enough for a human being?' He had only said, 'The single head a human being possesses is enough. Intelligent people would do well to note this and tell the general public.' *Kokkarako* announced that the reporter who had sent the incorrect story had been dismissed."

Laughter welled up within me. I had never expected Sundaram to conduct affairs so skilfully.

"Splendid! Splendid!" I said, patting his back.

"The patting can wait, Sankara. Listen to the matter," Sundaram said, continuing his story.

"After toddling through childhood, *Kokkarako* reached youth. Many thousands of people would not sleep without seeing it. I continued publishing curious matters. What ingenious methods I used for that purpose!

"One day, I could find nothing unusual at all. Not even one page had gone to press. The hour advanced from three to four. The paper had to be printed by five. What would you have done in my position?"

Fortunately, before I could answer, Sundaram carried on with his autobiography.

"Listen, Sankara! I looked all around. Suddenly, an idea arose. I took up my pen and raised the question, 'What If Hitler Became a Butler?' The next day, I had my artist dress Hitler as a butler and published a picture of him carrying food to Europe amid food vessels, guns, bombs, and smoke.

"Sales became tremendous. I employed thirty-two B.A. graduates as reporters and parcel clerks. If you joined, I would make you the leader of all thirty-three B.A.s. My wife Maragatham managed the women's section very capably. I bought a lovely little bungalow and moved into it.

"In the next bungalow lived Sriman Karunai Pillai, leader of the Karthikeyan Devotees' Association and treasurer of the Kangani Union, with his wife Kamalambikai. Many times I approached him and asked, 'What is your opinion of my paper? Could you not contribute something to it?' I exhausted myself asking. So many important people had donated material. Why could this Karunai Pillai not possess even a mustard seed of compassion? I pined over it. But did I let the fellow go? Maragatham and I discussed the matter and devised a way to punish him.

"According to our plan, my wife befriended Kamalambikai. One day, the two met as usual and were gossiping. All at once, Maragatham began laughing uncontrollably. Kamalambikai looked embarrassed.

"'Why are you laughing? Could you not tell me the reason before laughing?' she pleaded.

"Maragatham stopped and said, 'It is nothing. My husband is very affectionate toward me. He never refuses anything I ask. Yesterday, I deliberately decided to test his love. I asked him to put on a skirt and dance. He did it without objection. It was very amusing. I laughed because I remembered it.'

"That evening, instead of going to the office, I hid with a camera in Karunai Pillai's garden. He and his wife came outside.

"'What foolishness is this? Must I dance merely because you desire it?' Pillai asked.

"Rubbing her eyes, Kamalambikai replied, 'Yes. If I say dance, you must dance. If I say sing, you must sing. Is the editor next door a fool? His wife asked him to dance, and he danced. Fortunate women receive husbands who never cross their wishes.' Then she began sobbing.

"'Very well,' Karunai Pillai said. He returned wearing a skirt and performed a dance.

"The dance was magnificent. A dark, heavy figure, a round belly, and a bald head: surely the dance must have been excellent! From my hiding place, I photographed him dancing. Then I suddenly appeared before him.

"'What is your opinion of Bharatanatyam?' I asked.

"The man froze.

"That day's *Kokkarako* carried his photograph under the headlines:

> **Karunai Pillai's Dance**  
> **Bharatanatyam Is an Ancient Art**

"For some reason, a strange desire came over me that day. I wanted to sell our paper myself. I immediately put on a disguise, stood at the Broadway corner, and shouted, 'Kokkarako! Quarter anna!'"

"Sundaram! Stop, stop. Leave your autobiography aside. I have never seen such a newspaper. I have not even heard of it," I said.

"How could you have seen it? You would know only if it were published in this town," Sundaram replied.

"Was it not published here? In which town do you publish it?" I asked.

"Don't you know the editor from Kilpauk?" a voice behind us said.

I turned. A policeman took hold of my friend's hand. Smiling at me, he led Sundaram away.

"Sanka! The Viceroy has sent for me because he wants to discuss something. I will go and return to see you tomorrow evening. Be sure to read today's *Kokkarako*," Sundaram said.

What a pity. Only later did I learn that my friend Sundaram had twice failed while running a paper issued three times a week, then lost his mind after launching a fortnightly, and finally become a patient living at Kilpauk.

## English Translation

Krishnan embraced Gomathi, who came bounding joyfully at the sound of his flute. He wiped the face of beautiful Meena and kissed her, praised the loveliness of Kamalam's eyes, lamented that Ambujam had not come, happily told Komalam that yesterday's anger was gone, and stroked Sundari's back. He embraced Kokilam as though he would squeeze her neck. Maragatham stood at a distance, afraid to approach, so he went, dragged her near, and kissed her.

Such was the power of Krishnan's flute. Its music created such delight. Holding the beautiful instrument to his lips, sending melody through the ear into the heart, he made Sundari, Suguna, Karuna, and Kamala swim in a flood of pleasure. Yet he was neither proud nor happy over the victory. He looked here and there as though eager to see something; his caresses were only occasional interruptions.

"Do not go, Bhuvana! Must I play still more? Come closer! Will you not? Very well, go. What if you leave? I shall call Manikkam," he said, walking towards her.

Then he saw something beyond the creepers covering the fence. Delighted, he played again. Leela, for whom his heart longed to play, was watching from the other side. In his joy he played with extraordinary sweetness and closed his eyes, absorbed in bliss. A few seconds later he opened them and saw a lizard moving where Leela had stood. She was already walking quickly away.

Disappointment and anger seized him. He struck Gomathi with his staff, slapped Meena's back, threw a stone at Sundari, seized Maragatham by the ear, dragged her into line, and drove them rapidly back to the village.

Yes: Krishnan worked in the herd belonging to the Tiripuram estate. Suguna, Sundari, Komalam, Gomathi, and the other beauties were cattle. Only Leela was not; she was the Zamindar's daughter. Krishnan intended his flute to excite Leela, not the four-legged creatures. But the hearts of the cows and that of the maiden were differently made.

Leela heard that the newly appointed herdsman was an expert flautist and that his sweet music plunged the herd into delight. One day she went near the pasture. The flute pleased her. She returned every day, and both its sweetness and Krishnan's skill began to grow. That was not all: his walk, clothes, and manner, once rustic, gradually became those of an actor dressed for a country scene.

That day Krishnan greatly desired to speak to Leela. But she had urgent work and left after listening only briefly. Once she had gone, what need was there for those unnecessary cows to remain? In that anger the troubled king of the herd drove them home.

Krishnan was unmarried, in the difficult stage of having to find a beloved. Other men washed the cattle sheds, fed the cows, disciplined kickers, and found mates for strong animals with curved horns. Krishnan was their chief. He accompanied the herd, poured out sweet music, and at night advised his heart, "Wait a little longer. Those who endure shall rule the earth."

He had a recommendation saying he possessed special knowledge and experience in cattle farming - no ordinary recommendation, but one addressed to the Santhipuram Zamindar. It secured the job. Krishnan's declaration that he required little salary was another important reason.

Once he became king of the herd, many reforms appeared. Before him the cattle bore uncivilised names such as Curved Horn, Stump Tail, Painted Eye, and Embracer. His first reform was to abolish them and bestow elegant names: Suguna, Sundari, Komalam, Gomathi. Another was a concert in the cattle shed every Saturday. The village mocked this strange idea, but he did not care.

"What does the occupation matter? Why call tending cattle degrading? I call myself king of the herd. It is no mean profession. Saiva doctrine says Maheswara himself is lord of us cows; Krishna, incarnation of Hari, was a cowherd. There are stories of human heads broken for the sake of cattle," he would say.

Some asked why an educated young man had entered this work.

"It is true I am educated. That is why I came," he calmly answered.

Villagers pitied him. Educated men faced such hardship, they said, and could find no employment; this poor young man had come to survive by tending cattle. If someone told the Zamindar a few good words about his education, perhaps he would be made an accountant.

"Fools!" Krishnan told the cattle hands. "A motor company offered me two hundred rupees a month as agent; I refused. A mill offered work; I declined. Idiots think I took this post from lack of sense. Why do I need employment? Could I not perform my art before assemblies instead of delighting horned creatures and become a musician with money pouring in? I sought this work voluntarily. These coarse fellows do not understand my plan, and I shall ignore them."

The village concluded he suffered a peculiar madness. For once, public opinion was related to truth. His obsession arose from a wonderful story heard by moonlight upon a sand mound beside the sea.

"Amirtham, have you finished that storybook?" Leela asked.

The sweetness in her voice delighted a young man lying with sand heaped beneath his head, his mind sent to England and his eyes closed lest knowing his actual surroundings break his pleasant evening dream. He opened his eyes and saw two young women talking. He directed mind and ear towards them.

"I finished it, Leela. What an extraordinary story! Your voice receives second place in sweetness only to it."

"What extraordinary story?"

"Do you know the heroine's name?"

"Was her name Extraordinary?"

"No, her name was Athsayam - Wonder."

"Then tell me the story of Lady Wonder, Lady Marvel!"

"What will you give me?"

"I shall ask your husband to give you a kiss."

"Go away!"

The young man struggled to suppress his laughter.

"Tell me."

"Shall I?"

"Shall I call an astrologer to find an auspicious hour? I ask for the story and you make such ceremony."

"Wait. It is a Greek love story."

"Did the Greeks name women Athsayam too?"

"What a profound question! Yogananda Sarathiar translated it into Tamil."

"Why must Yogananda Sarathiar translate love stories? Amusing. Go on."

"In a Greek town lived a princess named Athsayam. She was beautiful - like you, except that she had no parrot's beak."

"Are you telling a story or asking to be kicked?"

"I am describing her. Did the author finish with two letters meaning 'beautiful woman'? He wrote six or seven pages: rain-cloud hair, lotus eyes, bow-like brows, and every other beauty. Preparations began for her marriage."

"The old story. Her parents chose one man while she loved another. There was a quarrel; she wept."

"She wept and there was a quarrel, but not as you imagine. The palace pressed Athsayam to marry the man she herself desired. She refused. 'You must choose whom I should marry. I cannot seek a husband myself; it is improper and beyond me,' she said."

"That truly is strange. Say it again."

"They urged her to marry anyone she liked. She firmly insisted they arrange the match. When pressed, she declared, 'Even if he is hunchbacked, blind, crippled, or foolish, I shall marry whomever you choose.'"

"Was she mad?"

"No. Her father was mad! Before dying, the king summoned the minister, commander, and treasurer and gave them a charter: 'My daughter must marry a man selected by you. Only then shall she become queen. If she instead marries by her own choice, she shall lose the kingdom, and you may appoint another person, or one among yourselves, to rule.'"

"Then he was a purposeful madman. He knew all three were scoundrels. Fearing they would slander and trouble whomever Athsayam chose, he gave them responsibility for choosing. If they later maligned their own selection, the people would blame them rather than her."

"But the charter still allowed her to choose for herself."

"Yes, on condition she surrendered the kingdom. The king reasoned that if she truly loved someone, he would likely be a courageous prince. Sacrificing a kingdom for him would be no loss, and his bravery could recover it."

"Why assume she would love a prince? Is there a law that a princess must love a prince? Love then has no work at all. I dislike the story."

"Wait before denying it your medal. Usually a princess does marry a prince. Look at you: a Zamindar's daughter. Will you not marry another Zamindar's son?"

"That is where you are wrong. You understand neither me nor love."

"You have reached love's far shore! Will you marry your gardener's son instead?"

"Why only the gardener's son? If love arose, I would even marry the cowherd in our herd."

"Very well, great connoisseur of love. We shall see when your story happens. Hear what happened in Athsayam's. After the king died, the three became palace guardians. They plotted to make her marry by her own desire so she would lose the kingdom to them.

"They gave her poems on love, staged romantic dramas in the palace, and filled it with paintings of love. Yet no romance sprouted. She remained firm: they must choose her husband. They did not know she understood the charter.

"The minister would ask, 'Princess, do you admire the young musician who sang in the garden yesterday?'

"'I always respect musicians,' she replied.

"He would begin laying his trap: 'Would you marry...'

"'If that is your command, I have no objection,' she said.

"The three grew angry. 'Do not take our lives. How can we know such things? You give us a fine task - hunting a man!'

"Athsayam suggested summoning kings from many countries. Thinking she was caught, the minister proposed that she choose one. She smiled at his trick and said, 'I shall marry whomever you order me to garland.'

"'Why drive in a whole herd for that?' the commander complained.

"'Then you may select the suitable man without leaving one place,' she neatly replied."

"Did she truly love nobody?"

"What woman never loves? Everyone must. But how many can declare it? Even if they do, will events follow their wish? Elders' preference is still law even for clothes and jewellery. What can women do in love? Read storybooks."

"So that is how you satisfy yourself?"

"Enough teasing. You are satisfying yourself merely hearing it."

"Finish the story."

"The three desperately sought to complete the marriage. Then Athsayam truly fell in love. A handsome prince of the neighbouring kingdom saw her one day."

"Cupid's five arrows struck."

"Only one arrow struck - not Athsayam, but her pet deer. Nor did the prince shoot it. A guard patrolling the garden mistook the disguised prince for a thief and loosed the arrow."

"Was he so ugly that the guard took him for a thief?"

"I said he was handsome. He wore a disguise."

"Then say so."

"The deer was struck; Athsayam wept. Afraid his mistaken shot would cost his life, the guard fled. The disguised prince comforted her, gently withdrew the arrow, applied leaves to the wound, and began speaking words mixed with honey.

"'Enough! You trespassed into my garden and tried to kill my deer,' Athsayam said angrily.

"'I did not shoot. There is no bow in my hand or quiver on my back. Look,' he humbly replied.

"She looked. He had neither bow nor arrow, but possessed a smile that could conquer her and love shining in his eyes. Pleasure entered her heart. As both stroked the deer, their hands met, and each one's gaze began stroking the other's heart. Love was born - and worry with it, for marrying him meant losing her kingdom.

"'Who are you?' she asked.

"'A fortunate man.'

"'Your town?'

"'Born in Veenapuri; now living in Inbapuri - the City of Joy.'

"Admiring his speech, she asked, 'Are you a *kavi*, a poet?'

"'I have only two legs, my lady,' he replied.

"She did not understand. *Kavi* can also mean monkey; he joked that having only two legs, he was no monkey."

"Why speak so crookedly? He had a monkey's mind."

"His mind was sound. Men speak incoherently when they suddenly see a woman."

"True. Neither their looks nor words remain straight. Continue."

"They talked a long time and formed an agreement of love."

"To marry?"

"Yes. She reached that decision. He rejoiced but proposed keeping the love secret and humiliating the three fools properly. He would station an army at the border. She should continue pressing them to choose a husband. Eventually they would bring some man and try to bind him to her. Whatever he was like, she should consent and let the wedding preparations proceed. At the wedding pavilion the prince would disgrace all three and marry her. They sealed the agreement with kisses.

"The conspirators selected an ordinary soldier as a puppet husband, hoping to wield authority through him. At first afraid, he yielded to ambition. Athsayam accepted him and a date was fixed. She sent a palm-leaf message to her prince:

"'Danger approaches. Come save your servant. I do not want a kingdom, only the sweet life of living with you. If some thought prevents you from coming at the last moment, what becomes of me? I will not permit some passing deceiver and scoundrel to touch me. The wedding pavilion shall become a funeral pavilion. Do not forget: shall a garland be placed upon a monkey?'

"Four days before the wedding, the prince secretly met the bridegroom. He explained love's holiness, showed Athsayam's letter, and described his military strength. He convinced the soldier the wedding could never occur and promised a reward if he cooperated. The soldier recognised both the sacredness of the love and the prince's power and agreed.

"'Behave as though nothing happened. I will attend as your friend. Say yes to whatever I say and approve whatever I do; I will protect you,' said the prince.

"The grand wedding began. The priest loudly chanted at the auspicious hour. Athsayam entered as though walking upon fire. The bridegroom sat majestically, and the three conspirators sat like crowned kings, praising their own cleverness. Seeing her lover disguised as the bridegroom's companion gave her courage.

"'The auspicious moment approaches. Let it proceed,' said the minister.

"The prince asked the bridegroom, 'Your Majesty, did you not say you would garland the bride only after rewarding the three noble men who arranged this marriage?'

"Following their plan, he said yes.

"'Shall I bring the rewards?'

"'Yes.'

"The prince summoned servants, who soon brought three donkeys.

"Fury seized the three. Guests wondered at the spectacle.

"'Should these gentlemen not be mounted upon their rewards?' asked the prince.

"'Yes,' replied the bridegroom.

"Athsayam laughed and the people grew angry. Units of the prince's army, which had entered the town in small groups, restrained the crowd. The conspirators were forcibly placed and tied upon the donkeys. Confusion filled the pavilion, while the priest lamented the loss of his fee.

"The prince removed his disguise. Athsayam leaned upon him smiling, then explained the charter and conspiracy to the people. Their anger turned upon the three. On one side proceeded a royal wedding procession; on the other, a procession of dignitaries riding donkeys. The royal procession had the smaller crowd.

"The prince richly rewarded the soldier who had played bridegroom. Queen Athsayam and King Azhagesan ruled justly for many years. That is the story. But read its descriptions of love and women will go mad."

"Only women? Do men not go mad reading of love?"

"I am not defending men, fool. Women need to read about love to become mad. Men need not read; the sight of a woman produces madness automatically."

"That is better! A marvellous story. I too must read it. Even if the man agreeable to one's heart is a cowherd, happiness lies in living with him."

The story Amirtham told that evening was written by no Greek author and translated by no Yoganandar. It was her own invention.

Hearing it planted Krishnan's madness. Like the women, he was a college student. He had gone for his usual evening walk by the sea and overheard their lively conversation. More than the tale, he adored Leela's exposition of love: "Even if he is a cowherd!" What lofty, noble, divine love!

He resolved to win such love even if he had to tend cattle. From the next day his expenses on face cream, powder, scent, cigarettes, tailoring, laundry, and the salon increased. For B.A. student Krishnan, love born meant money gone.

His father, who had gone trading in Burma, suffered disastrous losses. Krishnan's college education ended abruptly. Formal study withered, but his study of love continued sprouting. Romantic tales became his teachers: kings marrying gardeners, princesses marrying horsemen. He studied them as though preparing for an examination.

One author advised: "If a woman must respect you at first sight: clothes, clothes, clothes! Even if you roll in poverty, dress in the finest, most fashionable, even extravagant clothes whenever the woman you seek to conquer sees you." The author left the incompatibility between poverty and costly clothing to the reader and did not explain how a poor man might obtain such attire.

Another wrote that women dislike ostentation. A third prescribed humility: display it in speech, conduct, walk, dress, and manner, even if only in pretence, and no woman in this world or the next would resist. Still another began, "Do you seek victory in love? Here is the path."

Krishnan's mind became mud trampled by buffaloes. One sage said, "The lotus blossoms at the sun. Remain near her always, and she will become yours." Krishnan liked that. But the book that drove him to action confidently offered a tested method, a method known to hand and foot, poets, and God:

"Hear the method that tames cow, snake, child, tiger, killer, and mad elephant. You ask why I speak of beasts instead of gaining a woman's love? Fool! If the method can enchant a tiger, snake, or attacking bull, can it not enchant women with slender waists, swan-like walks, cool hearts, and dancing eyes? If I teach you to crush iron, would you ask whether it can crush sugar cane?

"Music restrains all creatures, makes them submit and show affection; literature and history prove it. Learn music and the world will say you conquered your beloved. Best of all is the flute. The lute is finer but more difficult. Take up the flute and receive dark-haired women as offerings. I swear by Krishna: did not the dark lord enchant the jewels of Gokulam and the gems among the Gopis with his flute? Without it, no love-play with the Gopis; without that play, no Krishna; without Krishna, no Gita; without Gita, no religion; without religion, no us!"

Krishnan acted. He took up the flute and gave many ears a chronic irritation. Vowing to conquer the Zamindar's daughter, he blew the notes without rest, rapidly crossed exercises and compositions, and acquired enough sparkle to receive concert offers. He sacrificed the chickpeas, coconut halves, and flower garlands he might have obtained at bhajan temples. Once confident in his training, he became king of the estate herd. He practised enchanting cattle and imagined them enchanted.

Krishnan's flute delighted Leela but did not nourish love. After college, she threw away her romantic notions. What woman wears a faded sari merely from old affection?

One day, after he had longed for her to speak, Leela approached him.

"Your flute playing is excellent."

According to the love manuals, he should have replied, "More excellent than the sweetness of your voice?" His tongue lacked courage.

"I stand near the fence every day and listen with pleasure," she said.

His books urged him to worship her: "You are the life of my music, the wick of my lamp, the path of life, the dwelling of delight, goddess of my heart's throne." Nothing emerged.

"I wish to test the power of your flute," Leela said and waited.

He thought: Must its power still be tested when this entire estate lies at my feet? He swelled with pride.

"In the next village..." she began.

"Is there someone who plays better than I?" he fearfully interrupted.

"No. What could equal your music? I tell the truth: you are Madhusudana reborn, disguised as a cowherd."

Instead of Leela's hand, he held the flute; instead of her lips, he placed it upon his own. Instead of saying he loved her, he began a stream of notes.

"Please listen," Leela said, killing the music at its birth. "In the next village lives a courtesan named Amirtham. She is educated, proud, and reputed a tiger in music. She respects no musician and especially despises flautists. A master as sweet as you must humble her pride. Somehow enchant her and remain in her house until she cries from love for you. Write to me of your victory. Here is money for expenses. I am leaving; return victorious. Do not hesitate."

She handed him a purse, tortured him with a sidelong glance, and departed. He lacked time and desire to refuse. He had read that praise heard from many others would make the woman one wished to conquer pour unchanging love upon him. Thus he went to defeat Amirtham, confident that if estate-born Leela had yielded to love, a courtesan was nothing.

Amirtham was the beloved of Barrister Navaneethakrishnan. She loved music and honoured musicians, enjoying old compositions, new tunes, and rare ragas. She took lessons and sang softly in bed while the Barrister was half asleep. He strictly forbade concerts but had promised a registered marriage.

Hearing a new musician had arrived, she wished to hear him. When no one arranged a concert, she paid the bhajan temple manager. The concert took place; she enjoyed it, bowed to the musician, and invited him home.

Krishnan had played several old compositions. Once inside, he concentrated upon displaying his art. Amirtham wanted to learn some songs. Krishnan had a flute but no singing voice, so teaching by playing a phrase and fitting it to her voice was difficult and took days. The Barrister's anger grew.

"Who is this crackpot, Amirtham? His flute and voice are torment. Has he settled here? He begins blowing before midnight is over. Do you find this amusing?" he began. As days passed he raised his pitch, and on a few occasions practised rhythmic patterns upon Amirtham's back.

She suffered. He created trouble without cause and suspected her needlessly, and music produced quarrels.

"Yes, that man will remain here. You must not prevent it," she finally dared say.

Barrister Navaneethakrishnan left in anger.

He was Leela's maternal uncle. Chitripuri nobleman Ananthapatham had two children: Navaneethakrishnan and Karpagam. Leela was Karpagam's only daughter. Enchanted by Amirtham, the Barrister had rejected his sister's request to marry Leela. Angry with Amirtham, he returned to Chitripuri and sank into sorrow. Then a letter arrived:

"I went! I saw! I conquered! The flute is sweet and its power immense. Amirtham is now my slave; without me she cannot live. This is truth. Barrister Navaneethakrishnan, who showered money upon her and kept her like a queen, ordered me to leave. Could that happen? I played a composition in Mohanam and a *padam* in Pantuvarali. Amirtham struck him in the face with the words, 'I cannot send him away.' He fled. Amirtham, once armoured in pride, lies bound by love.

"Yours,

"Krishnan, who conquered love through the flute."

Another letter was enclosed:

"Dear Navaneeth,

"I regret and feel ashamed that your pleasant life was destroyed by a mere flautist. What could I do? He wrote someone here boasting that he drove you from the house of the thief who stole your heart. I struggled to prevent its publication; that was my duty. You may wonder why he wrote here. The man who defeated you in your beloved's house is none other than Krishnan, who tended our estate herd.

"Yours,

"Leela."

Barrister Navaneethakrishnan felt ashamed and genuinely grieved. He sent a conciliatory message to his sister. The following month he and Leela were married with splendour.

When news of the marriage came out, Amirtham drove Krishnan away, saying, "Because of you I lost him." Krishnan babbled that the flute had robbed him of reason. What could the flute do? Was it responsible for the deficiency in his mind?

## Translator's Notes

Three pages previously marked as having no OCR text were recovered from transparency-bearing PNG scans before translation. Both numbered parts form one continuous story.

The title “கிருஷ்ண - லீலா” is deliberately layered: it names the cowherd Krishnan and the Zamindar's daughter Leela, while also invoking Krishna's mythic love-play, which the protagonist's manual absurdly treats as a technique to imitate.

The opening deliberately presents the herd's cows under women's names and with romantic language before revealing them as cattle. The English follows the same order and does not disclose the joke early.

The embedded “Greek” tale is explicitly revealed as Amirtham's invention rather than an actual translation. Names and wordplay such as அதிசயம் (Wonder) and கவி (poet/monkey in the story's explanation) are retained or glossed where necessary.

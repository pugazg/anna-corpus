## English Translation

The priest Punniyakoteeswara Ganapadigal enjoyed extraordinary respect in Brahmapuram. He had an imposing figure, entirely in keeping with the characteristics of his community. His belly demonstrated that the world was round. His orthodox tuft resembled one or two blades of grass that had sprouted and withered upon hard ground. He wore a five-fold dhoti, broad stripes of sacred ash, a sandal-paste mark, and a zari-bordered upper cloth. His unpolished golden complexion, desire-filled eyes, broad ears, and sharp nose completed his auspicious physical attributes.

His character, too, conformed to the duties of his caste. A faint smile always played upon his face. Through both speech and sighs he conveyed the impression that he cared for everyone. He never passed anyone on the road without asking after their welfare. On the appointed dates he never failed to remind clients of the various ancestral rites.

From time to time he would add: “Six tender coconuts are absolutely necessary, along with a little ginger, some unripe plantains if available, and two measures of samba rice or small-grained rice.” He asked for many things and obtained a few. Whatever arrived, he declared, was due to the Lord’s grace. He presented himself as an unruffled man.

When fixing auspicious hours and arranging rites and customs, the client’s preference was his Aries, Taurus, and Gemini.

“Mudaliar! Do you not have to go to court? I shall finish the ceremony before ten in the morning. People may call it an inauspicious day, but that will do no harm. Do you know why? The Mudaliar’s horoscope is of such a kind that even Saturn will perform the work of Venus,” he would say when the occasion demanded.

When he visited a fanatic for orthodoxy, however, he would say: “Did the elders proclaim for nothing that a good man will not do what an evil day dictates? The hour of the venomous bite must be avoided. Do you know how terrible the hour was when Dharmaputra sat down to gamble with Sakuni? Saturn cast his gaze! Dharma lost his kingdom, property, Draupadi, and everything else. Did Sakuni do it? It was Saturn’s work. The auspicious hour must be at dawn. It will be splendid!”

Ganapadigal knew which variety pleased every variety of client. He arranged the date, weekday, star, and ceremony accordingly.

Could he otherwise have possessed such influence in Brahmapuram? Could he have married his only daughter, Emalatha, to an engineer’s son with great pomp and paid for the son-in-law to study for the L.M.P. qualification? Could Emalatha have become a feast for young men’s eyes in an Ahmedabad sari, organdie blouse, ruby bangles, green-stoned nose ornament, and diamond earrings?

He was an orthodox priest, but did that occupation bring him distress? It brought gardens, groves, and landed property. Why would he abandon it?

It was not his fault that his L.M.P. son-in-law died suddenly. Emalatha’s beauty did not die with him. Yet because she had become inauspicious, fate required her to sit in a corner of her father’s house. That caused him pain. The tender, drooping shoot did not even have a mother.

As his daughter’s youth and beauty developed, Ganapadigal thought it would be supreme bliss if only her marriage pendant had remained upon her neck. If an “inauspicious” widow was beautiful, young, and fashionable, was that not a cause of fear for her father? Desire was a wicked, poisonous serpent. Who knew which anthill concealed which snake?

“The times have gone bad,” Ganapadigal lamented after much anxious thought.

For a while, she too thought and sobbed. Later, she passed her time looking at the eye-winkers, polo-collar wearers, smiling archers, and men with novel glances who walked along the street. She did not immediately conceive an improper thought upon seeing them.

At first she merely thought, “That man looks like my husband. He used to wear exactly that kind of collar.” Then that wicked feeling began to rule her, and she became its slave.

Kanakasundaram was an untethered bull. His parents had died when he was young. Somehow he studied, found someone’s patronage, and became a teacher in a school. He lived in a boarding house. Whether through the evil fate of orthodoxy or otherwise, the window of his room looked directly upon Emalatha’s garden.

At first there was shyness, then a kind of uncertainty. Next came a kind of happiness. Soon the sight of one another brought brightened faces, while absence brought worry. Then came letters written with the eyes. Finally actual letters began to travel between the widowed Emalatha and the young Kanakasundaram.

Was there not a wretched school? Neither he nor its window ever seemed to part. And Emalatha always seemed to have water to draw.

That green-covered periodical also reached him. If an old man proclaimed, “Cut through your bonds! Come out of prison! Pay no heed to the country’s foolish arrangement,” how could a young bull fail to feel his emotions surge? Along with his letters, Kanakasundaram began sending articles from *Kudi Arasu*.

Together with love, Emalatha received a mirror of the times. With a loving heart she became a woman ready to wipe away fear. The aged orthodox Ganapadigal knew nothing of what was happening in the younger world. He believed widowhood had settled permanently upon her. He did not know she was a parrot beating its wings to escape that prison.

“Sambho Mahadeva! You gave me every prosperity, Lord, but you also gave me this one affliction. When I think of Emalatha’s fate, all my property and comforts become burning fire. Lord of All, why have you imposed this punishment upon me?” he would say wearily. Whenever he saw Emalatha’s adornments, his suspicions grew. What could he do?

Just as Punniyakoteeswarar was about to complete his evening prayers, he saw a young man approaching. He closed his open eyes once again. That was his way. The townspeople said, “Who knows how deeply the Iyer cares for meditation?” It was the fruit of this device.

After the young man had stood before him for more than ten minutes, Ganapadigal opened his eyes with the words, “Parameswara! Treasure of compassion!” He looked at the young man. The face was new to him, but what of that? He must secure new clients. With that aim, he looked affectionately upon the visitor.

“I live in the next village. Tomorrow’s wedding is mine. The woman is of a caste higher than mine. She loves me, and I desire her too. But I am troubled because the marriage lacks scriptural approval. I overheard my parents saying that they wished you to conduct the wedding, so I ran here. I came to ask you not to attend this marriage that the scriptures do not sanction. Please show me mercy,” said the young man.

Ganapadigal thought. “The next village? Whose house?” he asked. After all, his auspicious hours and ceremonies depended upon the place.

“We are from Madras and have been here only a week. My father is a businessman. The Lord has given us every prosperity. We have a bungalow, a motorcar, and a small estate,” the young man said.

An itch arose in the Iyer’s heart. It was a wedding in a great household; there would be abundant fees and gifts.

“Sit here, my boy! Violations of scripture, dharma, and custom are indeed sinful acts. But they have expiations and remedies. It will cost a little, yet that is no obstacle. The Lord will be pleased.

“Moreover, you say the woman is of a higher caste. What caste does love possess? The beauty who shared King Shantanu’s love belonged to the fishing community. Did not Matsyagandhi later become Parimalagandhi? What is fitting for a woman is fitting for a man as well. Therefore, do not be distressed,” Ganapadigal replied with full scriptural authority.

The young man beamed and lowered his head. “There is one more special matter. A Gandharva marriage has already taken place between us.”

Ganapadigal thought: “Bravo! He is a man of action, and the girl is clever too. Why should scriptures and such nonsense still stand in their way? I shall put that right.”

“It will cost one hundred and fifty rupees. It cannot be done here; it must be performed at the house,” he said.

The young man handed him the banknotes. Ganapadigal returned home delighted.

The auspicious hour was three in the morning. The Iyer rose at two. Emalatha had hot water ready. He bathed, donned his ceremonial armour, and set out.

“Bolt both the back door and the street door, child. I shall return at eight,” he told Emalatha before leaving for the wedding house.

There was joy in the wedding house, and the priest shared in it. Half an hour later, the bride and groom arrived in a motorcar.

“That is proper! A Gandharva union has already occurred; naturally she must arrive this way. Besides, I performed the expiation for all this last night,” Ganapadigal said.

The ceremonial music rose deafeningly.

“When conducting such a modern wedding, why place a screen between bride and groom? It is utterly old-fashioned!” Ganapadigal himself began to preach reform.

“That is the custom of our community,” the wedding household replied.

Many people known to Ganapadigal were present. They praised him for coming forward to inaugurate an inter-caste marriage.

“You have acted according to the direction in which the times are moving. This is the proper course.”

“Caste distinctions must surely disappear. Inter-caste marriage must spread. Once Ganapadigal himself has approved it, can any orthodox man open his mouth against it again?”

“Desire chooses its own heroine, sir. Should a person marry some weeping face merely for the sake of custom?”

Ganapadigal rejoiced. He had already received one hundred and fifty rupees and calculated that another ten at least would come now.

The sacrificial flame rose like the blaze of love burning in the bride’s and groom’s hearts. The priest chanted the mantras with melody and feeling. The marriage pendant was tied. The drums sounded their loud climax. The screen was removed.

“Father! I bow to you!” said the bride.

“Ah! Who? Emala?” screamed the priest, and fainted.

After cool water was sprinkled upon him amid the loving cries of “Father! Father!” and the tender calls of “Uncle! Uncle!” the priest arose. He remembered what had happened. His heart became a basket of fire. With tears overflowing, he raged:

“Sinner! You have dropped a stone upon my head! Do widows like you do such a thing? Something may happen secretly, hidden like a fruit behind leaves. But an actual marriage? And I have served as its priest! What audacity! What wickedness! What will become of my honour? What will become of dharma? My livelihood is gone, foolish girl! This stout rogue deceived me with one hundred and fifty rupees! Am I to be party to this? I shall smash my head and die here. I shall tear out my tongue!”

They tried to console him. “You yourself conducted the marriage divinely and in full scriptural form. Why grieve now? Think of it as a vessel that has already broken.”

“Rogues! Villains! You deceived me. I shall not let this pass. What happened to Emalatha was not a marriage!” he cried.

“First came the Gandharva marriage. Now the wedding has been performed before the sacred fire, in the presence of Brahmapuram’s renowned priest,” said the groom, Kanakasundaram.

The bride Emalatha rejoiced in her thoughts: “I no longer need to keep walking to and from the well. I have found a husband pleasing to both eye and mind. If caste custom has been ruined, will the whole household sink? Natural law has endured.”

“Who are you, destroyer of my family?” Ganapadigal demanded angrily.

Delighted that she had obtained the marriage her heart desired, Emalatha said, “He is the Tamil teacher at our village school, father. He earns twenty rupees. He belongs to the Mudaliar community; he is not an alien creature.”

“A teacher? A salary of twenty? Sinner! You said you had a minor estate, a bungalow, and a motorcar. Do you have none of those? Are you merely a pauper?” the priest lamented.

“My daughter is gone! My livelihood is gone! My savings are gone! And this fellow has no money,” he cried.

“Why worry about money, father? Do we not possess it?” Emalatha asked. Ganapadigal felt as though his heart would burst.

“You will not see a single coin! I shall write everything over to the Iswaran temple. Beware!” Ganapadigal threatened.

“Twenty rupees is enough for two lives,” said Kanakasundaram.

“You sinful fellow! Do not destroy my honour. Take her and disappear from this town, somewhere far away. I shall give you two hundred rupees. You must leave this instant. I shall remain here quietly, invoking Siva. Be satisfied with my daughter; do not steal my honour as well. I am a Brahmin, and I beg you,” said Ganapadigal.

“I shall do exactly that, uncle. The motorcar has been arranged for that purpose. We planned the programme yesterday evening itself. We are going to Bangalore,” said the groom.

“We do not need the two hundred rupees, father. The jewellery you had made for me will fetch two thousand. That is enough,” said his daughter.

“Has that gone too?” cried the priest.

Who listened to his lament? The whole town did nothing but gossip. The couple lived lovingly in Bangalore.

Emalatha frequently wrote to her father. Ganapadigal continued his priestly work as usual. He was overjoyed to hear that a grandson had been born. Who else would inherit the accumulating property? It would all go to that boy.

Ganapadigal longed to see his grandson. He thought he should go secretly, without anyone knowing.

In Bangalore, as Emalatha rocked the child’s cradle, she cooed: “Grandfather will come! You are gold itself, sleep! He will buy you sweets and bring you a silk shirt. He will place a gold chain upon you.”

If anyone spoke impudently about Punniyakoteeswarar’s daughter living in Bangalore, the priest would console himself aloud: “If that was written upon her head, who could have prevented it?” In his heart, however, he felt a certain satisfaction.

The reform campaign began to gather strength. Vigorous propaganda exposed priestly fraud, religious obscenity, mismatched marriages, and the necessity of widow remarriage. It wounded the priest’s heart.

“See how thoroughly the times have decayed! Proper conduct is ruined. Respect for religion is disappearing. Customs concerning high and low caste are vanishing. It is the dance of the Kali age,” he lamented.

Only when Bangalore came to mind did he smile.

*Kudi Arasu*, 10 September 1939

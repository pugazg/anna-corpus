## English Translation

Maiden Surya! Do not immediately ask which film’s wondrous heroine she is: a peacock dancing in delight, a cuckoo singing in the grove, or a ravishing beauty who slays all who see her. We do not know how to dispatch such sweet news. The maiden I refer to is no screen beauty. She is one of the gods who destroy sinners and save the virtuous: a former god!

Several friends who read the essay “Former Gods” asked for more information.

I too read a few more works.

I met Maiden Surya.

Maiden Surya was a Japanese god, not today, for clarity has dawned in Japan. Is there an Achariyar there to place decay upon a market stall and sell it? In the days when Japan, like many other countries, worshipped countless imaginary gods, the deity seated there in splendour was Maiden Surya.

Even the fresh day of Pongal is converted into the Sankranti festival by hard-hearted men who preach orthodoxy and live by deceit. They arrange worship for the Sun God and tell us he rides a chariot drawn by seven horses, describing his nature and appearance. That very “Sun God” was a Sun Maiden in Japan. Here a male god; there a goddess. Not now, but in the days when there were plenty of dupes to accept what tricksters devised.

Although the sun was celebrated as a deity in many countries, it was generally made male. The Japanese myth-maker, however, gave it a woman’s form.

A lover of antiquity may say: “He may have called it female, but surely that country’s myth-maker could not have woven a story rich in imagination and charm. That requires exceptional intelligence and artistry, talents possessed only by Bharata’s descendants.”

The Japanese myth-maker was in no way inferior to ours. He too let invention flow without restraint.

The Japanese later rejected those tales as fabrications. Before doing so, they too had treated them like the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata*, recited them, devised forms of worship, and held sacred festivals. They celebrated them with the claim that reading them, or even standing nearby and listening, would remove sin and bring merit.

Until clarity dawned, they were “divine stories.” Rationalism prevailed. They bade farewell to Maiden Surya and sent her away. It was not as it is here.

Maiden Surya is a former god.

What about here? What have they abandoned or forgotten? Are they not performing penance in expectation of ever-new arrivals?

The Japanese myth-maker constructed the divine tale with imaginative skill.

“Beloved! Do you not know the beating of my heart? My spirit weakens, my darling! Will you not embrace me and fill me with joy? Look here. See the longing revealed by my eyes. See how the anguish of separation makes my body boil like fire. You who can give me life! Mighty one! We can dance and sing in delight. We can become a pair of doves!”

The divine woman calls to the handsome man. Who but she can know her sorrow?

The handsome man did not consent.

He said it was improper.

What could the woman stricken by love do? “What shall I do? How shall I be saved? Unless I unite with you, my life will not remain. The love kindled in my heart will not cool. Am I not beautiful? Even after seeing the limbs that display the pride of my youth, do you hesitate? My speech is syrup; my eyes are lotuses. Become acquainted with me and you will know the flavour of the honey I give!”

The jasmine maiden was spared the “trouble” of speaking in this fashion again. He had not rejected her. His thought was not that they should forgo pleasure. He had no “penance” to protect, nor any difficulty such as being unable to see the Lord or obtain his grace if he looked upon her.

He had not said it was impossible, only that it was improper. That is, it was improper for a woman to approach a handsome man, speak words of love, and invite him. That “right” belonged to the man. Therefore, he said, the work of declaring love was his.

“Stand to one side,” he said.

She withdrew, delighted by the assurance in his eyes that a feast awaited her.

The handsome man began:

“Lady whose words are fruit dipped in syrup! Look upon me and speak, my honey! Green parrot of the grove! Join me and give me pleasure, my doe! Fresh, fragrant blossom! Let your heart soften and accept me. I swear I shall never leave you and shall always give you joy. I have withered greatly; love is destroying my body, my doe!”

The two journeyed to the city of delight.

A sacred tale, do not be startled. This is not a new motion picture!

Even in a film it would not be easy to insert such a scene and dialogue. The men with scissors who guard morality would become fiercely angry.

The divine woman who approached and invited him, and the handsome man who declared that this was improper and that he must issue the invitation, were not human beings. They were gods.

Izanagi was the male god.

Izanami was the female god.

This was a love scene in the world of the gods, not an affair of ordinary people.

A celestial festival, not an earthly matter. At the time, Earth itself did not exist.

In the world of the gods lived Izanagi and Izanami, supreme beings.

The “delight” they initiated became the very cause of the world’s formation.

These gods were far more capable than the gods of our country.

What they gave birth to were not infants but countries and islands.

Yes, sir, yes! Japan was born as a result of the union of Izanagi and Izanami. These gods also gave birth to several other lands.

Can a country be born? Shame! What a mad tale. Is this a sacred story? Spit upon it!

A country is no ordinary thing.

Think of its vast land, rivers, lakes, mountains, valleys, towns, open spaces, roads, groves, fields, and canals. Does it make sense to say that a female deity gave birth to all this? Even a myth ought to contain at least a trace that allows one to say, “Something like this might have happened.” One can find people here today who mock it in this way.

“Were the Japanese so witless? Was a country born in a god’s womb?” they jeer.

Yet when Krishna, whom they still worship, was a baby and opened his mouth crying after his mother struck him, not merely Japan but the whole universe appeared inside it. A sacred story! Vyasa’s feast!

They can mock when a foreigner’s story says a country was born through a god.

Here a sacred story says the whole universe appeared inside the mouth of a god in infant form. If one respectfully says that it is impossible to believe, the Achariyar’s anger overflows and he raises his staff: his pen!

The Japanese no longer live in the period when they regarded the story of Izanagi giving birth to Japan as a divine tale. Here, however, queens of song perform over the modern scientific instrument of radio:

“The child opened his mouth;

Gopalan opened his mouth.”

The devotees stand entranced.

New understanding has arisen among the Japanese. They no longer regard Maiden Surya as a god.

As long as the Japanese people believed that once something was called a divine tale they must not entertain the slightest doubt, that asking for cause or explanation was sinful atheism, myths such as these remained upon the worship shelf and ruled the people’s minds.

In the age when superstition covered everything, the myth-maker fabricated such a story for the origin of Japan. Did he stop with that? Would a myth-maker ever stop?

After giving birth to Japan and other islands, Izanami gave birth to the god of fire. Was the Fire God an ordinary being? At the very moment of birth he burned his mother.

Izanami died.

She was a god! Yet the myth-maker said that gods too experienced birth and death. “Yes, Swami,” the people replied, because they were devotees.

Even now, what has the myth-maker here refrained from saying? What unbelievable thing do devotees reject? If the myth-maker said that by Mahesa’s act excrement gives off the fragrance of flowers, devotees here would still say:

“Yes, yes! It is only because of our sins that we do not smell flowers in excrement. To admit that publicly would be shameful, so let us agree with everyone else and say, ‘What a fragrance! What a fragrance! Is this not my father’s grace?’”

When devotees were numerous and rationalism had not entered the field, blatant lies were called myths fit for recitation, and people listened with melting hearts and relished them.

When Izanami died, Izanagi trembled and cried out.

“I cannot live without her! Wherever she has gone, I shall follow. What need have I of the body after life has left it? What life remains for me once the lamp of my heart has gone out?”

He spoke with anguish and ran and ran to find his beloved.

He went to the underworld.

Through myth, the Japanese myth-maker placed gods in the sky, humans upon the earth, and the dead beneath it.

In the underworld, Izanagi found Izanami.

Izanami had died. However tenderly her husband called, she could not return to life; her body itself had decayed.

He saw her and shed tears.

In the realm of the dead, the queen of beauty lay reduced to putrefaction.

“She gave me joy. She gave me the chance to know that this was the secret of life.

“The jewel among women bore many countries.

“The destroyer was born; my graceful beloved died; I became a walking corpse.

“Where is that cruel one?

“Where is the one responsible for my lovely maiden’s death?

“My fury will not subside unless I destroy him.”

Thus Izanagi lamented. Sparks of anger flew from his eyes. He killed the Fire God.

The Fire God died, and through his death countless new gods were born.

When his anger had somewhat cooled, Izanagi saw the heaps of corpses lying around him.

What horror! What horror!

Destruction everywhere! Ruin on every side!

“After beholding this horror, are my eyes anything but contemptible sores?” he grieved.

To cleanse the stain caused by seeing this dreadful sight, he washed his eyes.

Then Surya was born through his left eye, and the Moon through his right.

Does the idea of a god emerging from an eye make you want to clap and laugh? It was not only the Japanese trickster. The tricksters who lived here in former times fabricated similar tales.

Here there is a story of a deity born from a sneeze.

A little god has been born during a yawn.

Why go that far? Look at the sacred incarnation story of Lord Arumuga. A play about it is being staged even these days. Six sparks leave the forehead-eye of the resident of Kailasa, reach the Saravana pond, and Arumugan is born.

Here this still remains a sacred story.

Even people who drink the delicious nectar of modern civilisation and enjoy sweetness in life say they feel ecstatic when this scene is shown upon the stage.

In Japan today, there are no theatrical lions staging the birth of Maiden Surya. Here they not only exist; men who have come to rule the country lift the text, perform it, and praise it without ceasing.

Without ceasing, indeed!

After the Sun and Moon were born, Varuna, the rain god, was supposedly born from the nose.

Cry, and there is a god! Blow your nose, and there is a god!

Good heavens! The Japanese myth-maker pours out falsehoods as though competing with someone. It was a festival for priests.

Japan’s Varuna was named Susan.

Like our Krishna, this god delighted in mischief.

He began troubling even his own siblings.

Maiden Surya became furiously angry. “Shame! It is sinful even to remain among such impudent beings,” she thought. She entered a cave and hid, refusing to emerge.

Our country’s myths too contain incidents of this kind. Gods frequently go on strike in bitter anger. The other gods celebrate them, sing their praises, satisfy their demands, and bring them back to their old positions.

Maiden Surya was the mother of light, giver of life, lamp of the world, and divine radiance. What would happen without her?

The bewildered gods considered how they might somehow induce Surya to leave the cave. Were they mere humans, that they should lack ideas? How could gods suffer any shortage of sharp intelligence?

They discovered a device.

Standing at the cave mouth, they danced.

You may imagine that dance consists merely of glances, movements of the neck, display of the waist, swaying of the body, gestures of the hands, and arousing desire.

No. The gods’ dance did not stop at that point.

It was a naked dance, with contorted antics.

The other gods who watched the dance laughed. Hearing the laughter, Maiden Surya came out of the cave to learn what was happening.

The gods held a mirror before her and said, “Angry maiden! If you hide inside the cave, the whole realm will not sink. Look here. We have found another Surya.”

Fearing that some disaster might occur and that they might disregard her, Maiden Surya supposedly agreed to remain once more in the world of the gods.

How clever the gods were! Did you see how cunningly they brought the angry maiden back into line?

Maiden Surya did not even know about mirrors. Nor did she know they were deceiving her by showing her own form within one.

Such was the goddess’s “enlightenment.”

The myth-maker says that in Japan, the descendants of Maiden Surya became kings.

From time to time those kings supposedly supplied the people with an official list of gods. They issued orders naming the deities who might be worshipped, and the people performed worship.

The kings sometimes removed from office gods who had been receiving worship.

The king would order that certain gods must no longer be worshipped, and the people would stop offering worship to them.

Now it was not by a king’s order, but because reason developed, a renaissance arose, and the folly of believing useless inventions to be divine stories disappeared, that the Japanese made Maiden Surya a former god.

Countless gods once enthroned in temples became former gods.

Japan and many other countries accept the truths discovered through intellectual inquiry. Here they refuse them. From the dark forest spirit to the moon of the solar dynasty, they arrange a divine display and conduct a child’s game, then rejoice in proclaiming: “We have learned the origin! We have known the Lord! We have obtained grace! We have fostered theism!”

In Japan, Maiden Surya is a former god.

14 January 1955

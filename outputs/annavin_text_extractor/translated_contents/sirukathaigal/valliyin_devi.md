## English Translation

Tamil title: வள்ளியின் தேவி

The moment the green young calf cried "Amma," Valli would spring to her feet. "Devi has no mother. From now on, I alone am her companion," thought the little girl, who was not yet old enough even to understand how one became a mother. Such was her love for the calf.

At the sight of Valli, who cared for her lovingly, the calf would wag her tail, twitch her ears, bend her neck, and stand there as though saying all sorts of things. As if she understood the calf's speech, Valli would say, "Did a thorn prick your belly? Oh, poor thing, how much it must have hurt! Why did you go into the bush? You leave the grass and roam about like a rascal. See how you suffer now that a thorn has pierced you. Do not do that, Devi! I shall gather grass and give it to you at the proper time. What do you lack?"

She would talk and laugh, stand embracing the calf's neck, and sometimes even kiss her before looking around to see whether anyone had noticed.

The calf was no celebrated Sindhi or Saurashtra breed, but a village animal like Valli herself. Yet she possessed a beauty of her own and grew sleek and radiant. "She is a cow, not a deer, but her eyes have the beauty people attribute to a doe," many said.

For seven or eight days Valli pondered what to call her calf. Devi was the name of the clan goddess whom the villagers worshipped with offerings of milk, fruit, and prepared food. The child announced that she would give her calf that same name. Her father told her to do so.

Her mother, without pausing in her household work, said, "Why create needless work for yourself? Let her graze and stretch her legs. If you tie her up, she will be worth no money. Only by searching for grass will she grow strong and thrive."

Her mother did not understand the joy Valli found in seeking out and giving Devi everything she needed.

The cow that gave birth to Devi had come to Thenappan's hut. Planning to sell milk, save money, and clear his debts, Thenappan borrowed two hundred rupees from Varadappan, a moneylender in the neighbouring village, and bought a cow at the market.

In a small village, this was news. People came to Thenappan's yard, examined the whorls in the cow's hair and her udder, and said, "A good cow, my man, and not too expensive! Her milk yield will depend on good grazing, grooming, and feed. Look after her carefully and raise her well."

Thenappan laughed to himself. Did they think he needed instruction even in this?

Meygnanam Pillai, a prosperous resident of the upper settlement who had amassed considerable property in seven or eight years, owned a cow of which he said, "Even a thousand rupees could not buy its equal." He had the habit of caring for it with particular attention.

Thenappan worked in Meygnanam's house. After completing every task his master assigned, just as he was about to return home, the man would ask, "Have you attended to everything the cow needs?" Thenappan would do that too.

He had therefore become skilled in raising cattle. That was why he thought he could easily raise a cow, sell its milk, and save money.

Devi was the calf of the cow he bought.

Thenappan too rejoiced to see mother and calf beautifying his yard. But one day the cow returned from grazing with a swollen belly, breathing hard and staring about in terror.

The calf, unable to understand her mother's suffering, leapt about as usual, beat the air with her tail, and tried to snap the rope and spring forward to drink milk.

How could the cow explain anything aloud? Pain filled her belly. She could neither stand nor remain still. Unable to endure it, she kicked her legs, shed tears, and cried "Amma" in a choked voice.

Thenappan had unusually heavy work that day. The street pavilion had grown old and looked unsightly, so his master ordered him to dismantle it.

There were separate workers to erect a pavilion, but must they also be summoned merely to take it down? "You can do it," the master said. Once he had spoken, refusing would mean dismissal. Thenappan knew it and undertook the difficult work.

Poles fell from above, and he himself nearly fell below. He did not notice the ladder slipping. Dust entered and stung his eyes until tears flowed, but he had no time to stop and wipe them. He had not dismantled even half the pavilion, while daylight was already fleeing and making matters harder.

Amid all this, a black scorpion living in a hollow bamboo pole rushed out angrily and stung Thenappan's finger. He writhed and cried.

"Did you kill the scorpion or let it escape?" his master demanded, pouring out his anger.

Kamatchi, who swept and plastered the floor, said, "If you do not apply medicinal leaves, the poison will rise to your chest."

Chinnappan, who had come to borrow money, said, "Press lime onto the place where the scorpion stung. The poison and pain will vanish in half a minute. It is a proven remedy."

Meanwhile sweat covered Thenappan and his chest seemed to close. His master snapped irritably, "Go home now, Thenappa! What work has ever gone properly when I trusted you? You find one excuse after another!"

The burning now rose in Thenappan's heart as well as his body. He ran toward the hut of Uthandan, who dispensed remedies. Fortunately the man was there, and Thenappan received a herbal medicine. The poison subsided, but exhaustion overwhelmed him.

That was why Thenappan slept deeply that night.

Knowing all he had suffered, his wife was alarmed by the cow's cries but did not wake him. She went out herself, stroked the animal, and set out water. The cow writhed in still greater pain. For some reason, she licked the calf that came near her and shed tears.

"I call 'Amma,' and my mother usually rushes to me. What has happened today? My own mother is crying 'Amma, Amma' again and again and calling for help. What do I know? I am only a little calf." The animal could not say this, but her gaze did.

"Mother Mahamayi, show mercy! This is a speechless creature. Is it right that she should suffer? You alone must save her," Thenappan's wife prayed before lying down.

The cock crowed. Still in bed, she opened her eyes and looked beside her for her husband. He was not there. She sprang up in alarm and ran toward the yard. Thenappan sat before the cow with his head in his hands. The cow lay groaning, froth pouring from her mouth. The calf watched her mother's face and wept.

"What a horror! If we leave her like this, we shall lose her. Bring someone, anyone," his wife said.

Thenappan thought and made a decision.

"Take care of the calf. However difficult it is, I shall lead the cow to Doctor Govindan. When the master's Sindhi cow caught a cold and fever, he called this Govindan. The doctor came and gave an injection, and the illness fled at once. I shall show our cow to him. Doctor Govindan said that every disease afflicting humans now comes to cattle too."

He untied the rope.

The cow would not cooperate. She pulled back with the little strength that remained. Perhaps she thought, "Why do you torment me by taking me away while my calf is here? What use is medicine now? The illness dwelling in my belly all night has left me half dead. If I stay, I can see my calf until my eyes close and lick her again and again, giving her my love until my breath leaves me. Do not drag me to a hospital."

That may have been why the ordinarily gentle cow behaved roughly and even butted them that day.

Perhaps the calf thought that these people were wrestling with her mother and that she must do what she could. Crying "Amma, Amma," she kicked Thenappan, who stood nearby, and kicked him hard.

In anger Thenappan raised his hand to strike her. "Do not, Appa! The calf cannot bear a heavy blow," cried the little girl, standing in her defence.

The calf licked Valli when she came to her side. The mother cow saw it and cried out. The sight would have melted anyone with compassion.

Thenappan dragged the cow away. Everyone they met on the street said recovery would be difficult, and he began to sob. Yet he trusted that Doctor Govindan, reputed to possess a healing hand, would somehow save her.

Only four or five houses remained, then a small garden. Beside it was a yard where the doctor usually treated cattle. Thenappan arrived. The doctor was absent.

"Why, man?" he asked the doctor's servant.

"The doctor left yesterday. The dog lovingly raised by the Nedumpalur estate-holder had a safe delivery because of the medicine he gave it. They are holding a feast for him there," the servant replied.

Thenappan looked at his cow. She called out as though searching everywhere for her calf, but each cry grew weaker. The servant said, "A very fine cow. Had you brought her yesterday, she would have survived," extending a palm-leaf death notice through his words.

"Do not speak ill-omened words, Appa," Thenappan pleaded in anguish.

The cow could no longer stand and collapsed. Thenappan wept.

"Yes, yes! A motorcar! It is he!" he shouted joyfully.

The doctor arrived. Thenappan touched his feet and, without words, showed him the fallen cow.

The doctor checked whether her eyes still moved. There was a slight movement, but their light was fading.

When Thenappan began, "She cried throughout last night..." the doctor stopped him.

"Can you not see that the belly is swollen? Buying a cow and calf is easy, Appa, but raising them requires means and sense. The moment he saw that his Sindhi cow had a cold, your master sent word. You bring yours only after she is half dead. What can I do? You showed not even a mustard seed's worth of care. Unwilling to provide feed, you let her graze in the wild. She must have eaten some poisonous plant, and that brought her to this state. There is no medicine left for her now. Look, she no longer needs medicine at all."

As he spoke, the cow convulsed for a moment as though seized by a spasm. Then her troubles ended. She conquered the disease by becoming a corpse.

Thenappan did not return home that day.

No one could console the sobbing Valli. She had no heart to remain indoors and stayed in the yard, embracing the calf and crying.

From that day Valli's affection made up for the mother Devi had lost.

However much hard work accumulated, Valli never hesitated to do what Devi needed. She led her to a good pond, stood beside her while bathing and washing her, then admired her beauty. She gathered lotuses there, made Devi a garland, and delighted in the sight.

For several days Devi too looked toward the cattle shed and cried. But the villagers saw her gain new spirit from Valli's love and grow sleek and radiant.

"Have you seen how Valli's very life is bound up with that calf? It seems she will not perform even this many services for the husband she will one day have. What a spectacle!" toothless old women would say.

"Go away, Paatti! Must you always tease me?" Valli would reply, her eyes never leaving Devi and her hand continuing to stroke the calf's back.

After the mother cow died, Thenappan thought the calf was useless and that selling her would repay part of the debt. The moment he mentioned it, Valli ran to him, clasped his feet, and begged, "Do not sell my calf, Appa! Please! Whatever hardship it takes, I shall raise her. You will see, Appa: one day Devi will repay even the debt her mother brought us. I promise. Believe me! If the calf goes, I cannot bear it."

Thenappan laughed. "What madness has seized this girl? Very well, go! May you and your calf live happily. That is enough."

"My Appa is a golden Appa!" Valli cried, leaping for joy.

The countryside, displaying its lush green scene, is lovely ground not only for poets but also for painters. One day a young man photographed Valli playing with Devi and later made a painting from it.

"They even took a photograph of my calf, Appa!" Valli trumpeted.

One day Devi was in a playful mood. She tore the rope from Valli's hand and ran toward the field fast enough to defeat a deer.

"You leave even when I call you? Go then, Devi! I am no longer your elder sister!" Valli said, pretending anger. Expecting Devi to follow, she abandoned the game and walked toward home.

After going a short distance, she turned. Devi was nowhere to be seen.

"Devi! Devi!" she called loudly.

Only the barking of a dog answered.

The sorghum field had grown taller than a person. There was no wind, yet the stalks were swaying. The dog's noise came from there. Perhaps Devi was grazing inside and the guard dog had chased her, Valli thought anxiously.

The sorghum field had become a battlefield. The dog chased Devi relentlessly and bit her as though mad. Devi was accustomed to running, but that day a pumpkin vine caught her leg and stopped her flight. It became a hunt for the dog.

Realizing that Devi was in danger, Valli ran headlong toward the field. The sight there terrified her. The calf, exhausted and wounded by bites, had fallen. Thinking itself victorious, the vicious dog circled and barked as though proclaiming its valour to the village.

Valli trembled. She could not approach Devi. The dog glared and barked at her and came forward to bite. If she did not protect Devi, the wicked animal would attack the calf again.

What could Valli do? She picked up a stone, took careful aim, bound all her anger into it, and hurled it at the dog. The black stone flew with a hiss and struck the frenzied animal's skull. The dog convulsed, cried out, curled up, and died immediately.

Valli had no heart to say, "Good, dog, you are dead." She had thrown the stone only to drive it away and never imagined it would kill the animal.

"Tommy! Tommy!" someone called.

Valli was terrified. Was the dog's owner coming? If he saw what she had done, he would never spare her. She decided to hide, moved aside, and crouched in a bush.

Again came the cry, "Tommy! Tommy!"

It was dangerous to remain where she might be discovered, so Valli went home. The calf did not return.

"Where is the calf, Valli?" Thenappan asked.

She shed tears and slowly forced out the news as though swallowing a lump in her throat.

"Alas! Is that what you did? Tommy belongs to the rough estate-holder Manickam. By now he will have seen the horrible sight of his dead dog. His anger will blaze. What will he do? Valli, did anyone see you throw the stone?" Thenappan asked in terror.

"No, Appa," she said, hiccupping and sobbing.

"Hey! Who is there?" a voice called.

Thenappan came into the street. The estate-holder stood there with a gun. At his feet lay Devi, the calf.

"Is this calf yours?" he asked.

Thenappan nodded.

"Where is Valli?" Manickam asked.

Thenappan trembled, thinking the man had come knowing everything.

Manickam laughed. "Go and tell Valli that the calf has not died. She killed my dog with a stone, but the dog was already mad, and I had brought it toward the forest to shoot it. It broke its chain, ran away, and bit the calf. Valli saw it and killed it with a stone."

Thenappan rejoiced on hearing this.

Valli, who had withdrawn inside, came running out and eagerly stroked the calf. Devi immediately leapt upon her as usual.

"Take her to the doctor at once and give her whatever medicine is needed. Since a rabid dog bit the calf, poison may remain in her," Manickam said, cleaning his gun as he walked away.

Thenappan sank into grief.

"It was to the doctor that I took her mother!" he said sorrowfully.

"Will the doctor say even now that there is no medicine? Come, Appa!" Valli urged.

They went with the calf.

They found the doctor, but it was not the man from before. This one was young. He saw the calf's beauty and the girl standing there bathing her in tears.

"I am glad to see that you love this calf so much, child. Do not be afraid. No danger will come to her. I shall save her," he said.

Valli put an arm around Devi's neck and kissed her. The calf twitched her ears and showed Valli her affection. The doctor watched.

"We call it a speechless creature, yet see how even ears and eyes possess the art of speech," he said, administering the medicine while Valli watched.

The news that Devi had survived spread throughout the street.

Valli's face gained a new radiance. Joy flooded it.

"Is there another calf equal to mine anywhere in all the fourteen worlds?" she sang and danced. As though declaring that she could do the same, Devi too leapt and danced.

"This young calf will fetch only ten or fifteen rupees. Still, let me at least recover that much toward the interest," said Varadappan, who had lent Thenappan money, and began dragging Devi away.

Valli collapsed to the ground. The calf snapped the rope, ran back, stood beside her, and licked Valli's forehead.

Varadappan saw it. What was he to do?

"If I keep watching all this, my money alone will be lost. Hey, go! Pay me, and you may raise the calf with all the affection you please. Otherwise she too will join my herd, one among many," he said.

"Here is a twenty-rupee note. Leave the calf," said a young man who had appeared there from somewhere.

The calf returned to Valli's hand. Her eyes asked a question.

"One day I photographed you and the calf playing. Later I made it into a painting. Good people who saw it gave me a prize. This is a small part of that money," the young man explained.

As though she knew he was speaking about her, Devi turned her ears toward his voice. It was a beautiful sight.

"Look, Appa! My Devi has reached a state in which she can free herself. And that is not all. I told you earlier that Devi herself would repay every debt caused by her mother. Now see whether my words were true," Valli said, embracing Devi as though gathering her up in both arms.

Devi brought her mouth close to Valli's ear and happily told her all manner of stories.

## Translator's Notes

The name “தேவி” means “goddess” and is also the village clan deity's name; the title therefore carries affectionate and sacred overtones while referring simply to Valli's calf. Historical husbandry, folk-remedy, debt, and animal-health statements are translated as the story's period narrative, not present-day veterinary advice.

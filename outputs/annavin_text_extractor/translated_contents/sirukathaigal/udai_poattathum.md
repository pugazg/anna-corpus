## English Translation

"What do you expect me to do, woman? You torment me more than the man who lent the money."

"What do you know of the degrading things he says to me? How many times can I repeat the same answer? And how many instalments will he agree to?"

"Are we going to leave his money unpaid? Is he going to abandon its recovery? We do not have the money at present. We ask him to wait a little. What is wrong in that?"

"I have been answering him more politely and respectfully than you. Don't I say, 'Please wait a little. He is arranging the money. Once it arrives, we will place your money upon a flower and return it'? How long will this empty talk, this refrain of 'No, no,' sound sweet to him? Wouldn't he become angry?"

"What will he do in anger? Cut off my head?"

"Why must he cut off your head? Wouldn't it be enough if he stood in the street, loudly proclaimed our worth, and stripped us of our honour? Won't he do that?"

"Let him try, if he has the courage! Would I not file a defamation case the very next day? He may use whatever lawful means exist to recover the debt, but can he speak degradingly? You do not know the law. What does he know?"

"Is the law meant for filing a case against a man merely because he insists that a loan be repaid?"

"One can argue: 'Not now. It will take another year. The entire amount cannot be paid at once; it can only be paid little by little.' What do you know of the law's subtleties? Foolish woman! One can even argue, 'I owe you no debt at all.' Understand?"

"After stretching out your hand and taking his money..."

"Who stretched out a hand and took money? Did I?"

"It was borrowed in your father's name. I know."

"My father and I have no relationship at all in matters of property. If I raise the point that I executed a deed of release three years ago, foolish woman, the case will take six years to conclude. Understand?"

"So, in your view, the lender must not ask for repayment."

"He may ask respectfully. He may freely ask. But he must not speak insultingly. The law will not permit it."

They did not deny the loan they had received. Nor did they say they could never repay it.

"How can the borrowed debt be denied? How could the law permit a claim that it cannot be repaid? I am astonished and saddened to hear my learned friend, after so many years of experience, make such a hollow argument merely because he must advocate for his client.

"Why, his client asks only for another instalment period in which to repay the debt."

"Instalment! Instalment! How many instalments? He said July, and my client agreed. He waited until September. No money came. Another extension was requested and granted. By now, twelve extensions in total have been granted. Asking my client for another is neither justice nor law."

"This extension must be granted. Even the hardship my client endured for his daughter's wedding last month has not ended. His health too is poor."

"Let him go to a good doctor and attend to his health. What business does that have here? If another extension is granted, that client intends to keep obtaining extensions and finally cheat my client. He owns only one house. If he sells it, converts it into cash, and leaves town, recovering the money from him will become impossible. Information has been received that he is conspiring to sell the house and making secret arrangements.

"Therefore, I request this honourable court to order that my client seize, in satisfaction of the debt, the house of Thandavarayar, who has failed to repay his loan and has merely passed the time by asking for extensions."

You have seen the lawyer who told his wife that a lender must listen when the debtor asks for time, speak respectfully, and accept repayment in small amounts, and that otherwise the law even permits an argument denying the debt itself.

You have also seen the lawyer arguing that the house of a man who repeatedly obtained extensions and intended to cheat without repaying should be seized.

You are eager to know the first man's name and the second man's name, are you not? Prepare yourself for a disappointment.

There were not two men. The speaker was one and the same.

The Viswanatham who spoke at home was the man who spoke in court.

When speaking to his wife at home, he was simply Viswanatham. In court, he was Advocate Viswanatham.

At home, he spoke while drying himself after a bath.

In court, he spoke wearing a lawyer's dress.

Once the clothes were put on, the speech changed.

At home, he was a worried husband. In court, he was an advocate who spoke with cutting finality.

You see only one man, but two different conditions. In two different conditions, two different speeches and two different sets of clothes.

You may ask in which condition his speech was just. There was justice in both. More than justice, there was abundant necessity for him to speak in those two different ways.

Advocate Viswanatham had a duty to help his client recover the money lent without loss, and he performed that duty skilfully.

Vasantha's husband Viswanatham had spent beyond his income, fallen into debt, and become unable to repay it. He was distressed. Without offering a way out of that distress, and while pretending that he was deliberately withholding repayment, he spoke at home as he needed to speak to his warning wife.

Advocate Viswanatham struck the table and insisted that the debt must be repaid and no more extensions granted. If that argument succeeded and the debtor repaid the lender, Viswanatham himself might then repay part of his own debt instead of seeking further extensions and boasting to his wife that a lender had no choice but to accept them.

Viswanatham spoke at home because of inability.

Viswanatham spoke in court to remove the want present in his home.

One man, two different circumstances, two different speeches.

A change in clothes; an upside-down change in words.

"Is this how you beat a child? Look, the marks of your fingers are visible across the poor boy's back. How could you beat him like this?"

"Won't a child be beaten when he does wrong? For what your precious son did, do you expect me to ward off the evil eye instead of striking him?"

"What deadly wrong did he commit to deserve such a beating? You struck blindly, without even remembering he is a child. What murderous cruelty!"

"How many days can one continue giving advice? He must listen too."

"As though you possess even a drop of mercy, compassion, concern, or pity!"

"Only one who has raised children knows the hardship involved."

"Whatever wrong he does, dear woman, you must advise and correct him lovingly. You must not treat him with such brutality and cruelty. One can correct him with stern words and threats. But this? Bhadrakali! Should you beat him until the child writhes like this?"

"If you don't strike his knees and force it out of him, the fellow will not tell the truth. Will a peacock shed a feather merely because you ask, 'Peacock, peacock, give me a feather'?"

"No, No. 84! The man is very weak. He cannot bear the beating. It is a dreadful sight to hear him cry."

"No, 84. The truth can be brought out with threats and intimidation alone. Perhaps the fellow unknowingly did wrong. I feel he may reform."

"These people? Are you going to preach to them in the belief that they will reform? Fool! They must be beaten without mercy. You do not know them. Have not many people already given them advice and kind words? None of that works upon them. There is a proverb, 88: not even an elder or younger brother helps as much as a beating.

"Hey! Tell the truth, or I will split your skull. Tell the truth!"

You see Constable Ponnurangam, No. 84, speaking against mercy and beating the captured rogue. You think: why such brutality? Does he not possess even a trace of pity?

This very Ponnurangam was the husband who had earlier condemned his wife for beating their child.

At home, one saw a Tiruppur vest, a Kanchipuram dhoti, sacred ash from Tiruchendur, and a sandal mark from Palani. In his speech, one saw pity and a sense of justice.

At the police lock-up, one sees a khaki uniform, a red cap bearing the number 84, and a metal-ringed baton in his hand. The clothing has changed. The speech? Good heavens! It has turned completely upside down.

Ponnurangam, who advised his wife to correct the child with good counsel, had become a father after many years without children. That affection overflowed in his speech.

Ponnurangam, who said a rogue must receive a powerful beating to be subdued, was a policeman whom many expected soon to become a head constable.

The Ponnurangam at home and the Ponnurangam in the police lock-up wore different clothes and spoke different words.

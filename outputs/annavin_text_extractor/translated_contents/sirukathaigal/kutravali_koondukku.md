## English Translation

"What do you say to the charge made against you?"

"What is there to say, my lord? I am poor..."

"It is true that you stole the goat, is it not? Do you admit the offence?"

"Yes, master! It is true that I stole it. I have six children. Hunger, no work, starvation. My wife lies weeping in a corner. The children writhed with hunger. I could see no way out. I stole it. It is true that I stole the goat. I am poor..."

Will the law show mercy merely because he is poor? If he were rich, the law might at least become afraid and reveal some alley or loophole through which he could escape. But the goat thief is the father of six children, and those children are the only wealth he has. He is poor.

He stole a goat, while wealthy men who steal even women can throw dust into the law's eyes and escape. This man was destitute and therefore caught. He was tried and admitted his offence. Did the judge say, "Because he confessed, he is a man of good heart. Hunger alone drove him to steal; he is not wicked. Poor fellow, let us forgive him"? Did he, or would he?

What would become of the law then? If the law is not upheld at least when poor people are caught in it, when else will it get an opportunity? So the poor man who stole the goat was punished. The law triumphed.

The poor man punished for stealing the goat was a Black man. The incident took place in Johannesburg, South Africa.

Because the man explained that poverty had driven him to steal, people took pity on his condition and collected donations to appeal the case to a higher court and seek a reduction of his punishment.

One hundred pounds was collected.

Humanity had blossomed to this extent in a country ruled by racial fanatics who degraded Black people. Humanity had not died at its roots. From time to time, its fragrance still brought delight to the heart.

At certain moments, compassion and humanity blossom, and a poor person is saved. But every poor person will not receive such an opportunity. Nor is it possible to conduct a permanent campaign to rescue every person whom hunger turns into a thief from the grasp of the law. That is why good people insist that the present government must make a great effort to eradicate and reduce poverty as a programme of administration.

We see the undeniable truth of the saying, "When hunger arrives, the ten virtues fly away."

Poverty turns a good man into a bad one. It is the government that allows him to be brought to that condition. Bharathi's anger over this problem crossed all limits and made him thunder with rage great enough to destroy the world:

> If even one person has no food, we shall destroy the world.

Bharathi's anger stopped with the world. Valluvar went to the world's creator himself, frightened him, and issued a warning: if the condition exists in which a person must live only by begging, let that "maker of the world" wander and perish.

We need not possess the power to destroy either the world or its creator. It is enough for rulers to understand that the birth of crime and the flourishing of virtue depend upon a person's surroundings, and then act to reform those surroundings. Crime will be greatly reduced.

Compassion must arise, and the African incident shows that it sometimes does. But merely waiting in the hope that compassion will arise is not enough. It will not end the suffering of the poor.

People will not always speak with pity, as they did when they said that the destitute Black man stole because he was the father of six children. Instead, four people will speak and abuse him in four different ways:

"The burly fellow! He has six children, it seems. Does that give him the right to rob?"

"Today he stole a goat; tomorrow it will be a cow; after that he will break into a house. People like this must not be left alone. They are a grave danger to society!"

"Starving? Hungry? Why not beg? Or throw himself into some pond and die? How dare he steal!"

The "criminal" who first pleads, "Sir, I am poor! Hunger drove me to this wicked act," is pushed into the dock. Through repeated experience, he ceases even to confess his offence and begins trying only to deceive and escape. Little by little, he becomes an accomplished thief and truly becomes a grave danger to society.

In prison, an experienced thief may say to a novice:

"You fool! Of all places, you went into a third-class carriage and stole a bundle! Only poor fellows like us travel second and third class, and you laid hands on their property as if you were some great hero! I am Rajakutti, understand? A Seth was sleeping in a first-class carriage, and I lifted his leather bag. That is how it should be done! They tell the tale of Perumal begging and Hanuman snatching even that away. You went into a third-class carriage and took a poor man's bundle. Shame! Aren't you embarrassed?"

Every accomplished thief of this kind was, at first, a man overcome by shame, pain, sorrow, and bewilderment, pleading in a tearful voice, "Sir, I am poor!"

Even if compassion helps him escape one case, if poverty continues to pursue and torment him again and again, he will be drawn back into crime.

If he is not to become an expert in this way, rulers must understand that poverty is society's fundamental danger and must possess a plan to uproot it. Crime cannot be eliminated by preaching or by brandishing the law to create fear. People's surroundings must be reformed.

When a poor man stands in the prisoner's dock, it means that the true criminal is somewhere else. The true criminal, who refuses to change the circumstances that brought him to the point of committing an offence, stands elsewhere, outside the dock.

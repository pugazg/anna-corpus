## English Translation

"Why has that Saturn not cleared out yet? It is nearly six!" said the accountant.

"Hurry, Amma, hurry and finish tying it. It is nearly six in the evening! It is almost time for that rudraksha-wearing cat to arrive," said the flower seller.

"He never misses even a single day. Is he not a great devotee! It is not yet six; he will be here," said the temple priest.

"We shall see tomorrow whether the rhythm comes out properly. It is nearly six. It is time for that old monkey to arrive," said Vedam.

"Six o'clock? Quite right! Only now will the madman have set out for the temple," said Rami.

Saturn, rudraksha-wearing cat, great devotee, old monkey, madman: this five-name offering of worship was performed at the same moment, for the same person, by five different people. Were all the varieties of worship offered to him in all the other places to be described, the recital would run to a thousand names.

His name was Arumuga Mudaliar!

Did Arumuga Mudaliar merely sit still and accept this worship? Would he? There may have been an account book in his hand, but what was in his mind?

He abused the bookkeeper who was silently praying, "Why has that Saturn not cleared out yet?" calling him a "wage-eater" and demanding to know whether "that idler" had tied the garland yet.

He was speaking of the same flower seller who, at that very moment, was consecrating Mudaliar as a "rudraksha-wearing cat" before his mother. Mudaliar paused for a second. "I must go. If I don't, will that money-grabber leave me alone?" he muttered. At precisely that time, the temple priest was worshipping Mudaliar as a "great devotee" while speaking to another devotee. It was also the moment when Vedam, reluctant to part from the musician who had sung a beautiful composition so delightfully, said that it was nearly time for Mudaliar to arrive. Her offering to him was "that old monkey." Did he fail to offer worship to her in return? Stuffing four or five ten-rupee notes into his purse, he conducted a mental service: "If I do not pay her today, that Alli's durbar will be grand indeed!"

Just as Rami, Mudaliar's second wife, was saying, "Madman! He must be setting out for the temple only now," Mudaliar was thinking of his wife: "Has that scowling face made the herbal brew, or has she gone off to the cinema?"

After all these services had been performed at six that evening, rice-merchant Arumuga Mudaliar told the "wage-eater" to close the shop and leave. He took the garland tied by the "idler," went to the temple where the "money-grabber" stood, completed his view of the deity, and then paid the offering due to "Alli." When she cried that her lower abdomen hurt, he prescribed a treatment for it. He entered his house, drank the herbal brew prepared by the "scowling face," and rolled about in bed, worshipping in a yawning voice, "Father! Muruga!"

Were all the worship offered each day by those who bought "rice" at his shop, and all the worship he offered them in return, to be compiled, it would become a dictionary.

"Which sinner's shop did this rice come from? It is hard as stone and refuses to cook!" That was the kitchen's worship.

"How will money ever return if I give credit to that yellow-paper fellow?" That was the worship Mudaliar, ever intent on money, offered his customer.

Thus receiving and dispensing worship of every variety, he would arrive at his shop at eight each morning. At six in the evening he would leave, buy a flower garland, and visit the Bhuvaneswari temple. After receiving sacred ash from Vaitheeswara Iyer there, he would go to the house of the courtesan Vedam and spend a little peaceful time until eight or nine. Then he would go home to earn the anger of Rami, his second wife.

At her parents' house Rami had never earned a reputation for anger. Once, her father Gopala Mudaliar had berated her with every unspeakable word. Her mother had reviled her as a shameless creature who had ruined the honour of the family. Rami would stand like a tree or shed tears in torrents. She had never uttered a word in reply. She would console herself that answering back was not proper.

The cause of it all was the young man in the house opposite. Rami had surrendered her heart to him. Her parents had caught a faint scent of it and subdued the girl with their abuse. Yet Rami never grew angry whenever they scolded her then. That anger arose only after she entered her husband's house.

However much abuse Rami endured in her parents' home, she possessed a hope, and that hope kept happiness alive in her heart. He had written her a letter: "Whatever restrictions they impose in your house, do not be afraid, Rami! I shall marry you somehow. Let my uncle from the city come. I shall have him speak to your father and make him consent to the marriage. Otherwise, see whether my name is Ekambaram!"

That gave Rami hope and happiness. She believed that she would somehow have Ekambaram.

Even now his name remained Ekambaram. But Rami had become the second wife of rice-merchant Arumuga Mudaliar. On that day, only sorrow had entered the bridal chamber. When her husband entered the house, anger too entered her mind. Why? On the night of consummation, Arumuga Mudaliar revealed a secret in order to display his cleverness.

"Rami! Look this way. Ah, why so bashful? Am I a young man, Amma, for you to put on such coy airs before me?" Thus he began his amorous talk.

Rami said nothing. What Rami could have spoken?

A customer might stand arguing, "Mudaliar, this rice is no good. Give me another kind!" Would Arumuga Mudaliar simply say yes? He would offer countless explanations. He would rub the rice clean and display it, put a little in his mouth to demonstrate it, show the purchase invoice, swear solemn oaths, and do anything and everything until he loaded that same unwanted rice upon the customer's head and sent him away. He was a man of experience! Having defeated argumentative men, would such an experienced man fail before a woman who spoke with her eyes?

He took all her words such as "Hmph!" and "Oh, no!" to mean what a host tells a guest at dinner: "The leaf has been laid; I am bringing the bowl of ghee." Kannamma, before Rami, had been the same. Even Vedam, who had been his intimate since he was twenty, had at first put on all manner of coy airs. Arumuga Mudaliar had triumphed in the end. He was the man who had touched her and tied the marriage pendant; would he let Rami go? That was not Arumuga Mudaliar's mistake. His mistake was revealing a secret to Rami in order to display his ingenuity.

"Ekambaram was about to carry you away, was he not?" he began.

Rami, who had been facing the other way, turned towards him without knowing that she did so.

"By sending his uncle from the city, he had even won your father over," said Mudaliar. Rami's face reddened.

Mudaliar left that subject for a while and began to sing softly. In the morning he recited the Vinayagar Agaval in a loud voice; now he sang, only in fragments, the song beginning, "O doe, fragrant marjoram!" It was the very song he had repeatedly asked Vedam to sing, and which she had sung half-heartedly.

Rami longed for some further news of Ekambaram. How could she ask? The man who loved her had sent his city uncle as an emissary, changed even her father's mind, obtained her horoscope and taken it to the priest. Why, then, had he suddenly declared that he did not want the girl? Until that day Rami had never understood. The man who knew the secret was singing without speaking of it. For him it was a night of pleasure. Was it so for her?

He finished singing. He could not refrain from recounting the glory of his own victory.

"Rami! I shall tell you a story," he said.

Rami gestured that she did not know.

"Shall I tell you a story?" he asked. She did not ask him to tell it; he began on his own.

"In a certain village there was a girl just like you, very beautiful. When she smiled, a dimple would appear in her cheek..."

As he spoke, he searched for the dimple. It was not there! Would it appear unless she were happy? Her thoughts had gone to the memory that Ekambaram had placed his very life in that cheek. The bridegroom continued his story.

"A young fellow stood on his head insisting that he must marry that very girl. He tried all manner of schemes. He had an uncle in the city. He sent that man to advise the girl's father. Her father consented too. They gave the horoscope to an astrologer."

Rami knew the story up to that point. What had happened after that? That was what she needed to know. Mudaliar cleared his throat and continued.

"After they examined the horoscope, the fellow suddenly said, 'I cannot marry that girl.' He would not listen, whatever anyone said."

Having said this, he rose and took the brass vessel of milk from the table to drink. He saw a piece of banana lying in it and laughed. Still laughing, he continued: "When that fellow said he did not want the girl, it was as though a fruit had slipped into the milk for Arumugam, who had longed for many days to marry her. A very Rathi herself had come to his side!"

Thus he finished both the story and the milk. In supreme delight, he joined the maiden at his side with a husband's rights.

Rami knew that part of the story too! Why had Ekambaram said that he could not marry her? That was what she yearned to know. For a while she was afraid to ask how it had happened. Then she found the courage to ask. "A fine story," she said softly in mockery.

"Was it not good? Very well, you tell me a better story!" said Arumuga Mudaliar, beginning to fondle her. It was on seeing such fondling that Vedam had bestowed upon him the worshipful name "old monkey." Rami said nothing.

"Rami! Shall I tell you the truth? It was I who prevented that marriage from taking place. He wanted it, certainly. But once I set my eye on something, can it fall into another man's hands?

"Go and ask in Market Street. Millionaires will all run away saying, 'Good heavens! Arumuga Mudali has already named his price. We want no contest with him!' One needs the Goddess of Courage in everything. Your father heard the city man's words and grinned. I saw it. Did I abandon my courage? Is Arumugam a man to abandon courage?

"An idea came to me. I went straight to the Iyer's house, addressed him as 'Swami' and made one obeisance; five notes, ten-rupee notes. 'What is it, Mudaliar?' he asked. 'With your grace, I must obtain Rami as my second wife,' I said.

"'What a pity! Was it not only yesterday that Ekambaram's uncle brought me the girl's horoscope?' he said.

"'I came knowing that, Swami! If you set your mind to it, anything can be done,' I said.

"He thought for a while. 'Could I say that the horoscopes do not agree?' he asked me.

"'You could. But what if they consult another astrologer?' I asked.

"'That is precisely what I too am considering,' the Iyer drawled.

"At once I cast out an idea. 'Alter the girl's horoscope a little. It must be changed so that whoever is given it will say there is no compatibility. It will be enough to change the girl's birth star to Moolam. The scriptures say that if a man marries a woman born under Moolam, she will send her mother-in-law to a corner. That is enough. Tell the city man and alter the horoscope skilfully as well,' I said.

"Through the Iyer's grace, your birth star changed into Moolam. The city man fled in a panic. Falling at Ekambaram's feet, he said, 'Younger brother! Listen to me. Forget that girl. That curse of a woman was apparently born under Moolam! Your mother will die within three months!'

"Ekambaram's mother cried, 'Are you going to marry that wretch merely to kill me?' The house became a battlefield. He could say nothing. He declared once and for all that he did not want the girl. It seems Ekambaram even consulted another astrologer! They all said that Moolam would not do. What else could they say? Is that not what is written in the book?

"But did those men discover that Brahma had caused you to be born under Poosam, while your husband changed it into Moolam?"

Mudaliar thus finished his story. From that moment, the anger that took up residence in her heart only grew from day to day; it did not diminish in the least.

Choosing a time when Arumuga Mudaliar had gone to amuse himself with Vedam, Rami summoned Ekambaram with the maid's help and told him of the fraud that had taken place. Even after that, her anger did not subside.

"Scoundrel! Is this the kind of fraud he committed? He has torn out my eyes!" Ekambaram shouted furiously. "I was a fool. I was deceived," he wept.

"Do not weep, my dear! This is what fate ordained. What can we do?" Rami consoled him. Could matters end merely at consolation? She wiped away his tears with the loose end of her sari. Saying, "I have lost you," he embraced her.

"Enough, my dear! That was all the fortune allotted to us," she lamented. She wiped his tears. He tried to still the trembling of her lips with a kiss. Could he? They descended and fell into the well of pleasure. Afterwards they felt no regret.

Whenever Mudaliar was engaged in worship at Bhuvaneswari, Ekambaram would be sporting with Rami. After his worship, the ingenious Arumugam would "read Vedam" and return home. The moment he entered the house, Rami would say:

"Scowling face! What a schemer!"

3 February 1946

## Translator's Notes

The story repeatedly uses `அர்ச்சனை` (ritual worship or an offering of names) ironically for the insults the characters exchange. `உருத்திராட்சப் பூனை` literally means a cat wearing rudraksha beads and denotes a hypocrite who displays outward piety. The ending makes a deliberate pun on `வேதம்`: Vedam is the courtesan's name, while `வேதம் படித்து` ordinarily means “reading the Vedas.” `பழம் நழுவிப் பாலில் விழுந்தது` describes an unexpectedly fortunate outcome. The horoscope fraud turns Rami's actual star, Poosam, into Moolam; the dialogue invokes the traditional fear that a Moolam-born bride endangers her mother-in-law.

## English Translation

Tamil title: பித்தளை அல்ல, பொன்னேதான்!

"Muthu! Muthusamy! Muthu!"

"Appa! Why are you crying, Appa?"

"Muthu! I am a sinner! I am a wicked man!"

"Appa, do not cry. I will never follow such a wrong path again. I swear it."

"Do not kill me, Muthu! Do not kill me..."

"Whatever hardship comes, Appa, one must not steal. Theft is a degrading trade. One can labour for wages; one can even beg at four houses. But one must never steal, Appa!"

"Alas, Muthu! What can I say? How can I bear this? Muthu, I brought you to this..."

"Appa, I shall reform! Do not worry. Tell little sister..."

It was surprising that the policemen allowed them to talk this long. But they too had families, children, and affection. They therefore permitted the father to weep for a little while before his son, sentenced to six months in prison, was put into the van.

The policemen wondered that the man who had shown no fear or submission in court should weep like this at the sight of his father and even swear never to steal again.

Those who drew near said, "Could a father watch his son punished for theft without being distraught? Poor old man, that is why he cries so bitterly."

"You said Amma promised to have a gold bangle made for me. Do you remember, Appa? Look at what they have fastened on me instead: an iron bangle. Look at it, Appa! This is what theft brings. Is this all? The whole village will spit in contempt. Tomorrow the disgrace of being a man who went to prison will remain."

Crying "Muthu!" the old man fell before his son and wept, his hands reaching toward the young man's feet. They made Muthu enter the police van. Before climbing in, he looked around. Beneath a tamarind tree stood a woman wiping her eyes with the end of her sari. He looked toward her with a smile. The old man continued to cry, "Muthu! Muthu!" The van left.

"Were you born only to bring disgrace upon this family? You eat salt like any man; should you not possess feeling and honour? I labour like an ox to protect this household. You are twenty, yet fit for no work. Have you ever bent your body and laboured? It has been three years since your sister came of age. I suffer wondering whom I can find to marry her. No fellow will come forward because the village laughs at your worthlessness. Why do you heap this burning anguish upon my stomach?"

Muthusamy had grown rapidly, but never remained steadily in any job. He had no education and could obtain only wage labour. Yet if anyone abused him with a few words, he could not bear it. Rage took hold of him and he acted blindly.

"What cannot be controlled at home must be controlled by the village," people would say within his hearing.

In the settlement where they lived, other households strictly ordered their children not even to speak to Muthusamy. Young men his age felt ashamed to associate with him.

The only person who continued to show affection, laugh, play, and talk with him was his little "sister." She was not his full sister. His own sister had come to believe that her brother was the reason she remained unmarried and had begun to snarl whenever she saw him. The affectionate child was eight-year-old Pattu, daughter of his father's second wife.

At times Muthu's father Appasamy would lament, "That blessed woman escaped to a good end, still wearing flowers and turmeric, without seeing this horror."

At other times he would cry, "Sinner! You closed your eyes after tying this curse to my head and telling me to suffer."

For thirty years Appasamy had worked honestly and regularly in the same place and earned a reputation for humility. Beginning at a salary of eight rupees, he had risen to sixty under Emperumal Chettiar, a man who did not easily trust anyone.

After his first wife died, leaving him the two treasures Muthu and Manickam, Appasamy had been compelled to take Valli as his second wife. Many good matches had once come for her. Whenever angry, Valli complained, "My father had no means and gave me away as a second wife." Appasamy privately admitted the truth in her words.

Valli's daughter was eight-year-old Pattu, and it was she who loved Muthu so dearly.

"Anna! Come! Appa is not here. Come freely," she alone would welcome him.

"Even elder sister is angry with you, Anna. She told me too that I must not speak to you," Pattu would report.

If Muthu went even one day without seeing her, his heart felt troubled. Valli had tried every threat and even beat the child, but Pattu would not submit. The moment she heard Muthusamy's voice, she pushed past everyone, ran to embrace his neck, and brought him home. Pattu's affection alone kept Muthu's connection with the family from being severed entirely.

Muthusamy showed no interest in studies. People tried placing him in carpentry, printing, and construction, but no one offered affection or concern. Everyone seemed eager only to mock, abuse, strike, or kick him. A feeling of revulsion grew in him. He concluded that no one in the world wished to help him live.

When people see flies and ants, they either strike them or leave them to their fate. Though they saw him in human form, Muthu felt they cast him aside as no better than an insect. At first he felt anger and sorrow. Later those feelings vanished, leaving only hatred for the world and a peculiar boldness: the conviction that no one could do anything to him.

They say bamboo must be bent into useful form while it is young. Muthusamy should have been guided when he was a child. But did Appasamy have time? Even if he worked until his bones broke from dawn until lamps were lit, his master called him a lazy donkey. Where was the time to explain properly to his son what was good and what was evil? It was doubtful whether Appasamy even possessed the skill. He had no time to raise the boy with a judicious mixture of sternness and tenderness.

Valli would begin, "Have you heard the outrage your son committed?"

"I have no time for your sermon. The master ordered me to buy lemons, and I must go to the Iluppur market," Appasamy would say as he ran away. He tried advising Muthu properly once or twice and also tried beating him. There was no sign of reform.

As a child, Muthusamy became uncontrollably angry when he saw Emperumal humiliate and torment Appasamy.

Appasamy taught him to speak respectfully to elders. Emperumal was only thirty, while Appasamy was over fifty. Yet how contemptuously Emperumal spoke to him! Should he not show respect to an older man? He did not; he even struck him. Was this not injustice? Why did no one question it? Muthusamy's young heart seethed.

Only at eighteen did he clearly understand that the wealthy were not required to obey the moral rules binding everyone else.

One day Emperumal called Appasamy a lazy, hulking donkey. Muthusamy could no longer restrain himself.

"You are the donkey! You are the lazy one!" he shouted at Emperumal.

"What did you say?" Emperumal roared.

Appasamy sprang upon eight-year-old Muthu and beat him until his back was nearly split. Emperumal did not offer even the conventional protest, "Enough, let the child go." Instead he cursed him: "Is this how you raise a boy? Look, he will be beaten and kicked and become a street rowdy."

Whenever Muthu saw Appasamy standing meekly before Emperumal, rage overwhelmed him. Once Appasamy learnt that Muthu had watched men playing cards at a nearby rest house and beat the child mercilessly. But Muthu saw Emperumal playing cards with friends. Emperumal's eight-year-old son told him, "Play this one! Do not play that one!" and Emperumal kissed the child in admiration of his cleverness.

What kind of world was this? Everything Appasamy forbade occurred in Emperumal's world without rebuke or punishment. Should not justice be common to all?

Only much later did Muthu learn the truth: Emperumal's world existed to proclaim how justice ought to be, and Appasamy's world existed to listen and obey. Finding no place in either, Muthu entered a third world, the world of rowdies.

Valli said, "No matter how much affection I show, will the world believe it? It will say a second wife tortures the first wife's children. A child should be beaten when he does wrong, but the moment I touch him he runs screaming loudly enough for the end of the street to hear. The village curses me. I do not want the bad name. Let his father see, question, or cut him down. I shall not touch him. When I struck him once, the toothless old woman next door came to advise me: 'Valli, Muthu is a motherless boy. Do not be cruel to him.' Why invite needless trouble? If he had been born from my womb, I would strip the skin from his body and rub it with salt. If he will not submit, what does it matter whether he lives or dies?"

Afflicted by weariness, anger, irritation, and poverty, Valli could speak only like this.

No circumstances therefore arose that might prevent Muthusamy from going astray. He came home only for Pattu, bringing a toy or sweet before leaving. Valli tried and failed to stop it, then gave up. Besides, the toys he brought were good ones.

Muthu had earned the names rowdy, wanderer, and disobedient son, but never thief. Only in extreme anger would Valli shout, "If he does not steal and go to prison, you may change my name!"

No one in the village was surprised when idle, unruly Muthu was caught after stealing four gold bangles from Emperumal Chettiar's house and sent to prison. Everyone said this was bound to happen. Muthu did not deny the charge.

"Do you admit the theft?"

"Yes. I stole them."

"Why did you steal?"

"What kind of question is that, sir? Asking how I stole would have some meaning. Is 'Why did you steal?' a question? I had nothing, so I stole. They were there, so I stole. I wanted them, so I stole. If I asked, he would not give them, so I stole."

Many in court laughed. Muthusamy said it mockingly, not angrily. Appasamy stood in a corner with his head hanging.

Since it was his first offence, the magistrate sentenced him to six months' rigorous imprisonment. Muthu looked at the weeping Appasamy and shook his head as if to say, "Do not cry."

Kuttiammal, the shaven-headed woman who watered the plants and tended the cattle in Emperumal Chettiar's garden, had given her heart to Muthusamy. Muthu secretly entered the garden to see her. He relished the leftover rice she served him as though it were milk payasam, and listened without anger to her advice.

"In this enormous world, are you the only man unable to find work? You keep asking me to marry you. If someone asks what my husband does, what shall I say?"

"Say that, like Emperumal, my husband does not stand with folded hands and work under anyone."

"Fine talk! He has lakhs upon lakhs and can sit idle and eat. Are you like him?"

"Then say that there is one justice for those who have and another for those who do not."

"I do not know how to argue about all this. Tell me whether you have the means to marry me and give me a decent life."

Thus Muthusamy and Kuttiammal talked. Muthu never grew angry with her; so much sweetness was mixed into her speech.

That night too Muthu went only to speak with her. When he noticed someone coming toward the garden, instead of running elsewhere he leapt onto the house's flat roof. Finding no convenient hiding place, he entered the room there. It was Emperumal's bedroom.

Alarmed by approaching footsteps, he lay under the cot and became rigid as a log. Someone entered. Muthu thought he was finished and would be caught. Sweat covered him.

The figure gently closed the door, crept toward the iron safe, and opened it. Muthu almost wanted to laugh. The person took out a jewel box, removed four gold bangles, and secured them in his waistcloth.

Muthu peered at the face and recoiled in shock.

After locking the safe as before, the thief left. For several seconds Muthu did not even rise. Then he slowly came out, picked up the upper cloth the thief had dropped in his haste, and went outside.

Though he knew no one had seen him, he did not leave immediately. He stood thinking. Within moments he reached a decision and knocked a chair loudly to the floor.

"Who is there?" came a shout. Muthu recognized Emperumal's voice, and a peculiar light appeared on his face. He ran here and there on the roof.

"Thief! Thief!" Emperumal cried.

Servants ran upstairs asking, "Where? Where?" Muthu jumped toward the garden.

"There! There!" they shouted and gave chase. Muthu ran quickly while making sure they continued to follow. The street filled with commotion, and the police arrived and pursued him.

Muthu could have escaped by entering narrow lanes and reaching the lakeshore, but he continued straight along the highway, keeping himself within the police's view.

He entered his own house and bolted the door. Appasamy saw him and sprang up with a cry. Muthu seized his hand, pulled him into the inner room, threw the upper cloth before him, and said, "At once! The police are coming. Take them out! Take them out!"

Appasamy stared in shock.

"Appa, the police have arrived. Hear them knocking at the street door."

Tears filled Appasamy's eyes. He pointed to an old cloth bundle, put his hands on his head, and sat in a corner.

Muthusamy took the bangles from the bundle, placed them in his lap, and opened the street door. The policemen sprang upon him.

Muthu was neither startled nor afraid. Calmly he said, "Why, you came looking for me, did you not? Very well, come."

At the police lockup, he gave the bangles to the officer and made a statement admitting that he had stolen them.

"Appasamy! Did I not warn you? I told you not to let that fellow into your house, that he would bring you disgrace. Did you listen? He has displayed his skill right here! See how much shame and humiliation he has brought you. Such creatures ought to be cut down. Better that he die than that you should have a son like this," Emperumal said furiously.

Appasamy fell at his feet and begged, "Please, sir! Do not utter any curse against my boy."

"A father should possess affection for his child, but for an honest man like you even to call such a thieving fellow his son is a disgrace," Emperumal replied.

Appasamy beat his head and wept.

However fiercely they quarrelled, Appasamy had never raised a hand against Valli. But on returning home from court that day, when she reviled Muthu for bringing disgrace upon the family, Appasamy struck her in anger.

"Do not say a word about him! What right have you to abuse him? Do not speak contemptuously of Muthu because he stole and went to prison. I will not permit you to utter even one degrading word about him," Appasamy said with unexpected passion.

Valli thought his mind had become confused because his son had stolen and gone to prison.

Without speaking to anyone, Appasamy sat in a corner shedding tears. Valli had an important matter she simply had to tell him. She decided to begin gently.

"Do not cry. Some evil hour of ours gave Muthu this bad impulse. Do not grieve. Our hardship will end with this. You will see, he too will reform and become a good man," she said, offering Appasamy coffee. He did not even drink it.

"Do you know something? Among the old things in our house, there was that small ceremonial tray you said elder sister used for worship, the one we thought was brass. It is not brass. It is gold, real gold!"

Appasamy stared at Valli.

She brought the gleaming tray from inside and showed it to him.

"This is the one we thought was brass. My brother came, and you know he works in a jewellery shop. He looked at it and said, 'Valli! Who told you this was brass? It is genuine gold. It merely looks like brass because it has not been polished.' He took it away, polished it, and brought it back. This tray is gold! All this time we were deceived, thinking it brass."

Without taking the tray into his hand, Appasamy spoke in a low but deeply emotional voice.

"Yes, Valli. It is genuine gold. What we had thought was brass is not brass at all. It is pure gold."

Valli understood nothing.

## Translator's Notes

The title's metal comparison is the story's central moral reversal: Muthu, dismissed as worthless “brass,” proves to be “pure gold” when he knowingly accepts punishment for Appasamy's theft. The Tamil source is preserved verbatim; period caste, gender, labour, punishment, and stigmatizing social language is translated as narrative context rather than endorsement.

## English Translation

## A Difficult Problem

My friend Marudhavanam Pillai is a magistrate. He appears angry only in court. Even that, he refuses to call anger. "It is the embodiment of justice!" he says.

"Whatever you may say, Marudhu, I do not like justice assuming such a threatening form," I sometimes tell him, though never during court hours.

One evening, on seeing me, Marudhavanam Pillai said, "Come, man! I was looking for you. Tomorrow I must deliver judgment in a case. I want to tell you about it and learn what your verdict would be."

"Is this my work? The government pays you. You must read the law, analyse the events, punish the wicked and protect the good. Why give me that work?" I asked in jest.

"Imagine that you yourself have obtained the magistrate's post," said Marudhavanam, with the cheerfulness that naturally blossomed in him during the evening.

"Only if I were to obtain that post!" I said with a little dignity.

Marudhavanam began in his magistrate's voice.

"Name: Mahalingam. Age: thirty. No permanent occupation. Charge: he fraudulently sold a brass chain as a gold chain. That is the case. Judgment must be given."

"Very well. What is Mahalingam's statement?" I asked, like an official.

"There is no complication there. He admits it," said Marudhavanam.

"Is that all? What room is there for deliberation? The accused admits the offence. You need only consult the law book, find the section covering the offence and the prescribed punishment. What is there here with which to test me?"

"He sold a plated chain as gold and has admitted doing so. Although the evidence too is spotless, this remains a difficult problem," said Marudhavanam.

"You are speaking strangely," I said, with some respect, after seeing lines gather upon my friend's face.

Marudhavanam remained silent for a while. He looked troubled. I felt I ought to say something.

"Do you know this man Mahalingam?" I asked.

Marudhavanam's body shook at once. In the voice of a man pricked by a thorn, he cried, "What if I know him, and what if I do not? Do friendship and kinship exist in the eyes of justice? Madman! Such a thought never arises in me, and it should arise in no one. The wheel of justice is no ordinary thing."

It appeared I might have to listen to his sermon far into the night, and I grew a little afraid.

"There is proof that Mahalingam is a thief, a scoundrel, and a drunkard," my friend said, as though speaking to himself.

"When there is proof, you must punish the thief and scoundrel. There is no place for mercy or indulgence. Why deliberate further?" I became the magistrate; Magistrate Marudhavanam Pillai became a peculiar advocate.

"Just as there is evidence that Mahalingam is a thief, a scoundrel, and a drunkard, there is evidence that he is an innocent, worthy man who was himself deceived," he said.

It truly was a difficult problem. I understood that my friend had reason to worry.

"What an odd thing to say! How can the same man be both worthy and unworthy? It is like declaring a day to be both new moon and full moon," I said, dragging a simile to my aid.

Marudhavanam did not falter. "Do you know that a green chameleon changes colour repeatedly?"

"Yes," I drawled.

"What do you mean, yes? You say yes to everything. Is burning a town wrong?" he asked.

Startled, I said, "Certainly it is wrong."

"Wrong indeed. But was the burning of Lanka not regarded as a sacred deed?"

Fearing that the dispute was growing, I cast out an unnecessary sentence: "That was different. Hanuman burned the demon's city. That was..."

"An act of God. That is your argument, is it not?" My friend completed my sentence and continued. "Mahalingam is both worthy and unworthy. It is true that he deceived someone, and it is also true that he was deceived."

"I do not understand. Did the witnesses in court say that he was worthy, innocent, and deceived?"

"That is precisely what they did not say. There is no such evidence in court."

He went inside and returned with several letters. He picked one up.

"Look. This is the testimony of moneylender Gulabchand."

"This man came to our shop. It was rather dark. The lamp in our shop was not good either; it is the time of oil rationing. He gave us a chain. 'How many sovereigns?' we asked. 'Weigh it and see, Seth,' he said. We grew suspicious. Why would the man who brought the chain not state its weight? Once we became suspicious, we sent for the police. This has been our way of doing business for ten years. I said I was going for tea. Constable Number 308 was near the corner shop. I called him and showed him the chain. The man was locked up. By Ramji's grace, we escaped being caught in danger."

"Here is another witness, Sadasivam's statement: 'Mahalingam called me and said he would give me half the proceeds if I sold the gold-plated chain for him. He showed me the chain. I told him I could not become an accomplice to such theft.'"

"Constable Number 308 stated: 'When the Seth called me, I went to the shop. Fear appeared on this man's face as soon as he saw me. I asked what the chain was. He stood like a tree without answering. Meanwhile the Seth rubbed it and examined it. "Arre! This is not gold; it is brass," he said. I immediately locked the man up.'"

Marudhavanam said the evidence ran in this fashion and sat exhausted. I could not understand why he suffered when the case was so clear.

"He has been properly caught. What remains for me to say?" I asked.

Marudhavanam then read another account.

"I had a severe fever. Even the doctor was alarmed. He said there was much congestion in my chest and that breathing itself was difficult. What could my wife do? She had no jewellery on her. She once had a bangle, but it had been disposed of the previous month. There were not even vessels in the house. In this condition the doctor said an injection costing ten rupees was required and that money had to be found.

"My wife went weeping to the owner whose cart I drove. She explained that I had been abandoned without help and was in mortal distress. The murderous sinner showed no pity. Though I had been bedridden for ten days and half my life had gone, he refused to give even one paisa, saying that I already owed him six rupees. My wife apparently begged him to save her marriage by saving my life. He drove her away, telling her to walk around the temple and beg! I had laboured like an ox for him, but when my life was leaving, he refused ten rupees.

"Such stone-hearted men live while labourers toil even for a quarter-filled stomach. I despaired and thought I must simply die. If I died, what would become of Manga? My son Rangan was four and would wander in the street. In this state she was eight months pregnant. There was no one in the world to save me from death. Manga sat weeping.

"At that moment the blessed Mahalingam arrived. May he live a full life free of illness. On seeing my condition, he cried aloud. 'Varada! I did not know your fever was so severe,' he grieved. 'Sister, do not cry. God will show a good way,' he consoled my wife.

"His affection was worth more to me than the help of a thousand doctors. I had been left to die by the place where I had worked without anyone's help; he showed compassion to this useless log and comforted me. Seeing his charitable mind, I raised my hands in worship.

"My wife poured out the anger she had restrained until then. 'What way is God going to show me? Does God exist? Where is he? My husband is being tormented before me. I open my eyes and shed pots of tears. What does God do? He makes the poor wander in the streets. God indeed! The saying that God helps the helpless is a grandmother's tale.'

"'Do not cry, sister. It is because God exists that he sent me here,' said that embodiment of peace.

"'Yes, people with tender hearts like yours have to arrive and save God's honour,' she replied in agitation.

"She told him the doctor wanted ten rupees. Poor Mahalingam was frightened at the sum. Where could he have so much money? He was poor like me. I had one child; he had four. I at least had a place of work, while no job remained steady for him. He had an ailing wife, an aged mother, and no income. In such circumstances, could he produce ten rupees?

"'It is enough if you merely remain beside me. That is equal to ten thousand rupees. If a noble friend like you is near when my life departs, that alone will reduce the agony of death,' I said weakly.

"He rose. 'I shall return now,' he said. I thought he had gone away. Within half an hour he returned with the doctor. The doctor administered the injection and asked me for no money. From then on the fever gradually declined. Until I regained strength to rise and walk, Mahalingam bought me bread too.

"I asked countless times where he found the money. That charitable man never answered. That generous soul, that blessed spirit, that incarnation saved my life. The poor help the poor; only the poor become the god who gives life to another poor man. Mahalingam is no ordinary birth. He is a divine birth."

"That is cart driver Varadan's statement, not given in court but told directly to me. Falling at my feet, he begged me to save that compassionate man somehow," said Marudhavanam.

"It did not occur to a doctor that a life might be lost and that he should offer whatever help he could. It did not occur to a rich employer to lend ten rupees to a man who had served him like a dog and now lay in danger of death. Yet the heart of destitute Mahalingam overflowed with such affection. He trembled at the sight of a life trembling and was moved by a family's tears. Mahalingam is worthy, charitable, virtuous, and, as Varadan said, a divine birth."

The magistrate sighed. I forgot myself in astonishment. What a great karma yogi Mahalingam was! What compassion filled his heart! How praiseworthy was his deed! I extolled him.

"But do not forget the offence Mahalingam committed," the magistrate reminded me.

When I remembered that he was to be punished, my body shook. Punishment for such a man! Was there no reward for the affection, humanity, and aid he offered in danger? Did no one take these into account? No, because he was a thief. The man of sacrifice became a thief! I had foolishly asked whether new moon could exist within full moon. Here it was before me. Mahalingam, who poured out compassion, had committed theft!

"Hear another statement," said Marudhavanam.

"'Lord of justice! Mahalingam is a very worthy man. My son has a large family and will perform with his head any work another orders with a foot, merely to survive. He has been honourable since childhood. His is a life of hardship, yet if anyone comes saying they are hungry, he will feed them even if there is not enough for his own stomach. He has no evil thought. If you do not save him, his wife and four children will stand in the middle of the street. So will I. I am over sixty.'

"Mahalingam's mother said this, fell at my feet, and wept. Her statement establishes that he is innocent, worthy, and affectionate. Yet the case is plain. He is a thief and has admitted the offence. Why did such a worthy man become unworthy?"

"My head spins hearing this," I said. It truly did.

"Our gardener Duraisami said even more. Mahalingam always helped the poor. If someone struggled in the street with a burden too heavy to lift, he came to help. If a rowdy picked a quarrel, Mahalingam tried to make peace. Even if the rowdy struck him, he endured it. If orphans were found, he collected handfuls of rice from house to house and fed them.

"Once someone stole coconuts from our garden. Thinking Mahalingam had done it, Duraisami dragged him into a quarrel and beat him severely. Mahalingam endured every blow and swore that he had not stolen them. Finally another man was discovered to be the thief. Unable to bear his remorse, Duraisami went and asked Mahalingam's pardon.

"'What is so remarkable? It was merely a moment of poisonous anger,' Mahalingam said patiently.

"Duraisami said he had never seen such patience. Mahalingam was very worthy. When he performed unusually hard labour, he drank, but he was not one who drank and created a scene. 'If I had plenty of money, I would help all the poor. I possess nothing. What can I do?' he would lament."

"Alas! Why did such a man conceive an evil thought?" I asked with pity.

"Why ask? If I tell you, you will faint. Wherever there was distress, Mahalingam stood first to help. He bought medicine for the sick. If a poor person's house collapsed, he worked without wages for mere gruel. If a lost child appeared, he attended to no other work until he found the home and returned the child. Because all his care and attention went into helping others, even the work he obtained never lasted. 'Here comes the benefactor. Why does this man need a job?' employers mocked and dismissed him.

"His family was large and difficult to maintain. He helped without understanding the limits of his strength, which brought further expense. Such was his life.

"At this time he bought a lottery ticket and won one hundred rupees. He rejoiced. His debts could be cleared, his family made comfortable, and he could help others too.

"Then a man with filthy clothes, dishevelled hair, and an unsteady gait came to Mahalingam like Saturn himself. At the sight of his condition, Mahalingam's heart melted like syrup. 'Alas! How many days has it been since you ate? The very sight is pitiable,' he said, and took the man home.

"After freely eating a meal obtained without labour, the stranger began his story. 'Sir, I came on foot from Burma. I was a great merchant in Rangoon. Evil times reduced me to this condition.'

"Mahalingam had already heard of the terrible dangers and suffering endured by people returning from Burma: people who went ten days without food, people killed by snakebite, and people murdered by forest tribes. The moment the man said he had come from Burma, tears filled Mahalingam's eyes.

"'My whole family has been destroyed, son,' the stranger said. Mahalingam wept.

"'Somehow I arrived here. I have no refuge and do not know where to go. I found you like Lord Palani himself,' the man said, bowing.

"'Sir, an elder like you must not bow to this poor fellow. What great help have I given you?' Mahalingam replied.

"The stranger then slowly took a chain from his garment. 'Son, this weighs three sovereigns. I must sell it. With the money I can return to my village, open some little shop, and survive. I am new to this town. If I take the jewel to the market, they will not buy it easily. Seeing my disordered condition, they may suspect it is stolen property. Therefore do me a favour. It is three sovereigns and worth two hundred rupees at today's price. Give me merely one hundred rupees and take the chain,' he begged.

"Mahalingam did not refuse. He gave the entire lottery prize to the Burma refugee and sent him away. Later he brought that very chain to the Marwari shop to sell and was caught.

"Mahalingam's wife, with her four children, fell at my feet and gave this statement," Marudhavanam said.

I leapt with joy. "Can we not release Mahalingam on the strength of this statement alone?"

Marudhavanam smiled. "How can judgment be delivered that way? The law gives no room. We do not know who played the Burma refugee. Moreover, the police found materials for plating metal in Mahalingam's house."

"The rogue who came disguised as a Burma refugee must have left the plating materials there," I said.

"Perhaps. But do we not need proof? Mahalingam has no permanent occupation. He tried to sell a plated jewel and admits it. Plating implements were found in his house. There is evidence sufficient for punishment. What am I to do?"

"It is indeed a difficult problem," was all I could say.

Am I the one who must pass judgment? My judgment was recorded long ago, not merely for one case but as a general principle: the cause of crimes committed by the poor is their poverty. Therefore, it is not individuals who must be punished but the system of economic inequality. That is my verdict.

But Magistrate Marudhavanam Pillai asks what judgment he should deliver under the law in this Mahalingam case. You tell us. Let us see!

21 January 1945

## Translator's Notes

`துஷ்ட நிக்ரஹ சிஷ்ட பரிபாலனம்` is the conventional duty to punish the wicked and protect the good. `ருஜு` means proof or legally cognisable evidence. `தயவு தாட்சண்யம்` denotes mercy or indulgent consideration. The Lanka-burning comparison invokes Hanuman's burning of Ravana's city. `மாங்கல்ய பிச்சை` is a wife's plea that another save her husband's life and thereby preserve her married status. `பிடிசோறு` refers to handfuls of rice collected from different homes for someone in need. The final distinction between evidence accepted by a court and morally relevant testimony outside court is central to the story's challenge.

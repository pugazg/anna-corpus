## English Translation

Tamil title: சொக்கி

"This is only a fruit shop, not a shop selling sovereigns and jewellery! One buys a basket for so much and sells a dozen for so much, and must support a family on whatever profit remains. If ten fruits rot, a day's income is gone. One must keep them from spoiling.

"Is business today what it once was? Customers offer only two annas and demand genuine Nagpur oranges. If you say a Kashmiri pomegranate costs one and a half rupees, do they believe you? They demand that you break it open. They put a little in their mouths, chew, and say, 'Is this Kashmiri? Whom are you trying to deceive? Do you think this is the first time I have bought a Kashmiri pomegranate?'

"Some days uncontrollable anger rises. One feels like saying, 'Go straight to a market street in Kashmir and bring back baskets of pomegranates yourself!' But can one say that? The business would be lost, so one must endure."

Fruit-seller Paramasivam sat brooding in this fashion. Yet it was the income from that shop that had enabled him to buy a terraced house for four thousand rupees and land for six thousand.

He had only one son and hoped the boy would take up some other occupation. He tried, but could not arrange it. When rheumatism disabled Paramasivam, Poongavanam had to assume charge of the fruit shop.

Paramasivam taught his son every turn and subtlety of the trade that he could. What use was it? Poongavanam could not please customers.

Veliyappan behaved so skilfully that many said he had been born for the art. He opened a new shop opposite Paramasivam's. Where he had learnt it no one knew: a graceful smile, a melting salutation. Within three months he drew into his net customers who had patronized Paramasivam for ten years.

"Sweet sir! Taste one segment. Never mind if you do not buy, but do not find fault with our stock."

"You want Nagpur oranges? I can give you basket after basket. But this is actually our Salem produce. Just put it in your mouth and see. What can Nagpur do beside this fruit?"

"That is a different kind of guava, sir, the kind that causes stomach pain and contains many seeds. Look at this one: no seeds at all, and soft as butter."

"Hill bananas come specially to our shop from Kodaikanal near Madurai. You cannot get them elsewhere. Only three dozen remain. The merchant from Third Lantern Street swept away eight dozen."

He said all manner of things, somehow made visitors buy, and watched his business grow.

"Paramasivam's business has had its day. The son has no talent. Look at the shop sleeping. Ask for mangoes and he gives bananas. Ask for oranges and he asks whether you want sweet limes. Is Poongavanam fit to run a fruit shop?"

Customers themselves spoke so, and the words reached Poongavanam's ears. He could see the brisk trade in the opposite shop. What could he do with that knowledge?

It was a small town inhabited by people dependent on agriculture and pensioners who had retired exhausted from government service. Two fruit shops were too many. Paramasivam had prospered while his was the only one. The rival shop ruined the trade.

Could he go to Veliyappan and plead, "Appa, this town cannot support two shops. Do not compete and destroy me"?

Confined at home by illness, Paramasivam lamented, "Our son cannot compete in trade even with a fellow who arrived yesterday. Worthless boy! He need say nothing else. If he merely tells people, 'This is our Paramasivam's fruit shop,' would they even look toward another shop?"

His son would never follow any immoral path and had no bad habits. Paramasivam worried only that he lacked business skill.

As always, Poongavanam polished the fruit and arranged it attractively. The moment one side began to spoil, he removed the fruit and hid it separately. As always, he showed incense and flame before the picture of Venkatachalapathi and came to the shop only after consulting omens. He never failed to place a vermilion mark on the cash box. Everything continued as before, except the business.

Those who saw Poongavanam noticed a light in his eyes and a smile at the edge of his lips. Paramasivam worried because trade was poor, but his son seemed captivated by sweet flute music.

That was how sweet Sokki's speech was. Speech alone? Her gaze, walk, and laughter too.

Sokki belonged neither to another town nor a strange place. She was his maternal uncle's daughter. Her father Malaisamy had leased a mango grove. Sokki brought baskets of mangoes to the fruit shop and sometimes took other fruit from it to sell around town.

"I kept fearing you might not come today, Sokki. My heart was in a panic."

"Look at this man! A woman's heart like mine may melt or dissolve, but should the heart of such a big man panic?"

"Are you asking me that question?"

"Why? Should I go ask your father?"

"Go ask the priest at the Hanuman temple."

"Is Hanuman found only in the temple?"

"What did you say, Sokki? Are you hinting that I am Hanuman?"

"Why would you be Hanuman? Hanuman carried a great mountain. If there is a basket full of mangoes, you need another man merely to help lift it. Would I call you Hanuman?"

"Your impudence grows every day, Sokki."

"Let it. What is that to you? The man who marries me must worry about it. Why should you?"

"Which fellow is going to carry away my property?"

"Your property? Who, me? Look at his desire! It is by filling yourself with such useless desires that you spoil your business. I can see it."

"My business is ruined? Do you mean mine? Are you looking at the crowd always gathered in Veliyappan's shop? Madness! He deliberately cuts the price. Just watch the enormous loss that will fall upon his head."

"Leave what may fall upon his head. Have you seen the ring that has climbed onto his hand?"

"Is it my work to inspect rings on Veliyappan's fingers?"

Whether Poongavanam noticed or not, Sokki had watched rings appear on Veliyappan's fingers, a gold-chained watch on his wrist, and a new polish on his body. Whenever she delivered three baskets of mangoes to his shop, how could his progress escape her eyes? Veliyappan too was no stranger, but a distant relation.

Sokki spent a little time here and a little there. She was guileless, of marriageable age, and beautiful enough to cast a light of joy through any house she entered.

They say beauty has many forms, of which two are important. One resembles milk, the other liquor. The first is sweet and gives strength; the second intoxicates. Sokki was a pot of milk. She knew none of the glittering, swaying, affected gestures that induce intoxication. She was no fashionable lady carrying cosmetics and ornaments because youth had not given her its natural charm. She was like a red tender coconut hanging over the riverbank, or red sugarcane on a field ridge. One may paint bamboo and make it look like sugarcane; it does not become sugarcane.

A trained dancer might imitate the beauty of Sokki walking with a basket at her hip, but it would only be imitation. Sokki's movement was wholly different, not learnt through habit or training, but given by nature through her bodily form and innocent heart.

Poor Poongavanam could not analyse any of this. He only knew that his heart found no peace on a day when he had not seen her.

Even while Sokki was still some distance away, she would call out or speak to someone. At the sound of her voice, Poongavanam forgot himself. As she talked, he became like a man drinking fruit juice, and a fresh radiance bloomed on his face.

Poongavanam naturally had a dignified appearance. Anyone seeing him would say without hesitation that he could harm no one. Yet when his father spoke of marriage, he would say coaxingly, "Why marry me now?"

"Chinnappan is your age and has three children," Paramasivam would reply.

"I am only three years older than Sokki," Poongavanam would say, indirectly revealing his wish.

Understanding him, his father would remark, "I hear they intend to give Sokki to Veliyappan."

Poongavanam would become furious, go to bed without eating, lie without sleeping, and cry without shedding tears. He muttered verses of Pattinathar and told his mother he would shave his head once for all his troubles and become an ascetic.

"It seems Sokki has sprinkled enchantment powder over our son," his mother told Paramasivam.

Paramasivam sang an old line, "She winked and severed the neck."

"Is youth returning?" his wife asked, suppressing open laughter.

After Paramasivam said Sokki was to marry Veliyappan, Poongavanam grew angry even at the sight of her entering the rival shop.

"Which fortress did you spend so long planning to capture?"

"Must I report that conversation to you?"

"If it were something you could tell others, you would answer at once."

"Why are you so jealous of Veli?"

"You used to call him elder brother."

"I do not call him that now."

"Has Veliyappan become 'he' with respect?"

"He has a good nature. You do not know him. He speaks so cleverly that time disappears while talking to him."

"Say that he has blinded you."

"Why are you speaking strangely? Why such hatred toward him?"

"He is a great emperor who has carried away my crowned queen. That is why I am angry. Go and do whatever pleases you. Can I stop you?"

"I tell you, you are jealous of him. You envy the rapid progress of his business. Why not trade as cleverly and accumulate money as he has? Who prevents you? Does he stand in your way?"

"I do not need to accumulate money."

"Why not? Oh, I understand. You are going to become an ascetic, so why would you need money?"

"Did Amma tell you, Sokki?"

"She did. 'Sokki, do not think my son an ordinary man. He is about to become a holy ascetic. Then iron will become gold at his touch and iluppai flowers will turn to jasmine,' she told me."

However angry he was, Sokki's playful speech drove the anger away. But when he saw her talking and laughing at Veliyappan's shop, fury rose until he felt like running over and cutting both down with a single stroke.

That irritation made him drive away even customers with, "One price only. Yes, look elsewhere." At times he realized what he was doing, but only after the fury subsided.

"Do you know something? Veli sings very well."

"Is that so? It sounds as though you kept time for him."

"See me coming to tell you, though I know how jealous you are of him."

"Why should I envy him? Has he won a great medal as a master musician?"

"Watch whether he wins a medal in the coming month of Vaikasi."

"Has the date been fixed too? Is it an auspicious time?"

"Why twist one thing into another? I mean the drama at the goddess festival in Vaikasi. Veliyappan will act in it. The medal will certainly be his; he sings so well."

Poongavanam decided that he too would act. Everyone, including Veliyappan, welcomed him. Sokki alone opposed it.

"You cannot compete with him. Just watch what happens," she warned.

Poongavanam was not discouraged.

The performance took place. There was uproar, praise, and applause for Veliyappan's songs. Poongavanam received only mocking cries: "Go inside! Sell fruit, fellow, fruit! Do not sing. Is that a song? How much for a dozen?"

As Sokki predicted, the gold medal went to Veliyappan.

Moorthy, a native of the town who had gone to the city years earlier and become a film producer, led that year's festival. It was from his hand that Veliyappan received the medal.

Poongavanam did not see the presentation. As the ridicule grew louder, he hurriedly removed his costume, went home, and lay down. For three days he did not go outside; chills and fever tormented him.

"How was my prophecy? He received the medal exactly as I said. Did you see?"

"A great medal! You alone may admire it. Am I going to join a drama company? What does it matter if I cannot sing?"

"Do you know he is going into cinema? Have you not noticed that he closed the shop?"

"No shop any more? Is it true? The sinful fellow has thrown earth upon his livelihood."

"His livelihood is not ruined. Moorthy, who heard him sing and speak in the play and gave him the medal, promised to put him in films and took him away."

"Did he go merely believing that promise?"

"He had been waiting eagerly for a chance to enter cinema."

"How do you know he longed for it?"

"He told me every day: 'Sokki, you alone must help me.'"

"What help could you give him? I do not understand."

"What have you ever understood, that you should understand this? How could you know the truth, that Veliyappan won the medal and a place in cinema because of me? You cannot even tell whether your fruit has shrivelled or rotted."

"Leave the mockery and tell me the details, Sokki."

"You know Moorthy's elder sister Meenakshiammal. Her house is beside our grove."

"I know. What has she to do with this?"

"You would ask that. When Veliyappan learnt Moorthy would conduct the festival, he planned to join the play, display his talent, and find a path into cinema. He asked me to speak to Meenakshiammal and obtain Moorthy's help.

"I told her Veliyappan sang and acted well and looked magnificent in costume. I asked her to make certain that he received this year's medal. She said, 'Very well, Sokki,' and kept her word. Moorthy took Veliyappan straight into cinema. My worship bore fruit; the fruit slipped and fell into the milk."

"Your worship succeeded. I am very happy. Veliyappan will earn lakhs in cinema, cover you in diamond and gold jewellery, ride in a motorcar, and live in a bungalow. Live happily and joyfully."

"Wait. What do you imagine while saying this?"

"I understand perfectly. Why did you secure the medal and cinema chance? Is even that hard to understand? You intend to marry Veliyappan. That is why you speak with him so much."

"Speak! Say everything that comes into your head."

"Have I no other work? What does it matter to me who marries whom? Does anyone nowadays look at character? They look only at money. Will you alone be different?"

Poongavanam had no heart to continue. He turned to the business, and Sokki walked away laughing.

Since Veliyappan's shop had closed, its regular patrons came to Poongavanam. Trade was good.

Several days later, Poongavanam overheard his parents talking at home.

"Is what I heard true?"

"What did you hear?"

"Did Veliyappan's father go and ask Sokki's father to give her to his son?"

"Yes. I heard so too."

Poongavanam did not wish to hear more. Anger and sorrow seemed ready to tear his heart apart, and he went outside to wander.

People who had not visited the shop for many days began returning. Poongavanam's business grew rapidly. He rented the shop Veliyappan had vacated and used it to store stock. Paramasivam was delighted.

"When is the wedding? Have they fixed the date?" Poongavanam asked Sokki.

"The elders must choose the date. Should I choose it myself?"

"Why need elders? This is an age in which a woman chooses the bridegroom she wants."

"Even so, should they not come and ask the girl's parents whether they consent?"

"I hear they did ask your father."

"Do you know only that they asked, or also what my father said?"

"What would he say? Veliyappan has entered cinema and will become a wealthy gentleman. Would he refuse to give you?"

"He said he would not."

"What? He refused?"

"He said, 'I will not give my daughter to a man who has entered cinema. She is a village girl, a poor girl. One day, dazzled by the glitter of cinema, he might decide to drive Sokki away.' So he refused."

"What a pity! You struggled to obtain Meenakshiammal's recommendation and put Veliyappan into cinema, but your father has shattered your castle of dreams."

"Neither he nor anyone else can demolish my castle."

"He has demolished it now. You thought your father would happily and proudly consent because Veliyappan entered cinema, but it did not happen."

"I knew beforehand that my father would refuse to give me to Veliyappan if he entered cinema."

"Knowing your father's nature, why did you help him enter it?"

"Ask why. Appa had planned to give me to Veliyappan. He said the man traded well, lived locally, and belonged to a suitable, respectable family. You know Appa's obstinacy.

"I devised a trick. 'Anna, your voice is magnificent. If you enter cinema, you will become a great man,' I kept flattering Veliyappan. He already possessed the madness of wanting cinema. He lamented, 'Sokki, who do I have to recommend me?' I remembered Meenakshiammal, praised him to her, and it worked."

"What a scheme!"

"I had to attend to my own affairs, since you lack that ability. I knew that if Veliyappan entered cinema, Appa would refuse him, and that is what happened. And that was not all. I also understood that while Veliyappan ran a fruit shop here, your shop would continue to droop. Now I have struck down two mangoes with one stone. Have you seen Sokki's handiwork?"

"Sokki! Sokki! Then you wish to marry me?"

"Shall I write it and paste it on my forehead? Madman! Attend to your business. Our parents and elders will attend to the rest."

13 June 1955

## Translator's Notes

“Sokki” is retained as a personal name. The surrounding narration repeatedly plays on `சொக்கு/சொக்குதல்`, enchantment or becoming captivated, so translating the name itself would erase the wordplay. Period fruit varieties, currency, kinship, trade, cinema, and gender descriptions are retained in narrative context.

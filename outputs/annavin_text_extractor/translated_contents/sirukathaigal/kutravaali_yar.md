## English Translation

"A prostitute?" The question is born in anger.

"Yes." The answer comes in a voice filled with sorrow.

The questioner stands stunned. He expected her to lose her composure in shame. Her response is nothing of the kind. She does not stop speaking.

"Yes. Are you asking why?"

Humour breaks through even the sorrow in her voice.

"No," he says fearfully.

"Do not ask that question!" she says. Is she speaking, or mocking him?

"Why?" The man covers his terror by adopting a courageous manner.

"If you ask why I became a prostitute, you will acquire the task of discovering who the criminal is."

She was the one who said it. Yet she disappeared from the questioner's sight. Other forms appeared, indistinct but recognizably women, figures covered by a veil of smoke.

Who were they all? One looked like Vanaja. No, no. Was that Muni, the woman from the plantain-leaf garden? No, it resembled Kuppi of Curved Street.

Such thoughts filled his mind. Only one woman stood before him, but in his eyes she changed again and again into the forms of many women, all of whom had fed his sexual hunger, all of whom he had made prostitutes.

He could conduct the inquiry no further.

"The case is adjourned until tomorrow," he said briefly, and went into the mansion.

She returned home with a victorious smile.

A special conference took place at the mansion, not in its hall but in the cattle shed behind it.

"Yes, why did my master stare about in confusion? That thieving wretch rattled on so rapidly. Instead of using the staff in his hand to thrash her, why did he hang his head before her?"

Maruthayi asked this simply to clear a genuine doubt. For some reason, the natural question angered her husband Ponnan.

"Why do you possess such wicked thoughts and degraded intelligence? Poor man, something softened his heart, and he let that woman go with only a verbal warning. Yet you ask why he did not beat her to death. Why has your heart turned to stone?" Ponnan demanded.

Would Maruthayi let the matter go?

"I knew you would begin! You always melt when you see that crooked-parted woman. Have these ruined eyes gone blind? She bares her teeth, and you twist and bend before her. Is this a drama I have not seen? That is why pity melts you. You think the master should not punish her."

"Fine, woman. I know no sin."

"I know that too. You tried your performance, and it did not succeed."

"Go away, Maruthayi. I shall tell you the truth now. I too had my eye upon that woman for some time. Not now. It was before I married you."

"Having begun to speak, why pour out lies? Whenever she sees me, she asks like some great lady, speaking in a peculiar breathy manner, 'Dear woman, is your husband keeping well?'"

"Does she ask that? Ah, you never told me even once."

In the mansion hall, Malleesurar, who had gone to conduct an inquiry and instead invited anxiety upon himself, was reciting the *Tiruvachakam* in his customary sweet, soft voice.

One cannot even say that he read it. He knew the *Tiruvachakam* by heart and could sing without looking. His eyes moved across the book, but he did not see letters. He saw a garden, the foot of a well, a place where paddy was dried, a bhajan hall, and a hotel. All of them were cases that ought to be investigated.

Without realizing it, he smiled.

"You accursed woman! Did you drag my name into it?"

"Must I drag it in? Does Aiya not already know?"

"Wretched woman! What happened?"

"An inquiry."

"What did he ask?"

"He asked whether it was true that prostitution had occurred."

"Did he ask so plainly?"

"Not merely plainly. He asked abruptly, like a sharp slap upon the cheek: 'Are you a prostitute?'"

"And you?"

"I said yes."

"Boldly?"

"Not merely boldly. I asked as though scolding him: Why ask an unnecessary question? Have you no other work?"

"How much courage you possess!"

"How cowardly I used to be! For almost a month you encouraged me: 'Do not fear. I am here. Do not fear. Will your head fall off? Who will ever know? My elder brother is the great man here. No trouble will come to you while I am here.'

"That was why, when you first seized and pulled my hand in the brinjal garden, I shook violently with fear. Today I stood like a stone statue before your brother. But I did not remain silent like stone. I spoke."

"What did my brother say?"

"What could he say?"

"Do not take my life. Tell me what happened."

"He asked whether I truly was a prostitute. When your brother boldly asked that of me, whom his own younger brother had made a prostitute, I became angry.

"I said, 'Yes, I am a prostitute. Do you ask why I became one?'"

"Wretched woman! Did you yourself provoke him to ask?"

"Yes. Your brother became mute."

"Thank goodness, I escaped! If he had asked, 'Why did you become a prostitute? Who caused it?' what would have become of me? You would have said I was responsible."

"No. I would not have said that."

"Half my life has returned. I feared you might expose me."

"Why would I expose you, fool? You are not the first criminal. You are number two. Before I became prey to your desire..."

"Before? Before?"

"Deceitful woman! You acted the chaste wife in the brinjal garden. Were you already experienced before then? Donkey! Who was that fellow?"

"Someone older than you. Older not only in age, fool, but also in experience, skill, status, and education.

"Before I became your kept woman, I had been your sister-in-law. On that very day, before I saw you, you thought the brinjal garden suitable for your lustful sport. Your brother thought the ruined hall of the Kali temple was enough.

"I lay there asleep from exhaustion. I even fell at his feet. When he shook me awake and embraced me..."

"My brother?"

"Why not? Is he not a man too? More than that, he is a great man of the town, empowered to investigate and punish the poor. I am a poor widow. Must I not therefore become prey to a king of the jungle like your brother?"

"I cannot believe it."

"Go and ask your brother so that belief may arise. If you have courage, tell him to ask me at tomorrow's inquiry who made me a prostitute. I shall expose all your worthiness in broad daylight.

"Your brother dared accuse Velan and me of trying to flee the town and conduct an inquiry. Velan wished to protect me, one who had committed wrongdoing and also suffered cruelty. Before Mother Karali, he pledged himself and took my hand. Look, he even tied the marriage tali.

"He said, 'People will mock us only if we stay in this town. Come, let us go elsewhere and live there as husband and wife.' We set out. At the edge of the town, the hunting dogs you sent caught us. They beat Velan until he writhed and brought me here for inquiry.

"I have suffered enough pain. Let the inquiry proceed. Let us reveal everything that exists, so the town and the world may know. That is the resolve with which I spoke.

"Your brother is a coward, terrified that everything may emerge. You imagine that Velan lies there with his body swollen from beating, that the woman who tried to run has been brought back, and that now the hunt is yours. Do you know what I have resolved?"

"Why are you threatening me?"

That was the end of clear speech. Then came turmoil. Victory belonged to the woman. The lustful man lay in a flood of blood.

"Velan, let what has happened remain in the past. I forgive that woman. It was right for you to make her your wife. You must leave for another town before dawn. Our cart will be ready. Go. I have secretly explained the matter to the driver.

"If day breaks, the fellows in this village will say whatever comes to their mouths. They will demand that the woman who entered prostitution be punished. Therefore..."

Malleesurar was speaking to Velan when a terrible sound made them both start.

"Someone has fallen into the well!" Velan cried.

Malleesurar shouted for his men. Carrying torches, they ran there.

The well at the Kali temple was an unfathomable abyss. She was in it.

Two bodies.

The case lay beyond Malleesurar's authority. A police officer from the neighbouring town therefore appeared.

The panchayat members delivered their verdict for the inquest: Malleesurar's younger brother Markandan had vomited blood and died because a ghost struck him. Possessed and tormented by that ghost, Karuppi had fallen into the Kali temple well and died.

2 March 1947

## English Translation

“Sarasa is terribly wicked! She is an educated woman, so she finds fault with everything her mother says,” Kuppammal remarked on the bank of the village pond.

“But Sarasa is very beautiful, like a golden idol! She is her mother’s only daughter, and the poor girl has no father. Who knows from what distant land the husband destined to look after their property will come?” said Annammaal.

“Whenever I see Sarasu’s eyes, I am reminded of that woman in the cinema. What is her name? Devika Rani, or something like that,” said Sornam.

“Devika or Renukamba, how would I know any of that stuff? Sornam has had a little education.”

“She knows,” said Kuppammal.

“Whenever Sarasa comes to the pond, a song comes to my mind,” said Sornam.

“What song is that? Sing us a little,” everyone asked.

Sornam began to sing: “O maiden who came to let the lotus of your face appear upon a pond filled with blooming lotuses!”

“Look at this woman! She has quite a taste for beauty,” they all said.

“Yes, yes! Only if we go home quickly will the food there have any taste. Pick up your pots; let us go. Once we begin talking about Sarasa, we lose all sense of time,” said Annam. Everyone lifted their pots and went home.

Indeed, whenever anyone began talking about Sarasa, time passed unnoticed, just as Annam had said.

She was of medium height, with an eye-catching fair complexion. Her lovely eyes stretched towards her ears and were streaked red. Her cheeks were mirrors; her eyebrows could be called the bow of Manmatha. She had lips like the kovvai fruit. The graceful creeper possessed pearly teeth, but they appeared only on rare occasions, enchanted whoever saw them, and then vanished as if by magic.

There was hardly anyone who did not praise Sarasa as the embodiment of delight, a painting by nature, and a radiance of joy.

Sarasa was the Taj Mahal of that little village. She was the Koh-i-Noor of her family.

Sarasa was about eighteen. Her father had died when she was a little girl. Her mother passed her life taking ever-renewed delight in looking at her.

Just as Sarasa’s beauty continued to grow, so did her intelligence. By intelligence, one does not mean merely reading *Alli Arasani Malai*, *Nallathangal’s Lament*, and the story of the wondrous Valli who married Andhagan. Hers was rational intelligence.

The maiden looked like gold, and her intelligence flashed like a diamond. Her mother followed the customs and speech of the old days. With a faint smile, the daughter would tease her: “Mother, dear mother! Our mother was born and raised in the Krita Yuga. Mother, will you not show me the peepal tree that Akhilandam next door circumambulated? Has Balamma in the neighbouring house bought a cow solely to pour milk into the anthill?”

“Go away, you wicked girl! What do you know about peepal trees, banyan trees, and all that? Before powder and ribbons arrived, everyone knew the greatness of the neem tree. When your father was alive, this house used to be like a temple, do you know? You were born and drove even him away. Now everyone is afraid even to come here. While you were asleep yesterday, the priest of the Velaathamman temple came. He says the Goddess must be bathed in tender-coconut water.”

“Did you give him money, mother? Ten rupees or five? How many rupees did you give?” Sarasa would ask.

“Sarasa is a wicked girl. She reads all sorts of books and demands a reason for everything,” Sarasa’s mother would tell the priest, Subbayyar.

Subbayyar was the village priest. He kept every wealthy family in that district rolled and folded inside his almanac. From estate affairs down to buying a buffalo for the house, nothing happened without consulting Subbu Sastri. Such was the Iyer’s influence.

After bathing in the morning, applying sacred ash, and setting out with the bundle of almanacs under his arm, the Iyer would not return home until after noon. There would also be two or three coins tucked into his waistcloth.

“Kamalam! My girl, Kamalam! Have you fallen asleep?” he would call, knocking on the door. His only daughter, a twenty-five-year-old widow, would come yawning and open it.

* * *

Singaravelu Pillai had heard about Sarasa and had also seen her. To the landlord of Sandhaipettai, merely raising the subject of Sarasa was like taking a rejuvenating elixir. He was determined somehow to marry her. He had entertained this thought ever since his wife died of smallpox. He had Vedavalli as a kept woman, but Vedavalli had already turned forty.

He imagined that having a tiny young thing like Sarasa as his wife would bring a bliss unavailable even in Indra’s heaven. As was his custom, he told the priest about it.

“The girl is certainly very beautiful, but she is a wicked one. The moment she sees my head, she mocks me: ‘The priest has come, mother! Is it for a plantain or an okra?’ Her mother is deeply devout. On memorial-rite days she gives charity to her heart’s content and never neglects her religious observances. I have already spoken to her mother. Your horoscope is very favourable now. Before long, a new bride, a new cow, or a new dhoti is destined to come to you of its own accord,” the priest would tell Singaravelu in honeyed tones.

“Swami! I have never forgotten that Ambika in all these fifty years. Not once have I failed to sponsor a consecration ceremony. We shall see how Ambika bestows her grace,” he would reply.

“I have been thinking of arranging a hundred-thousand-name offering in your name tomorrow,” the priest would say.

Singaravelu Pillai would call his accountant and say, “Give the Iyer whatever he needs,” then resume researching which oil would best darken his thoroughly grey hair.

If he could somehow bind these two families together, the Sastri thought, his own life would become a celebration. Was he not the agent of both families?

One evening, the Sastri was flattering Sarasa’s mother. As he poured out horoscope predictions in a torrent, the wicked Sarasa arrived.

“Why did your astrological calculations fail to reveal that Kamala would lose her marriage pendant in the second month after her wedding?” she struck at him.

With a sickly smile, the Sastri said, “Fate, child. It was the Lord’s decree.”

“When you could not discover the calamity awaiting your own daughter, you have come to read and announce the fruits of my horoscope?” the maiden asked sarcastically.

“Well, what happened has happened. My daughter Kamalam now passes her days somehow, praising the Lord and living quietly. That is all her fate has ruined,” the Sastri said with some sadness.

“Poor Kamala! Thinking of her makes grief choke my throat. What a nature the girl has! What beauty! The child is as fresh as a lemon,” said Sarasa’s mother.

With a slight smile, Sarasa said, “Go on, describe Kamala a little more. Is this all that she was allowed to find in life?”

“Go away, you wicked brat! Look how faithfully Kamala observes every ritual and rule. Has she ever passed a day without going to the temple and worshipping? You go about saying, ‘I am going to this meeting; I am going to that settlement,’ and bring disgrace upon the whole community,” her mother said.

“Kamala is my friend! I care more about her affairs than you do. Tomorrow at three in the afternoon I am going to show you a spectacle, Sastri. Even if you sit beneath the tall brass lamp all night turning over every bundle of your almanacs, you cannot discover what I am going to show you tomorrow.

“Kamala is beautiful. Kamala is a young woman. Kamala is a widow. She is my dear friend. You have not understood her fully; I have. Go now and return tomorrow. You will witness a sight that first bewilders your mind and then cools it.”

After saying this, Sarasa left for the Women’s Progress Association.

“Look here! You must remain absolutely silent in this room. I am going to lock it from outside,” Sarasa said the following evening, locking the Sastri in a room.

The Sastri was rather afraid of what trick this “wicked girl” might play. After a while, he heard two young women laughing happily in the next room. He listened intently. Yes, there was no doubt: it was his daughter Kamala’s laughter. Never before had he heard Kamala laugh in that way. What laughter or joy was permitted to a widow?

“Dear Sarasa, today you have made me fortunate. I shall never forget you,” said Kamalam.

“You may even forget me, Kamala! But could you forget *Ethukula Kambhoji*?” Sarasa asked.

“Go on, Sarasa, teasing is always your occupation. Who could forget after hearing my husband perform the *Ethukula Kambhoji* alapana once?” Kamala replied.

“Tell him to record it on a disc. Even when he has gone out, you can put it on and keep listening,” said Sarasa.

The Sastri in the adjoining room understood none of this. He knew the influence of stars, calculations of auspicious time, and the glory of darbha grass, but what did he know of *Ethukula Kambhoji*?

“What accursed thing is this Ethukula Kambhoji? The girls are talking about ‘my husband’ and a gramophone record and all manner of things,” he thought in bewilderment.

“Sarasa, will my story appear in all the newspapers?” Kamalam asked.

“Without fail! It will appear in bold type with a photograph: ‘A reform marriage between Mrs Kamalam, daughter of the renowned Sastri, and Subramania Pillai, the nadaswaram artiste of the Siva temple, was happily conducted through the efforts of Mrs Sarasa. District Munsif Comrade Kathiresan presided.’”

Before Sarasa could finish, the Sastri wailed loudly: “You sinner! You have destroyed my household! What shall I do? Kamala, what have you done?”

“It is nothing. Do not be afraid, Kamala! Your father has seen reform and is crying out in fear, that is all. He will recover after a little while. Legally, he cannot do anything to you,” Sarasa said.

In the adjoining room the Sastri kicked the door with loud crashes. “Destroyer, open the door! Have you married a nadaswaram player? Was this why you went to the Siva temple every day? Ruined woman! Could you not have fallen into some pond or pool and died? You have brought a burning brand into my family!” he howled.

“Sastri, calm yourself. Everything is the Lord’s doing,” said Sarasa.

“Enough of your talk! You are a very good woman indeed. You had to be born in this village to destroy my household,” the Sastri said.

“Sastri, what wrong do you find in what your daughter has done? You stand here spoiling for a fight because she married a man of another caste. What of your own conduct? Do you imagine that because Kamala does not know it, neither do I? Our association’s investigator has your entire ‘horoscope.’ Your speech is all Vedas and Vedanta; your costume is orthodoxy. But what of your conduct? Sastri, who is your kept woman Valli, and to what community does she belong? Shall I tell Kamala?” Sarasa demanded angrily.

“Siva! Siva! Sambho Mahadeva! What offence have I committed against you that a thunderbolt should fall upon my head like this?” the Sastri cried.

“Very well, listen to some more news. Kamala and Subramaniam are leaving for Madras tonight. They will live there. I too am going there next week,” said Sarasa.

“Sarasu, what is this enormous trouble you have stirred up?” Sarasa’s mother said as she let the Sastri out of the room. Without even looking back at Kamala, he went home.

That very night, Kamala and her beloved husband Subramaniam left for Madras.

Sarasa looked at her mother with a faint smile. “Mother, when I possess the ability to arrange a widow’s marriage, how could you plan to give me to an old man?” she teased.

Then she showed her a photograph. “Look at this picture, mother! This is the man I am going to marry next week.”

Sarasa’s mother looked at it. The picture was of a charming young man, fashionably dressed and handsome.

“Who is this, child? What caste is he?” her mother asked.

“This is the man who is going to become my husband, mother. His caste is the male caste. That is all I know,” Sarasa said, affectionately pinching her mother’s cheek.

“I do not like anything you do. You are ruining caste and lineage and everything else,” said her mother.

“Whatever I may ruin, your wish is that I should be happy, is it not, mother?” Sarasa coaxed.

Her mother could give no reply.

“Go away, you wicked madcap! However you do it, live happily. I shall look upon it and rejoice,” her mother said.

1939, *Kudi Arasu*

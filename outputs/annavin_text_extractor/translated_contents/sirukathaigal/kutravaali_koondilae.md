## English Translation

He was a father. The four children who had been killed were his own, and they had been killed by him.

Everyone would call this an atrocity.

A father killed the treasures born to him, the children he had raised, rejoicing when they smiled and grieving when they withered.

What a strange thing, you may say. It is not strange, but a painful true incident. It happened at Karuma Goundan Palayam in Erode taluk.

A father has the right to punish and correct children who do wrong. But this father did not kill because the children had committed cruelty or an offence. It was his affection for them and his unchanging love that drove him to murder and made him a killer.

The court pronounced the sentence: death.

"The death penalty is not enough for him. He must be tortured. That alone is the proper punishment for this cruel man!" say those who imagine that the responsibility for dispensing justice belongs to them.

"The sinner killed his own children! Is he a father? He is a monstrous demon!" say some.

But his heart spoke in this manner:

"Yes, I killed them! I am a demon, cruel and merciless. Call me whatever you wish. Abuse me. I do not care. Why should I care what is said by those who do not know my condition or understand its purity? I am indeed guilty and deserve punishment. The same love, the same affection, that inspired my joy when I heard I had become a father caused me to kill my children. Will the world accept this?

"It was by thinking of my responsibility and duty that I sought permanent peace for my children. I cut with a sickle the very children whom I had struggled so hard to protect. I then fastened a rope to a beam and tightened it around my neck. The society that now abuses me rescued me from that rope.

"When I told my plight to everyone I met, shed tears, pleaded, and asked for work, that society did not think, 'Poor man! He is the father of four children and bears the duty of protecting them. By giving him some work, let us save both him and his family.' Instead it thought, 'Let him go somehow. Let him be ruined. Why should we care?' But when it saw three corpses and one child barely alive, that same society began abusing me.

"When I showed my stomach, described my starving and withering children, and asked for work, society said, 'Get lost, you useless fellow! Go elsewhere. There is no work for you here.' When I said I had a wife, society mocked me: 'A wife indeed!' That same society rescued me from the noose and now comes forward to dispense justice."

His wife's overflowing tears, the children's pitiful cries, and a society that showed no compassion plunged him into anguish.

He had a wife and four children, the responsibility to protect them, and the despairing question of how that could possibly be done. All these increased the pain within him.

He searched for work and searched again. Everywhere, the answer was no.

One day, exhausted from looking for work, he returned home. He thought and thought. He thought for three days.

His wife had gone to work in the fields. He saw that the children born to him had lost even the strength to say, "We are hungry." He could not bear to leave them in such torment. Nor could he simply run away somewhere or seek death for himself, because he loved his children. If he fled or died, what would become of them? He considered all these things.

He resolved to put an end to the suffering. He decided that his conclusion required no one's mercy or indulgence and firmly believed that the right was his. He bolted the street door and took up the sickle. One, two, three, four: he killed all four and then put the noose around his own neck.

Society, which had shown no compassion when he sought work and had done nothing to prevent a father from being crushed by poverty, saved him from the noose.

The government, which had not given him a life while he suffered, sentenced him to death.

Rangasamy committed the killings. Yes, that was his name. The court delivered its judgment: death.

The judge who pronounced the sentence stated that, as a father, Rangasamy lacked the capacity to bear the burden of caring for the children; that he had experienced considerable hardship in protecting them and had become frustrated; and that he had therefore committed these acts to slip away and escape from those burdens.

"He lacked the capacity to care for the children," the judge said. Why did he lack it? Had he not protected them for so many days?

If it was merely irritation, he could have run away somewhere. It was not irritation but a sense of duty that governed him.

The judgment said he acted to slip away and escape. Had escape been his aim, he could have abandoned them to suffer and fled alone. But he did not. His heart would not allow him to leave them in anguish. That was why he became a killer.

With the punishment imposed, the work of justice was considered finished. They stopped a man who had tried to die and declared, in effect, "We shall send you to death ourselves."

Those who imposed the sentence did not say who must accept responsibility for eliminating unemployment, poverty, and the poverty that turns even good people into murderers.

Had Rangasamy, who received the death sentence, occupied the judge's place, would he not have brought into the prisoner's dock the society that made him a killer and then accused and abused him after his crime, and the law that has power only to punish without reminding the rulers of their duty to protect him?

Would he not have asked the government, which could devise no way for him to protect his children and bore no responsibility for giving all people the means to live: "From where did you acquire the right merely to punish the people?"

29 March 1953

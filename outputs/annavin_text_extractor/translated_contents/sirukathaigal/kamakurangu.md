முகத்திலே களையும் இருக்கிறது. குதிரை சிலாக்கியமானதுதான்,
அனால் வண்டி மட்டும் ஏற்றதல்ல. ரப்பர் டயர் இருக்கிறது.
ஆனால் குதிரையின் போக்கைச் சமாளிக்கும் சக்தி இல்லை
வண்டிக்கு, வேறே வண்டியாக மட்டும் இருந்தால், ஜோர்தான்!

குதூகலமாக வாழ்பவர். மிராசுதார், அவருக்கென்ன
ஆனந்தத்துக்குக் குறைவா? அந்த ஊரே அவருடையது; ஆள் அம்பு
ஏராளம், வருவாய் அமோகம். வாட்டம் ஏது? வருத்தம் ஏது?

## English Translation

A first-rate horse! Its legs were flawless. It needed no whip. Its trot was majestic. At the sound of another carriage approaching, alarm flashed in its eyes, its legs quivered, and it sprang away like the wind. Its face had distinction. The horse was excellent; only the carriage did not suit it. The carriage had rubber tyres, but lacked the strength to withstand the horse's pace. With a different carriage the sight would have been splendid.

Mirashdar Meenakshi Sundarar had searched far and wide, sent men to many markets, and obtained that horse at great expense. The carriage was inherited property, and there was no need to sell it. He therefore harnessed the new steed to the old carriage. Those who praised the horse pointed out the carriage's crooked frame. Meenakshi Sundarar agreed and ordered another. Until the new carriage arrived from the workshop, he continued using the old one.

The Mirashdar habitually lived in delight. What reason had he to lack happiness? The village was practically his, servants and retainers were abundant, and his income immense. What hardship or grief could touch him? Life was a feast, and after the new horse arrived his joy tripled.

Could an animal that gave him such pleasure lack grooming, grass, or grain? It received wheat gruel and, at times, various strengthening foods; handsome ornaments; and a silk covering against the night mist. The horse lived merrily. It was no labourer's horse compelled to earn ten annas before the midnight train. It belonged to a Mirashdar who drank life's sweetness while multitudes toiled. There were no blows upon its back, sores from thorns, wounds near its eyes, or spikes in its bridle. It lived in grandeur.

Meenakshi Sundarar's daughter Madhuravalli shared her father's delight. When the horse came to live at the mansion, the young beauty would go to it surrounded by servants, stroke its back, embrace its face with soft hands, and kiss it. She said its eyes looked only at her, proposed tying a golden bell around its neck, and declared the ancient carriage unworthy of it.

The whole village said the same: the carriage was old, the horse new, and the vehicle unfit for the animal. Yet the rides continued. How could the horse say, "You admire and praise my excellence, and even say this carriage does not suit me. Why then do you still harness me to it? Is this right?"

The horse lacked the power of speech. Young women possess voices, but can they describe the deformity of the vehicles assigned to their lives and refuse them? Just as a horse is dragged forward and harnessed to a carriage, maidens too are bound. The journey of life proceeds. When accidents occur, the empty word "fate" always stands ready.

Mahibalar, the Mittadar of Magalipatti and a distant kinsman of Meenakshi Sundarar, decided Madhuravalli had been born to preserve old claims between the families and make their friendship close. At first his debt and Meenakshi Sundarar's revenue lands produced the thought. One day he saw Madhuravalli, and the thought hardened into resolve.

She had no intention of enchanting anyone; what need had the Mirashdar's only daughter to live by seducing others? Yet her form and features naturally captivated those who saw her. Does not the fragrance of a flower dancing upon its vine draw bees? Does not the forest stream become music to wild creatures, and do not the stars shining in the sky feast the eye? So did Madhuravalli appear to every beholder.

She was not merely a rich man's daughter but somewhat educated. Though she knew little, she had learnt enough to follow new fashions in clothing and ornaments, use new kinds of glittering forehead marks, and dress her hair in modern styles. Rank, beauty, and adornment formed a triple necklace; red lips, a moon-cool gaze, a slight smile, and a cuckoo's voice were its companion goods.

What wonder that waves rose in the Magalipatti man's mind? When his desire was first mentioned to Meenakshi Sundarar, the latter was occupied with the new horse and conventionally replied, "We shall see. Why not? We shall arrange it."

Mahibalar rejoiced. What could he lack now? His debt would be settled and the fruit would be his. But he was the old carriage, unfit for Madhuravalli.

The Magalipatti Mittadar energetically prepared himself as a bridegroom. Hair-darkening oils, rejuvenating tonics, silk upper garments, imitation-stone rings, a small wristwatch, and a cane bearing a golden lion's head made his bridegroom's appearance blossom. This was the second time he sought such a young delight. His first wife, unable to express even her fundamental objection, had hidden anger and sorrow until she withered and died. Poor woman. What did that matter? It was her "karma."

Mahalinga Iyer of Magalipatti was praised by his Brahmin circle as exceedingly clever and learned in Vedas and shastras. What could others know of that sacred language? They merely said, "Is that so? Saraswati dances upon his face."

It was he who came to fix the auspicious wedding hour. The Mirashdar received him with honours, asked after his welfare, and arranged a meal at the priest's house. Both families showed special respect to Brahmins. Having eaten his fill, the priest praised them as children of Lakshmi and seats of wisdom, then announced his mission. Only then did the Mirashdar realise that trouble had grown from his casual words.

"I said something in passing, but never thought the Magalipatti gentleman would insist. There is a great difference between his age and Madhuram's," he said.

The priest smiled. "If we consider age, should a marriage fitting the lineage never occur? Set age aside. The epic itself says Lady Sita was older than Sri Rama. The Magalipatti alliance is no trivial matter; the relationship is ancient."

Just then a servant ran in.

"Master! A great disaster! The carriage struck a tree and shattered. The horse too is badly injured. A bull came running in fright. The horse kicked up its legs, and we could not restrain it. The carriage was old, master, and when it struck the tree..."

While the servant related the accident, the Mirashdar hurried to the place. He suffered at the sight of carriage and horse. The animal seemed to argue with its gaze: "The fault is not mine. You failed to harness me to a strong, weighty carriage suited to me. The fault is yours."

Its leg was badly hurt. The veterinary doctor declared it would never again be fit for a proper ride. "My magnificent horse is lost," the Mirashdar lamented. Exhaustion left him unable to speak much. At that moment the Magalipatti priest poured out a stream of praise for the Mittadar's wealth and brought him around. A wedding date was fixed.

The marriage took place with magnificence that amazed the region. Guests enjoyed the feast, spectacle, and entertainment, pitied the injured horse, and went home. Could they discuss the twenty-year-old maiden being made wife to a fifty-year-old man? These were powerful people; why invite trouble? They closed their mouths.

They did not speak. Did Madhuravalli? Her heart did not blossom, but her eyes remained open while her mind slept. A Mirashdar's daughter marrying a Mittadar and a zamindar family's woman entering another landed household were ordinary events. Thus Madhuravalli entered Magalipatti without conscious turmoil. The Mittadar had made special arrangements for her reception.

Should splendour not suit their status? A procession advanced with celebrated nadaswaram music, and that night a *sadir* dance was held in a vast decorated pavilion. The assembly enjoyed it not from artistic feeling, but because the dancing women stirred desire with their eyes. The Magalipatti man made a remarkable sight. Beside him sat his private secretary Vedantham, a Brahmin dismissed from his post as manager of the Kaniyur estate. Vedantha Iyer constantly whispered in his ear. The Mittadar listened with pleasure, cleared his throat, twisted his moustache, and stared at the dancers. Others in the hall enjoyed looking at them whenever the Mittadar was not watching.

It was past midnight when the celebration ended. Madhuravalli's attendants prepared a beautiful bed and asked whether they should remain with their mistress. She said she needed no companion and would call if required, then lay down tired.

The grandeur of the festivities and the respect everyone showed the Mittadar had convinced Madhuravalli that she had obtained a place suitable for a great lady. Satisfaction danced upon her face as she slept.

At midnight a crash made her wake screaming. She raised the lamp. A woman stood there trembling violently, streaming sweat and tears. Hearing their mistress, servants rushed towards the room. The woman fell at Madhuravalli's feet.

"Save me! If they see me, they will kill me. Grant me my life!" she pleaded.

Madhuravalli pitied her. She immediately hid her beneath the bed and stood at the doorway.

"What is the noise?" she demanded of the approaching servants.

"We heard something here, as though someone ran inside. That is why we came."

"Have you no sense? Can you not tell where a sound comes from? Excellent servants indeed! Go and look near the garden," she ordered angrily.

When they had gone, Madhuravalli bolted the door and calmly said, "Come out."

The woman emerged from beneath the bed and bowed again.

"Why, it is you! You were one of the dancers."

"Yes, madam."

"And the other?"

"My elder sister - that is, my dancing companion."

"Then why did you run here at midnight?"

"Can you not understand?"

"Why should you not be a thief caught while trying to steal?"

"Madam, I am no thief and stole nothing."

"Do not argue. Because I saved you from those servants, do not imagine I shall believe you a goddess of virtue. Why did you run here in disorder at midnight?"

"When a woman comes trembling at such an hour and begs for refuge, can you not understand what it means?"

"Enough riddles. Why is a dancing girl wandering at midnight? Tell me the truth."

"I came here and danced. But your husband..."

"He what? What are you babbling? Speak quickly."

"He insisted that I spend the night with him."

"And so you, Goddess Savitri, declared you could not submit to such wickedness and ran here in terror? You seem a very crafty one. To whom are you telling this tale? If a Mittadar called, women like you would walk upon their heads to reach him. Yet you sing the refrain of chastity: he called, he forced, and you refused. Will you tell the truth, or shall I summon the servants and have you seized and punished? Decide within two minutes."

"You are a fitting companion for that lustful monkey. I lose nothing if you refuse to believe me. Your husband forced me to come with him, saying we would merely talk for amusement. Within a short while the lust-maddened old man resolved to assault me. I resisted, said I had a headache, told him it was improper. He would not release me. I had no other way, so I deceived him and fled. That is the truth."

"I ask you seriously: why did you refuse his wish? Who will believe you? He is a Mittadar and you a woman who lives by sale. Why should you refuse him?"

"Who could desire that old libertine? Women like you may consent to marry such an old man, but that is another matter. Can he become an object of love?"

"You dare call my husband old to my face! Do you know what I shall do to you?"

"I need not know. As I escaped that libertine, I can escape whatever you wish to inflict."

After this exchange the dancer sat boldly upon the bed.

"Come here. Why should we quarrel? You too are unfortunate. Do not be angry that I called your husband an old libertine. I spoke in anger, but not without reason. Do you know the Magalipatti Mittadar's age?" she asked more gently.

Madhuravalli marvelled that the trembling refugee had gained such courage. Perhaps it was a pretence.

"Your tricks will not work on me. Behave respectfully. He is old; why does that concern you?"

"Perhaps it does not concern you. But after seeing your youth and beauty, and knowing you are a Mirashdar's daughter, I began wondering how you accepted so ill-matched a marriage and what happiness you could find tied to him. I forgot my own trouble in worrying about yours."

She affectionately took Madhuravalli's hand, drew her to sit beside her, pointed at their figures in the large mirror, and embraced her.

"Do we not now look almost like sisters?"

Madhuravalli's amazement increased.

"You are an enchantress! It seems you can even change my anger," she said, playfully pinching the dancer's cheek.

"Your play now gives me great pleasure. But when I remember your husband's conduct, I feel disgust. When men grow old, obsession with women mounts to their heads, and monkey antics begin."

"I forgot even to ask your name. We play as though we had known each other from childhood," Madhuravalli laughed.

The woman truly was an extraordinary magician. Somehow she had enchanted Madhuravalli, pinching her cheek, combing her hair with her fingers, stroking her brow, embracing her, even kissing her. Madhuravalli thought her mischievous from childhood. In a very short time, affection greater than her former anger had arisen. The beautiful figure sitting, playing, and talking beside her exerted a magnetic force.

"You did not ask my name, and I did not tell you. I have a thousand names. Which shall I give?"

"Go away, little mischief-maker! I ask your name and you joke. What is it?" the Mittadar's wife coaxed.

"You refuse to believe me when I tell the truth. Truly, I have not one or two names but many. People call me by a different name on each occasion."

"I shall have to punish you before you come to the point. I will summon the servants and say this thief entered my room to steal. Then see what amusement awaits you," Madhuravalli threatened playfully.

"Call them and tell them about me; I shall tell them something too," the adventurer challenged.

"The old tale? That the Mittadar dragged you to his bedroom and you, a chaste woman, fled? Tell it freely. The Mittadar will not fear, nor will others lower their regard for him."

"Why tell that? I shall tell them something about you."

"What is there to tell about me?"

"A great deal. And even if there were nothing, I could invent an accusation."

"Can you accuse me? What charge could you make?"

"No, no. Play can turn serious."

"Tell me, rogue. You will not lose your head."

"So brave because you are the Mittadar's wife! But you would tremble if I accused you."

"Let us see your cleverness. I shall neither tremble nor cry. Am I a child?"

"Not merely a child, but a foolish child, an immature woman! Yours is a useless life, a flower without fragrance. Had you understood life, would you have consented to marry an old man?"

"When I ask your name, you threaten accusation; when I ask what accusation, you talk of old and young. You change the subject to deceive me. Tell me your name."

"How many times must I say it? I have many names. Call me Savitri, Anasuya, Vasantasena, Sita, Alli, Pavalakodi, or Rukmini. Today I am Malarkodi; I do not know what name I shall receive tomorrow."

"None suits you. Call yourself Chatterbox."

After this, the two beauties decided to sleep. Madhuravalli fell asleep first. Malarkodi could not. Sitting upon the bed, she watched Madhuravalli, beautiful even in sleep, and sank into thought.

Before dawn Malarkodi woke her, took leave, and returned to her quarters. Only after she had gone did Madhuravalli think angrily of the Mittadar's lustful conduct. Malarkodi's words seemed to take form and dance before her: "That old man behaved like a lustful monkey! I came to dance; he tried to dishonour me."

What an unworthy man! Would he torment a woman hired to dance, against her will? By chance she had fled to Madhuravalli and complained. If she told someone else, would the town not laugh at his character? Age had grown, but wisdom had not. Let him come smiling and talking; she would speak to him then.

Meanwhile, Malarkodi's companion asked where she had spent the night.

"Why ask about that shame?"

"I thought you went towards the garden and slept. What happened?"

"What could happen to us? That old man sent for me."

"Which old man?"

"The old bridegroom."

"Mind your tongue! Do you mean the Mittadar?"

"Oh, does a Mittadar remain young however old he grows? I do not know that law. My eyes saw only an old body, not his estate."

"So he called you..."

"You imagine he said, 'Dear Malarkodi, your sister and you danced so wonderfully that I am delighted; ask any boon and I shall grant it.' Fool! He dragged me towards his bedchamber. I deceived the old monkey and ran."

"Where?"

"That is the amusing part. By chance I entered his wife's bedroom. Poor woman: young, beautiful, and good-natured."

"Then?"

"How could he enter that sanctuary? I stayed with her until dawn."

"Did she ask nothing?"

"Not one question - nine thousand! Before leaving I told her to behold her husband's character. Sister, even I, who live for money, find him a torment. That woman is like a lotus of pure gold, innocent and laughing freely. How can her heart consent to life with that old monkey?"

"You should have preached that philosophy too. You always take the first ceremonial betel leaf for useless work."

Madhuravalli imagined Malarkodi speaking with the other dancer about the Mittadar's lust and her own plight, both laughing together. The imagined scene raised her anger. She did not attend the daytime feast, and did not touch the food brought to her room.

The Mittadar did not boycott the feast. He washed away his disappointment at losing the midnight feast with drink; only Madhuravalli's absence troubled him slightly. Unwilling to remain in the mansion, he went hunting after ordering that the dancers must not be allowed to leave. Guards were posted at the quarters occupied by Malarkodi and Manikkam.

Vedanthachari searched for the Mittadar, learnt he had gone hunting, and waited rather than follow him into the forest. The Mittadar returned after dark with two quail.

After tossing all day in anger and sorrow, Madhuravalli formed a plan. She would ask Malarkodi not to tell anyone of her husband's conduct. Trusting that their new friendship would make her request effective, she wrote:

"Do not tell anyone what occurred last night. Please do not let his name be dishonoured. I ask this because of the friendship formed between us and trust you to preserve the secret for my sake. Next month I am going to my father's mansion. Please come and see me there. In one night you stole my very heart. - Madhuravalli."

She enclosed a gold chain and sent both through a maid.

As the maid delivered them, the Mittadar arrived in disarray with Vedantham. The servant panicked and stammered. The Mittadar seized and read the letter.

"Faithless woman! Sinner! I am betrayed!" he cried.

Everyone except Malarkodi trembled.

"Were you with Madhuravalli last night?" he demanded.

"Yes. I talked with your wife all night. She is very good," Malarkodi said with courage in her voice.

The Mittadar looked at Vedantham, who glared at Malarkodi.

"You thought no one would know your deceit. You fooled us," he roared.

"You were fooled; I did not fool you. This rogue must have told you," Malarkodi replied, looking towards Manikkam.

Angrily rubbing her eyes, Manikkam admitted telling the story and reproached Malarkodi for daring to spend the entire night in the Mittadar's wife's bedroom.

"Vedantham! My fury will not subside unless she is tied to the tamarind tree and beaten by the village watchman with a switch," the Mittadar raved.

"Go and bring Madhuravalli, Vedanthachari! The Mittadar himself has commanded you. Why stand still? The moment he spoke yesterday, did you not run to take me to the garden? Being tied to a tamarind tree and beaten is no great pain compared with Madhuravalli living with this old monkey," Malarkodi declared in a voice of anger and pride.

No man would dare speak thus before the Mittadar. The maid wondered where a dancing woman found such courage.

"All this disaster arose through you!" the Mittadar turned upon Vedantham.

"Do not be angry, Mittadar. You ordered me to bring the younger one to the garden, and I obeyed. Leaving you together, I came to pass the time talking to the elder. That stupid woman did not tell me at first; she spoke only much later. I panicked at once and grieved over the offence and ill fortune. What could I do? Forgive me," Vedanthachari pleaded, almost falling at his feet.

The Mittadar collapsed upon a seat, struck his head, and handed him the letter.

"Read it! My honour is gone; my life trembles. That sinner writes, 'I lost my heart; do not forget; do not tell anyone; come next month.' What further proof is needed? What a life I lived, and what thunderbolt has struck me! Rama!"

Vedanthachari advised him to remain calm. They had the letter; they could drive the dancers away and prevent the matter becoming public. There was no need for anguish. The Mittadar's sigh showed the advice useless.

"Stay here. I shall return," he said, running with the letter to Madhuravalli's room.

The door was bolted. He hammered. Madhuravalli opened it and saw him furious. Her own restrained anger burst its banks like a flood, and she tried to close the door.

"Faithless woman! Do not display your tricks here!" he threatened, forcing it back.

"You are a Mittadar and know how to threaten. I am a Mirashdar's daughter and am weary of seeing such threats. Do not display your heroics to me," Madhuravalli answered firmly, sparks flying from her words.

He seemed struck by electricity. Anger yielded to fear. She was no ordinary woman: neither frightened nor flustered, but ready to accept whatever happened. His dread increased.

"Did you write this?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

She slammed the door so rapidly that it struck him and sent him falling outside, then bolted it. She did not wipe the tears beginning to fall.

At that very hour her father Meenakshi Sundarar, who had gone away before the festivities ended in search of a familiar diversion, arrived at the estate. The mansion was in uproar. A quarrel between the dancer and the Mittadar would have been intelligible; so would a quarrel between Madhuravalli and her husband. But people said the quarrel involved all three. Unable to understand, the Mirashdar demanded an explanation. Hearing her father had come, Madhuravalli left her room.

"Father, I cannot remain here any longer."

"No fool will consent to keep you here after such disgrace," the Mittadar thundered.

"Control your tongue. I am not an angry man, but when anger comes, I cease to be human," the Mirashdar warned, naturally taking his daughter's side.

"The town will laugh when it learns your daughter's character. Read this letter," said the Mittadar.

The Mirashdar read, started, and asked his daughter, "Did you write such a thing? To whom?"

At that moment Vedanthachari dragged Malarkodi into the arena.

"To her," Madhuravalli calmly replied.

"The dancer?"

"Yes, Father. At midnight this worthy man invited her from dancing to dalliance. She ran to my room."

The Mirashdar glared at his son-in-law instead of asking whether that was honourable.

"She is precious. She joked with me all night. I sent the letter because I feared that if she told others of his character, his honour would be lost," Madhuravalli explained.

The Mirashdar's second glare said: "My daughter tried to preserve your honour, fool, and you rage at her?"

"What did you talk about all night?" the Mittadar asked Malarkodi.

"Many things: you, the world, and the suffering of a maiden married to an old man. Mittadar, last night was my night of delight, and her only joyous night as well. Neither of us will receive such a night again," Malarkodi said, sorrow filling her voice.

The tone sounded strange and new to Madhuravalli.

"Why make so much of this?" the Mirashdar asked the Mittadar.

The Mittadar looked fearfully from Vedantham to Malarkodi but offered no explanation.

"You are this old, and I gave you my one parrot-like daughter. You summoned a dancer for dalliance. She disliked your old man's antics, and my daughter consoled her. For that you leap upon my child like a dog. Why do you need a wife at all?" the Mirashdar shouted until the mansion shook.

He called his retainers, and two carriages were prepared.

Vedantham and the Mittadar whispered.

"How can we explain?"

"If we do, we disgrace ourselves."

"If we do not, the Mirashdar's anger will not subside."

"What will he think of us if we tell him?"

"He is taking the woman away in anger. What if he never sends her back?"

"Shall we remain silent now and trust him to return her when his anger cools?"

"What if he learns the matter through that fellow?"

While they held this council, the Mirashdar prepared to depart. Madhuravalli sat in one carriage and invited Malarkodi to accompany her.

The Mittadar panicked and ran to the Mirashdar.

"I shall explain later. Please! I fall at your feet. Take your daughter if you wish, but do not take Malarkodi. Listen to me in this one matter," he begged.

"You are mad. I dislike every bit of your speech, look, and conduct. If Malarkodi agrees to remain, keep her like a king," the Mirashdar replied, growing angrier at what seemed the Mittadar's continued obsession even after public exposure.

Malarkodi could not refuse Madhuravalli, who embraced her and insisted she would leave only if Malarkodi came. The carriage began moving. The Mittadar threatened to lie beneath its wheels and die, and seemed ready to do it.

"What affliction is this? Why torment us? Do you think I am taking Malarkodi away to perform lustful antics like yours? Leave the wheel. She will stay with my daughter for a day or two," the Mirashdar soothed.

"That is precisely what must not happen. Do not reject my plea. With your daughter Madhuravalli, him..." the Mittadar cried.

"Him? Whom?"

Convinced his son-in-law had truly gone mad, the Mirashdar watched him point speechlessly at Malarkodi.

"You mean this person?"

"Yes."

"This one?"

"Yes. Malarkodi is not a woman!"

Vedanthachari took the Mirashdar aside.

"Do not be angry. Malarkodi is not a woman but a man who plays female roles in the Manmohandas Company. His disguise, speech, and acting are exactly like a woman's. He and his beloved Manikkam danced yesterday. Her name is Manikkam; his is Seethapathi. I did not know yesterday, nor did the Mittadar, your daughter, or anyone watching the dance. Everyone believed him Manikkam's younger sister.

"The Mittadar desired him under that belief and summoned him to the garden. When the Mittadar revealed his intention, Seethapathi became frightened, fled, and entered Madhuravalli's room. She too believed him a woman and sheltered him. He remained there all night. Manikkam later told me he was not a woman, and then the Mittadar learnt it. Discovering that a man had spent the night in Madhuravalli's chamber enraged him; her letter arrived at the same moment, like oil upon fire."

The Mirashdar was astounded. Meanwhile Seethapathi gently explained the truth to Madhuravalli. She stared at him. What perfect acting! Malarkodi was a man. She had played with him all night; he had embraced her, pinched her cheek, even kissed her. The adventurous rogue!

Anger rose, but amazement drove it away; pity displaced amazement; and love scattered pity.

"The fault is not mine. Circumstances became so. By now the Mittadar will have told your father. After you leave, they will strip away my disguise and have their men beat me until their anger subsides. Madhuravalli, my life itself is strange. By night I am a queen; by day a poor labourer. At night I have mansions, upper chambers, maids, and kings kneeling before me. At dawn I am again a worker. Stranger than all of it, last night was a night of happiness. Now thunderbolt after thunderbolt awaits me; perhaps even death. Do not think I deliberately deceived you. Forgive me. I am innocent," Seethapathi said with feeling.

"You - innocent?" Madhuravalli asked, not angrily, but with a smile and a new look.

Having received the explanation, the Mirashdar came to the carriage.

"Get down," he sternly ordered Seethapathi. Then he told his daughter, "Malarkodi will remain here. I shall explain later."

"I know already - only now. But Father, do not leave her here. They will kill her," Madhuravalli said.

The Mirashdar took Malarkodi into his own carriage. Four guards with torches went ahead, and the carriages departed. The Mittadar stood stunned, staring after them.

Seethapathi became famous as principal actor of the Madhura Manoranjitha Sabha. The town gossiped: "Do you know? Seethapathi received his blessing through Mother Madhuravalli's favour." That is, they alleged a relationship between the actor and the Mirashdar's daughter. Who knows the truth in that gossip?

Madhuravalli refused to return to the Mittadar's mansion for the rest of her life. Yet she never failed to attend the Madhura Manoranjitha Sabha's plays. Whenever the company staged *The Lustful Monkey* in any town, Madhuravalli could be seen there.

Rumour said the Magalipatti Mittadar tried repeatedly and unsuccessfully to stop that play. It became the company's finest production, its diamond-jubilee drama, and received immense support.

*23 January 1945*

## Translator's Notes

Six pages previously marked as having no OCR text were recovered from transparency-bearing PNG scans before translation. Both numbered scan sets form one continuous story and are retained in their original order.

“காமக் குரங்கு” is the dancers' recurring insult for the ageing Mittadar and later the title of Seethapathi's successful play. It is translated consistently as “The Lustful Monkey.”

The story initially presents Malarkodi with feminine grammar because every character, including Madhuravalli and the reader, is meant to understand the performer as a woman. The English translation preserves that staged perspective until the source reveals Malarkodi as Seethapathi, a male actor specializing in female roles.

The source uses period social and performance vocabulary including மிராசுதார், மிட்டாதார், சதிர், and ஸ்திரீபார்ட். Their meanings are rendered contextually without silently modernizing the retained Tamil OCR source.

Hearing the hand drum, anklets, and moving song, devotees gathered at the shrine of Ulagammai, whose sacred temple stood at the edge of the village.

No worship of this kind had been held for a long time. The priest had grown rusty. Determined to display all his skill that day and transport both the villagers and visitors from other places, he had spent four or five days before the ceremony memorizing songs, testing the anklets, and practising his dance of bliss.

Ulagammai's temple was specially decorated. Villagers who had made vows offered many kinds of food and performed worship.

There was an enormous crowd of women. When Pechiyammal arrived, even the priest beating the hand drum stopped for a moment to look at her. Every other woman gazed at her in wonder. Those who knew her spoke, and even strangers moved closer to inspect her. At Pechiyammal's arrival, they forgot the worship and even Ulagammai herself.

Pechiyammal was a queen among women who had earned great fame by saving her husband's life through her courage. Many wondered where she had found such bravery. Some said it was the power of her auspicious marriage pendant. Others explained that her sickle was very sharp and could sever a head with one stroke.

"Pechi, explain what happened just once," Muthammai asked.

"I told Muniyamma the whole story only yesterday. How many times must I repeat it? Even my mouth hurts," Pechi replied. But did they let her go? They pressed her until she spoke.

A regal-looking woman who had been staring as though she wished to swallow Pechiyammal came and sat nearby.

"Who are you, madam? You are not from this village," Pechi asked.

"No. I am from elsewhere," the newcomer replied sorrowfully.

"Whose house are you visiting here?" Pechi asked.

"I came only to see Ulagamma's worship," she replied, then added, "and to hear your story before I leave."

Thus she encouraged Pechiyammal to speak. "Let the worship continue. Come, we will go toward the bank of the tank and talk," Pechi said, leading the new woman away.

She seems to be a woman from an important household, Pechi thought. Her beautiful complexion, walk, and dress show that she lives in comfort. Fortunate woman! If she were like us, drinking boiled gruel made from coarse millet and going to work in the fields, how could her body possess such shine and lustre? She must be the lady of a great estate.

Thinking so, Pechi behaved respectfully.

"What is your name, madam?" she asked.

"Parameswari. They also call me Kanni," the newcomer replied sadly.

"I did nothing extraordinary, Kanni. Your name is Kanniyamma, isn't it? My husband and I had gone to a relative's house for a celebration. While we were returning, some wicked man, apparently named Shanmugam, must have sniffed us out and followed. We did not know.

"My husband had some money tucked into his waist. That wicked fellow had scented it. We were walking along talking about village affairs when the man ran toward us shouting, 'Who is that? Hey! Stop! I will cut off your head!'

"'Pechi, danger has come,' my husband said while thinking how to overpower him. The wicked man swung a staff and struck him on the head. 'Ayyo, Pechi!' my husband cried. My insides trembled. Blood gushed out.

"The wicked fellow began snatching the money from my husband's waist. I saw it. I thought, 'What value has our life now? Let him be a great tiger. Before we lose our lives, I must do whatever I can to destroy him.'

"I tell it calmly now, madam. At the time, my heart pounded. I only screamed, 'Ayyo! Sinner! Murderer!' If a man splits my husband's head before my very eyes, how much fury will rise within me? I became Bhadrakali.

"I gave such a scream, Kanniyamma. When I remember it now, even I am frightened. I took up the sickle and struck at the sinner's neck. He screamed, 'Ayyo!' His head did not fall off, but the man fell down. Was it an ordinary blow? At that moment I was filled with heroic fury.

"A man who plans evil meets evil. The fool thought, 'She is only a woman.' But Kanniyamma, I do not know where I found such courage. They say the wicked fellow is lying in hospital.

"My husband embraces me and calls me his darling, the apple of his eye, and his lion cub. He is overflowing with happiness. I rejoice that my marriage pendant was saved. The entire village has gathered to honour me.

"Everyone says, 'Look at Pechi's courage! If one is a woman, this is how she should be. She is a blessed woman who saved her husband's life.' I feel terribly shy. Yet if I had not lifted the sickle and struck at that moment, the sinner would have killed my husband. By Ulagatha's grace, I found courage and my turmeric and vermilion endured. That, madam, is my story."

"Did you feel no fear, Pechi?"

"How could I not? But what else could I do? Can one surrender to fear at a moment of danger?"

"I was ruined because I remained afraid."

"Did some danger befall you too, Kanniyamma? Why are you wiping your eyes? What happened? Tell me."

"I am ashamed to tell you, Pechi. Even one-thousandth of your courage would have been enough. I did not possess it. That wicked fellow..."

"Who? What did he do?"

"He entered my house, broke open the chest, and stole all my property. I had a crown made of gold for my head. He took it. Cash and jewels, everything is gone."

"Who was in the house?"

"Only I."

"Did you not know the thief had entered? Why did you not scream and wake the whole village?"

"I could not scream, Pechi. I could not."

"Why? Were you afraid the sinner would kill you if you shouted?"

"You foolish girl, Pechiyammal! You speak this way because you do not know who I am. There are police to prevent theft, murder, assault, and fighting in a village. If a rogue struck the police chief himself and he screamed for the townspeople to help, what would become of his dignity?

"You cannot understand my position. I am foremost in that town. Whenever anyone encounters suffering or danger, they come and tell me. They live believing that my grace alone is enough to obtain everything. Does not your village have Ulagatha? Think of all the worship and influence she possesses. I am like that. I am Ulagatha to my town."

"Your speech is strange, madam. When the thief came, were you asleep?"

"No. When the whole world slept, I, who remain awake protecting the people, was watching as the theft occurred."

"Was there no stick nearby? You could have picked it up and thrown it."

"There was even a trident."

"A trident? You could have thrust it into his chest."

"I could have. But am I Pechiyammal?"

"What does it matter which woman you are? He steals our property in our house. You are awake, and you say there is a trident. Why did you remain still? What sort of fear is that?"

"The townspeople praise me as Parameswari, the one who dispels every fear."

"That is still more amusing. You remain frightened, unable to catch a man who enters and steals your property, yet they praise you! What a curious town. What is its name?"

"Athoor. What is the name of this village?"

"This village is called Padmanabhamangalam. Leave the village name aside. Tell me why you remained like a stone."

"I am indeed stone."

"Your story grows more amusing. What happened afterward?"

"The police are investigating clues."

"The police spoke very highly of my courage."

"Why only the police? Even I must praise your courage, Pechiyammal. The people should honour and praise brave women like you. The villagers should know the stories of women like you.

"Instead, celebrating people like me who cannot even catch a thief, performing worship, singing praises, presenting offerings, burning camphor, and chanting, 'Mother! Compassionate one! Parameswari! Sovereign of all worlds!' is not worth even its salt."

"I do not understand anything you say. Do they perform worship for you?"

"Yes, for me."

"What madness is this?"

"Madness that has continued for a very long time, Pechiyammal. You possessed the courage to save your husband's life. I lack even the bravery to catch a thief. But do you know who I am?"

"Who?"

"Pechi, I am the deity called Kannika Parameswari."

"What? A deity?"

"Yes. A deity indeed."

When this conversation ended, the regal woman speaking with Pechiyammal vanished from sight.

"What is this, Veera? Have you begun unloading your sack of lies even before me?" Bharatha asked.

"No, Bharatha. It is not entirely false," Veeran replied.

"Do I have matted locks or even sacred ash, that you expect me, and this country, to believe whatever lie you tell?"

"Bharatha, it is imagination, but truth is mixed into it," Veeran said.

"What truth has entered the absurdity you tell?" I asked sarcastically. With confidence, he handed me a daily newspaper and pointed to two marked items. These are the reports I saw:

## First Report

> Mundak Kudumban and his wife Pechiyammal, of Senthilan Farm settlement in Padmanabhamangalam, had visited a relative in Pottaloorani village and were returning to their own village on the seventh.
>
> On the way, a man named Shanmuga Velalar reportedly appeared suddenly, struck Mundak Kudumban, and attempted to seize the money at his waist. Pechiyammal then struck Shanmuga Velalar on the back of the neck with the sickle in her hand and knocked him down.
>
> Shanmuga Velalar is reported to be in hospital.

## Second Report

> Some thieves reportedly cut the chains and broke the locks on the sanctum door of the Sri Kannika Parameswari Amman Temple at Athoor, entered, and stole the goddess's crown.
>
> The thieves are also said to have opened the lock of the adjoining room with a duplicate key, stolen jewels and cash, and escaped through the rear of the temple.

What more could I do? How could I fail to give you this truth-infused fiction that Veeran gave me? That is why I have presented it.

## English Translation

I was astonished to see Justice Janaki driving his car rapidly through China Bazaar. I wondered what matter could be so important that he had to hurry.

Oh, what are you thinking? You wonder why, after saying "Janaki," I refer to him as a man. It was not a mistake. Janaki is not a woman. It is short for Janakiraman, and he is indeed a man.

"Whatever happens, sir, I shall remain in the Justice Party. Whether I prosper or decline," Janaki would say. His friends therefore named him Justice Janaki. Even the newspapers referred to him that way.

Justice Janaki's dearest friend was Jamadagni Aiyar. So what if he was Jamadagni Aiyar? He possessed some concern for Janaki's affairs, and so the friendship existed.

The town carried many rumours. One can close the mouth of a furnace, but who can close the town's mouth? Let us merely say that the two had been childhood friends. Why should we stir up the reasons? Who knows which snake occupies which anthill?

Let us attend to the cause of Justice Janaki's haste that day.

The motorcar stopped at a perfume shop. The shopkeeper received him respectfully.

"Has he not come yet?"

"Whom are you asking about?"

"Him? He came and left, sir."

"Very well. I need good javvadu and attar."

"You tell me to give you good goods? What kind of talk is that, sir? Those words wound my heart deeply. Have I ever given you bad merchandise? What bad goods exist in my shop?"

"I did not mean that. The attar and javvadu are needed for a special purpose, and they must be excellent. The man to whom I shall give them is an extremely fashionable person."

"Take these and give them to him. However fashionable he may be, watch him fall utterly silent."

With the parcel in hand and a smile playing upon his face, Justice Janaki entered the car and drove directly to a shop selling zari garlands. He bought a large garland. The car set off again and reached a bungalow, where Jamadagni was waiting.

"Why, Janaki! You took so long that I came straight to the bungalow. Well, did you buy everything I told you to?"

"Certainly. Look here."

"Splendid! You have conducted the business briskly. Had you done things this way earlier, you might have become a Rao Sahib by now."

"How are the goods?"

"Oh! If you yourself go to the shopping street, could the goods fail to be good? By the way, where are the tins of cigarettes?"

"Good heavens! I forgot them entirely."

"What a fine man you are! Cigarettes are life itself to that person. He will not enter the Secretariat without a cigarette tin in his hand. You forgot the most important thing."

"We can buy them on the way."

"Fine. Has the statement been typed?"

"How can the statement be prepared before you arrive? I shall call the typist now. You dictate, and he will type it. Our typist is very capable."

"He is our Narasimhachari's son, isn't he? A good boy. It is his misfortune that he works here as a typist. His brother-in-law is a high official in Assam."

"Srinivas! Vasan! Srinu!"

The typist entered, bowed to Jamadagni, and stood waiting. Jamadagni began composing the statement while Justice Janaki savoured the cinema section of the newspaper.

## Justice Janaki's Statement Praising the *Sunday Observer*

I was heartsick upon seeing reports of the recent provincial Justice Party conference held at Salem. How could non-Brahmins like me fail to be distressed at seeing a few Self-Respecters reduce a great party to rubble? Shall Naicker's crowd destroy the party we nurtured so dearly? How can we tolerate it?

I strongly condemn the Salem conference. At this moment, I commend the courageous editor of the *Sunday Observer* for girding himself to save the Justice Party.

Renaming the Justice Party the Dravidar Kazhagam is an act of treachery. We shall not accept it. Not one of us went to Salem. Does that not show that the conference was unrepresentative? Therefore, henceforth, let everyone support the efforts of the editor of the *Sunday Observer*.

Yours,

Justice Janaki  
(son-in-law of Diwan Bahadur Iruvengidam Pillai)

Justice Janaki signed the statement joyfully.

"A new kind of radiance can already be seen upon your face. Why should it not? Soon you will be Rao Sahib Janakiraman. Then there will be a banquet. Many people will praise all the services you have rendered to non-Brahmin society. You too will flay the Brahmin in your speech."

"Get away, Jamadagni! Are you the one mocking me? Attend to the business that must be done."

The motorcar set off once more towards Mount Road.

The two climbed to the upper floor of a newspaper office. Inside there was constant laughter and conversation at high volume. Their footsteps therefore went unheard. Amid the confused clamour, only a few sentences fell clearly upon Justice Janaki's ears.

One voice said, "I called Periyar the first leader of Dravidastan, and this fellow looked at me strangely. Does he know my plan? The moment I delivered the line, there was tremendous applause!"

Justice Janaki thought, "The crowd at Salem must have loved Periyar greatly. The *Sunday Observer* itself praised Periyar so highly at Salem. If it now gathers a party against him, who will join on the strength of this man?" He remained upon the stairs, thinking.

Another voice said, "We must move this office elsewhere. From now on, many more people will come and go."

"If we make Diwan Bahadur Devabhushanam vice-president, we can run the office from his bungalow," another voice replied happily.

Then came a litany against many whom Janaki regarded as Justice Party notables.

"Him? A complete idiot."

"He knows nothing about politics."

"He trembles merely on seeing the Governor."

"A thorough miser. He will not give any money."

"What spectacle is this?" Janaki wondered. "They perform such worship even for their own leaders. How can they run a party, especially against those Self-Respect fellows?"

Jamadagni opened his mouth to say something, but Janaki signalled him to remain silent.

"All that is fine. How is Mr Netto?"

The answer came: "You might as well ask how an onion is and how garlic is. Tell us what you are like. Apparently, whenever he returns from Kanchipuram, you go and recite to him everything that happens here."

"Who said that?" There was some anger in the question.

"Look here. Do not try to deceive us."

"That is not my habit."

"Is it my habit, then?"

"Who knows?"

A peacemaker intervened. "Enough, enough. Why indulge in needless talk among ourselves?"

Justice Janaki felt as though he might faint.

"Finally, whom are we to appoint as leader?" asked a practical man.

"What about Sir Shanmugam?" someone proposed.

"That man has already ruined the possibility. Do you know the injustice he committed? Fine, let his praise of Naicker pass; perhaps he spoke according to the mood of the gathering. But why did he call Sir Shanmugam a traitor? It must have reached Sir Shanmugam's ears. If we invite him now, will he come?"

"Let him stay away. Who needs him?"

"Bobbili too has been condemned."

"Yes."

"Who else is there? The Kumararaja will not do."

"Yes."

"What is this? You say yes to everything. Whom are we to make leader?"

"Only then can we make these rich fellows who desire titles and offices dance as we wish. The government will certainly give titles to someone or other. When a man receives one, our Pandi will go to him and declare, 'You obtained it only because of us.'"

"Pandi does not know how to lie."

Pandi laughed. "Everything is for your sake, man."

"Yes, now you say it is for me. Four months ago you joined the doctor and beat the drum for him. Before that you lay crying, 'I surrender at the sacred feet of the Kumararaja.'"

"This is always the Kanchipuram man's habit whenever he sees Pandi. He says the same thing every time."

"Everyone is like that, sir. Even this editor who speaks so much, did he not once trail behind Periyar crying, 'Periyar, Periyar'?"

"Pandi, there is a difference between my following him and your following him."

"That is precisely what I said we should avoid. I told you not to undercut a man that way. You do not know. In earlier days I worked together with Naicker. Ask him. He has always held me in great regard."

"He was fond of me too."

"Yes, man. He casts some kind of spell upon everyone."

"There is no spell. He does good work. At his age he travels from town to town night and day. Look at us. After the Salem conference ended, we gathered here and merely talked. In the meantime, see how many towns he has visited and how many meetings he has conducted. People are naturally affectionate towards a man who works like that."

"Yes, that is true. Not one of our men performs even one-thousandth of that work."

"They want first-class travel and a bungalow. They grow tired after speaking for half an hour or even fifteen minutes. They begin to stammer merely on seeing a crowd."

"That is our great weakness."

17 September 1944

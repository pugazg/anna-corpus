## English Translation

On the language question, 11 March 1958.

**Anna:** Mr Deputy Speaker, the debate concerning the language question has grown heated, as the hon. Anbazhagan from our side explained before this Legislative Assembly. I wish to consider this question from an entirely different angle. The decisions we make today on the language question…

If a decision were to bind only us, we might hastily make it out of enthusiasm, giving primary importance to each person's likes and dislikes. But the decisions we make can bind not only us but our descendants to a great extent. When assuming a great responsibility, we must not allow either the arrogance arising from our boundless enthusiasm or the political feeling that we possess the power to control everyone to prevail. Since what we do may affect later generations, we must approach this question with the utmost caution.

That, I think, is why those responsible for this Government see Chinnadurais at one extreme and Annadurais at the other. [Laughter.] The rulers who stand between the two extremes hesitate to accept, as the hon. Chinnadurai says, that Hindi is the language of the people. At the same time they hesitate to accept Annadurai's position: “Let English remain; Hindi is unnecessary.” They are trying to find some good path between the two. I appreciate and welcome this good intention. I say with pride that we see such an intention in no other State of the Indian subcontinent.

While we can say this with pride, they made a sincere effort to find a good way to settle the question amicably. With great concern, they wanted people from all parties to express their respective views in that effort, and each did so. But meanwhile, certain issues that arose in the country have compelled everyone to explain his views to the nation.

At the same time, Leader of the Opposition V. K. Ramasami Mudaliar suggested that a person might leave the Language Committee, attend another meeting, and speak differently there about the position between the two—especially someone like me. They repeatedly and earnestly invited me, and I went. Those who said that I did not contradict there what I had said in the Language Committee mentioned only one thing: that I said Article 344 should be amended.

Whoever invites whom, it is human nature to think that each side should yield something to the other. In matters where concessions can be made in that spirit, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam will yield and genuinely try to resolve the questions. But this question binds not only us; it also governs the lives of future generations. We must therefore examine certain fundamental truths even if they are bitter.

When we speak of this country with our State in mind, an old generation is passing and a new generation is rapidly growing, while we live in between. The old generation deeply believes in India, the Indian subcontinent, Indians and Hindi, and that, because India became independent, Hindi must be the national language. We belong to a generation that holds India to be, in truth, an imagined construct. If we succeed, a time will come when India is treated as a subcontinent composed of many States that will become separate nations.

The emotional fervour my friend Chinnadurai feels for India, Indians and Hindi will not prevail. If Chinnadurai succeeds, that fervour will become permanent. We stand between these two outcomes. We are a generation in a difficult position, pulled on one side by memories of the past and on the other by thoughts of the lives of future generations.

Therefore, the resolution we make here must not be based merely on what has come down from the past. It truly must not harm future generations. I humbly submit that everyone must therefore approach this question without becoming subject to personal likes and dislikes.

When I ask that we speak without such likes and dislikes, consider my friend Sardar Vedaratnam Pillai, who always speaks calmly, gently and with a conciliatory outlook. If even he can say, “Go to Malayalam country or Kannada country and see; you will not return alive,” it clearly shows how far this question has inflamed passions.

He said that he had heard that, although these people oppose Hindi, the wife of Nedunchezhian, a member of their party, serves on a Hindi Development Committee. In examining this question, passions have gone so far that people investigate what the wives of individual party leaders are doing. When it was said that the lady was on the Hindi Development Committee, I made inquiries—not merely because Thiru Vedaratnam Pillai had said it, but out of my own curiosity. I understand that she is not on any such committee. Her husband, who bears the considerable responsibility of knowing what she does, says so.

The reason matters have gone this far is truly the nature of the effort we have undertaken…

**The Hon. Thiru Subramaniam:** If a wife holds a different opinion, she has that freedom.

**Anna:** As the Minister says, there is nothing wrong in a wife holding a view different from the policy held by her husband. Had those who first raised the matter realised this, it would not have been discussed. But it is good to have a spouse who understands matters. What I want to point out is that passions have risen so high that, in approaching this question, people probe “from the palace to the women's inner apartments.”

The important stage reached today concerns the report prepared by the Madras State Government. Because a “general agreement” was desired, I took part in that committee on behalf of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and gave my assent to the report. But seeing it wrongly interpreted and misused has made it necessary for me to explain both my policy and my party's policy. The amendments submitted on behalf of our party are intended to strengthen this report, not weaken it.

Ordinarily, a problem may be resolved in three ways. The important underlying truths…

If this report is sent to the Central Government with the statement that the other opposition parties too have given their assent, Hindi zealots in the North will use it to introduce Hindi here. Since the report would appear to show that nobody in Tamil Nadu opposes Hindi, they would argue that Hindi may be introduced in Tamil Nadu.

That is why we are making provision to impress upon the Central Government that vast numbers of people in Tamil Nadu oppose Hindi, say Hindi must not become the official language, oppose the constitutional article declaring that Hindi should become the official language of the Central Government, and argue that the article must be amended. I would say that our doing so will help the rulers of this State argue before the Central Government against the imposition of Hindi.

Members of the Congress Party may say, “There are 150 of us; the weight we command is sufficient.” I do not disparage that weight. It is a principle of democracy that those in the opposition can grow, and others will consider the possibility of their growth. Therefore, if the amendment seeking a change to the Constitution of India is included so as to make clear the extent of anti-Hindi opinion here, the report may then be considered to have been sent unanimously from here. Though unanimously sent, it will make those in the North understand that each party's view has been included in it to an appreciable degree.

It was asked, “What is the North? What is the South?” One cannot answer by simply drawing a line on a map and saying that what lies above it is the North and what lies below it is the South. There is no such political doctrine. When we speak of the North, we have in mind the Central Government, which assumes full responsibility and authority over all States, and the States closely connected with that Central Government. It is in that sense that we speak of “the North.”

Rather than tell the Central Government that all of us have reached a compromise on the language question, we consider it more appropriate to make it understand that certain fundamental differences remain. We therefore strongly press this Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam amendment seeking a change to the Constitution of India.

Apart from that, I am very pleased that the rulers take a position capable of preventing the idea that Hindi alone can be the people's language from being allowed to grow in this country. Today they do not possess zealotry to that extent. I am very happy to see that our Cabinet, and particularly our Finance Minister Subramaniam, has an abiding attachment to Tamil. That is a special quality of the Kongu country. Kongu Tamil alone is Tamil without admixture from other languages—Tamil with little mixture of a northern language or any other language.

Just as this distinguished son of the Kongu country labours for Tamil, if the children who study in the future and generations to come are to be protected from danger, the amendment we propose must also be accepted. Without calculating whether 150 people can accept an amendment proposed by fifteen, and in accordance with the principle that “politics is not mere arithmetic but is a political philosophy” and the doctrine that opposition parties are capable of growing, I conclude my speech by requesting the Finance Minister and the ruling party to accept our amendment.

## Translator's Notes

The recovered opening and closing scans complete the beginning and end of this extract. Material remains absent between Images 4 and 5 after the phrase “a problem may be resolved in three ways”; that internal boundary is marked rather than reconstructed. The OCR reads “344-வது பிரிவு”; this is retained as Article 344 because it is the visible constitutional reference, though the surrounding debate may also concern other official-language provisions. “அரண்மனை முதல் அந்தப்புரம் வரை” is translated literally enough to preserve Anna's criticism of political inquiry intruding into leaders' domestic lives.

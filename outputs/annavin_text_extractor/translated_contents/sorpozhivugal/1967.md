“1967”
11-08-1957-ம் தேதி ஞாயிறு காலை 10 மணிக்கு அறிஞர்

களாகக் கொடுத்திருக்கிற தலைப்பு 1967” என்பது. நானாகத்
தேர்ந்தெடுத்துக் கொண்ட தலைப்பு அல்ல; நம்முடைய நண்பர்

தெடுத்தது என்றுகூட நான் கருதவில்லை, நம்முடைய மாநில
நிதியமைச்சர் அவர்கள் ஒரு பத்து ஆண்டுக்கு நீங்கள் எல்லாம்

நாட்டுப் பிரிவினையில் இந்தப் பத்து ஆண்டு 1957-லிருந்து

நடைபெறக்கூடும் என்பதைக் கேட்கத்தான் இந்தத் தலைப்பைக்
கொடுத்திருக்கக்கூடும் என்று கருதுகின்றேன்.

புரட்சி என்பது நமக்கெல்லாம் தெரியாமல் போனதற்குக்

கவனிக்கக்கூடிய பருவத்திலே இருந்தவர்கள் அல்ல. அதிலும்
குறிப்பாகத் தி. மு. கழகத்திலே இன்றையதினம் ஈடுபாடு

தாழ்த்தப்பட்டிருந்தாலும், அது மறுபடியும் தளைகளை

யையும் 1917-லே பெற்றது.

நண்பர் சென்னையிலே சட்டக் கல்லூரியிலே படித்துக்
கொண்டிருக்கிற மாணவராக இருந்து அதிலே ஈடுபாடு

இருந்தார்கள். அப்படித் தோற்றுவிக்கப்பட்ட அந்த இயக்கம்

ஆரம்பிக்கப்பட்டாலும் அதனுடைய அடிப்படையிலே மூன்று

சொல்லுவார்கள்.

ஏன் அவர் அந்த அளவுக்கு பொருளற்ற முறையில் சொல்ல

## English Translation

On Sunday, 11 August 1957, at ten in the morning, Arignar Anna delivered this special address in Madurai.

The subject our friends have given me to speak on here is “1967.” It is not a subject I chose for myself; our friends chose it. Indeed, I do not even think they chose it entirely on their own. I believe they selected it with our State Finance Minister's statement in the Legislative Assembly in mind: that all of you should remain “quiet” for ten years. If ten years are added to 1957 in connection with the partition of the country, we arrive at 1967. I think they may have given me this subject in order to ask what could happen during these ten years.

Our chairman told you that, as soon as the words 1957 and 1967 fell upon his ears, his thoughts took wing and flew back to 1917. In truth, the first stage of a great revolution was launched in Tamil Nadu in 1917. Many of us did not recognise it as a great revolution because, in 1917, many among us were not yet old enough to take an interest in political affairs. More particularly, many of those actively involved in the DMK today would have been infants sleeping in their cradles in those days. Yet, just as we now witness many kinds of awakening among us, and just as young comrades everywhere now enthusiastically dedicate themselves to an ideal, a great awakening of the mind arose in Tamil Nadu in 1917.

**Editorial footnote:** In 1957 Anna declared that the DMK would capture power in 1967. The DMK came to power after ten years of planned work.

There were many reasons why that awakening did not continue uninterrupted. We have still not properly assessed the circumstances under which that revolution was later suppressed.

As our chairman observed, it was in 1917 that Tamil Nadu truly began to become conscious of itself. It came to understand that it was a distinct people, that its people had been degraded, and that, despite this degradation, they could break their fetters and advance once again. It acquired this self-confidence in 1917.

Just as many of us became involved in the Dravidian movement while studying in colleges and high schools, our friend who presides over this gathering today became involved in it in 1917 while he was a student at the Law College in Madras. Countless others throughout Tamil Nadu similarly supported the Non-Brahmin movement. Although the organisation thus founded began under the general name “Non-Brahmin movement,” three ideas were already present within it in embryonic form. It was said that its leaders spoke openly and emphatically about the need for Non-Brahmins to advance. But even then they had examined the fields in which Non-Brahmins had to advance, the reasons why they had failed to advance in each of those fields, and, after identifying those reasons, the means by which every deficiency could be removed and remedied so that Non-Brahmin society might progress once again.

**Editorial footnote:** On 11 November 1916, Dravidian leaders gathered at the Madras home of the lawyer Ethiraj Mudaliar and founded the Justice Party. Theagaraya Chetty was its secretary. The Justice Party itself was called the Non-Brahmin movement.

At that time they did not forget the political sphere. They did not neglect the economic sphere. Nor were they indifferent to the social sphere. Dr. Nair was then a great father of revolution who gathered all these ideas into a single form. Yet, just as we in Tamil Nadu have forgotten our own kings, we have failed to understand Dr. Nair properly today. When we turn the pages of our history books to discover the son of a king, we find “Varaguna Pandyan's son was the second Varaguna Pandyan,” followed by a footnote saying that some dispute even this. In the same way, we have preserved a confused history of Dr. Nair.

I believe there are two reasons for this. First, political events even more tumultuous than those of 1917 occurred in the intervening years. Second, in keeping with a trait of Tamil culture, we long ago lost the habit of recording our own worth and greatness. If you wish to investigate the history of Rome today, historical records exist for everything from the size of Julius Caesar's sandals to the earrings worn by Cleopatra. But if you examine the history of Tamil Nadu and ask whose son a king who ruled this region was, even if two or three historians, four or five Tamil scholars, and ten or fifteen months were brought together, each person could supply only a different account; they could not all offer a common conclusion.

**Editorial footnote:** In 1916, Congress fielded T. Madhava Nair in an election from the Madras Legislative Council to the Parliament in Delhi. But T. N. Sharma and V. S. Srinivasa Sastri won.

This does not mean that our scholars lacked learning. Our ancestors failed to record human deeds because of the mistaken belief that it was demeaning to do so. The movement begun in 1917 spread throughout South India like a great forest fire, as its enemies described it, and made even the teachers of political philosophy reclining in armchairs in England open their eyes wide. It is therefore no surprise that Dr. Nair's history is unavailable to us.

When I speak of Dr. Nair today, those newly involved in the Dravidian movement listen politely merely because I am saying something. But the emotional awakening that arises in my mind when I think of Dr. Nair, and the still greater awakening that arises in the mind of our chairman, will not arise in many of you. That is not your fault. It is a deficiency caused by our failure to compile and preserve our history.

Dr. Nair was a great leader who worked simultaneously in the political, economic, and social spheres. The later Justice movement was neglected to some extent by the people because it failed to carry all three forward together. Dr. Nair gave equal weight to all three, drew them forward as one, and infused every sphere with a revolutionary spirit. That is why the college students of those days came to hear Dr. Nair with the same enthusiasm with which people now come to Dravidian movement meetings on the Triplicane beach.

Today Congress leaders, without shame or fear, heap accusations upon the Dravidian movement in an effort to destroy it at its roots. Similar words of abuse were heaped upon Dr. Nair as well.

**Editorial footnote:** The journal *Justice* records that seven conferences and hundreds of special meetings were held between August and the end of December 1917.

Just as the Dravidian movement has survived all this abuse today, and not merely survived but gained new strength and continued to grow, Dr. Nair too endured every accusation for as long as he lived. By Tamil Nadu's misfortune, he did not live long. During his lifetime he possessed the courage to withstand the arrows hurled by his opponents.

On one occasion, prominent Congress leaders from Mylapore and Triplicane mocked Dr. Nair and people like him as singers of praise to the British, servants of the British, and supporters of British imperialism. I have read in their historical records the words with which Dr. Nair replied. He said: “My Congress friends who speak of nationalism earn money in India and take it to England to spend. I run a hospital in England, earn the Englishman's money, bring it back, and spend it in India. Am I the true nationalist, or are they?”

Dr. Nair had a hospital on a street in England where distinguished physicians lived. He was an eminent surgeon. Even English patients waited there for his arrival. For three or four months each year, he travelled to England, treated patients, learned about political conditions, earned money, and brought it back to spend here. Whenever I read that he made this argument, my heart swells with joy as I reflect upon what a person of such courage the Tamils possessed in 1917. It is right that we should rejoice in that memory.

On another occasion, when Dr. Nair was condemned by the nationalists of this country, he made an open declaration that appeared in an English newspaper of the time. I found within it a treasury of the idea of Dravida Nadu. He wrote clearly: “At any time Tamilians will prefer London to Delhi.” If asked whether we were to be enslaved to London or enslaved to Delhi, Dr. Nair wrote even then that we South Indians would, if necessary, remain subject to London and win our freedom from it, but would not remain enslaved to Delhi.

It was Dr. Nair who sowed the seed from which Dravidian ethnic consciousness spread through Tamil Nadu. He was born in Kerala, lived in Tamil Nadu, and laboured for the Tamils. Dr. Nair may not be known even in Kerala as well as he is known in Tamil Nadu, because, on reaching young adulthood, he left Kerala and settled in Tamil Nadu.

In those days, our Justice Party leaders worked together: Dr. Nair from Kerala, eminent leaders from Andhra, leaders from Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, and, because Orissa was then joined with us, leaders from Orissa as well. The very name they gave their organisation was the South Indian Liberal Federation, also rendered as the South Indian Liberation or Welfare Rights Association. Their emphasis was on South India.

Later, however, believing that they were expanding the Justice Party, they stretched “South India” still further, held a conference in Bombay, and established branches in Kolhapur and Calcutta. In doing so, they themselves surrendered the organisation's distinctive South Indian character. That, rather than any defect in the seed sown by its founder, was why the Justice Party later fell into a bad condition. Its founder had sown a sound seed and given sound ideas. I reflected on this as our chairman spoke today.

You all know very well that whenever Congress leaders set out to abuse the Dravidian movement, and me in particular, their single accusation is: “Do we not know that Annadurai? He is a Justice Party man.” You have heard them say it.

Every time they have said so, I have replied clearly and firmly that I feel neither shame nor pain in acknowledging my involvement in the Justice Party. I have said many times on the platforms of Tamil Nadu that I did everything within my power to help the Justice Party movement reach the stage it ought to have reached. My conscience knows this, and so do those who have studied the political conditions of Tamil Nadu.

Therefore, when Congressmen say that the DMK descends in a direct line from the Justice Party, I am neither distressed nor ashamed.

In its later years, the Justice Party became an organisation composed only of people seeking titles and offices, depending upon the protection of the law, and believing it enough merely to secure seats in the legislature. But when it was founded, it was a movement intended to uplift ordinary people, redeem Non-Brahmin society, and enable that society to win freedom and progress in the economic and political spheres. It was not an organisation founded merely to obtain titles. There is therefore nothing demeaning for anyone in acknowledging association with that movement.

In later years the movement was like an elephant sinking into mud. When one leg has entered the mire and the elephant tries to lift it with the other three, all three sink as well and the elephant cannot emerge. In this fashion the Justice Party gradually became detached from the popular movement. The reason was that education had not spread widely enough in the country at that time for people to understand the principles and ideas the Justice Party expounded.

I remember it clearly. At a conference held in Cuddapah, I spoke for an hour, in English, about the Tilak Swaraj Fund fraud case then widely discussed in Tamil Nadu. The Raja of Bobbili, who was then president of the Justice Party, and several other Justice Party leaders were seated beside me. A prominent member of the party sat nearby.

For an hour I explained how one crore rupees had been collected in the name of the Tilak Swaraj Fund, how grave frauds had occurred in the handling of that money, and how the newspaper *The Mahratta*, published in Maharashtra, had run a series of editorials exposing those frauds. When the afternoon session adjourned and we were leaving for lunch, the Justice Party notable put his arm around my shoulder and said, “Annadurai, you speak very well. But why did you tell such an enormous lie?”

I was stunned. “Sir, what did I say? What are you referring to?” I asked.

“You said the Congressmen collected one crore rupees and swallowed it. How can you say such a thing? When did they collect it? Where did they swallow it?” he asked.

I replied, “Sir, has not T. A. V. Nathan written six excellent editorials about it in our own *Justice* newspaper? Have you not read them?”

He said, “I am not in the habit of reading *Justice*.”

It was only after leaders of that kind arrived that the Justice Party declined.

At Salem I tried to release the Justice Party from the hands of such leaders, not to destroy the party's principles.

The Non-Brahmin movement was founded in pursuit of equal justice; it did not personally hate Brahmins. You know the accusation now levelled against me: that for the past six months Annadurai has suddenly become favourable to Brahmins. Whether I am favourable to them or not is another matter. In truth, there is no need to hate Brahmins personally. The Justice Party was called *Neethi Katchi*, the Party of Justice, in Tamil precisely because it rested on the principle that Brahmins should receive their proper share and Non-Brahmins should receive the share properly due to them. When a Justice Party ministry was formed, a Brahmin named S. S. Sethu Rathnam Iyer also served as a minister. I draw your attention to that fact.

Where the Justice Party failed was in mistakenly calculating that Non-Brahmins consisted only of educated degree-holders seeking government employment. It counted only such people as the B.A. graduate unable to find a job, the M.A. graduate seeking a higher post, or the tahsildar awaiting promotion as collector. It failed to see Kandan pulling a handcart, Varadan keeping a small shop, the agricultural labourer, the unemployed man, the labourer who toiled until midday yet lacked a single mouthful of rice and suffered blows from the mirasdar, the worker enduring the overseer's whip, and the poor man who, unable to make a living here, fled to Ceylon as a coolie. It refused to see these great numbers of poor Non-Brahmin people.

I think there may have been two reasons for that refusal. First, the leaders may have believed that once their government came to power they could uplift these people. Second, they may have thought that taking up the problems of the poor would damage the issue they had already embraced.

But I have watched the Garuda Sevai festival in Kanchipuram. Brahmins stand beside the ceremonial vehicle holding an enormous umbrella whose weight is almost unbearable. The vehicle itself is heavy, and its bearers sway as they carry it. Hundreds of thousands of people stand before them, packed so tightly that, as the saying goes, there is not enough space for a sesame seed to fall. If the umbrella sways even slightly in the wind, it could topple.

At such moments I have closely watched the Brahmins holding it. I have seen a Brahmin accustomed to chewing betel prepare his betel without letting the umbrella fall. He does it with remarkable skill: taking the leaf with two fingers, spreading lime with another, and continuing all the while to hold the umbrella. At the same time, if he notices familiar devotees along the route, especially devotees of some means, he presents them with a smile. He keeps an eye upon Lord Varadaraja's *makara kandigai* necklace and, at the same time, searches for the stem of his tobacco leaf.

The Justice Party failed to perform that feat. It looked only at educated Non-Brahmins. Like the man in my example, it failed simultaneously to see poor Non-Brahmins and those tormented by the cruelty of destitution. That was why I concluded that, if the party remained in their hands, the Justice movement could never become a movement of the common people.

Although it had been born in a palace, the Justice Party had to come out into the open, beneath the banyan tree, if it was to serve the people. Siddhartha too was born in a palace, but before establishing the Buddhist path he came beneath the sacred fig tree. With this conviction, at the Salem conference Periyar Ramasamy laboured greatly and, with all of us assisting him, brought the Justice Party out of its palace and beneath the banyan tree.

After it had been brought there, I decided that the Justice movement should not remain beneath the banyan tree indefinitely. I have led it into a somewhat broader open ground. Periyar Ramasamy concluded that remaining beneath the banyan tree was sufficient.

Our friends have therefore begun to wonder what this political condition will become and how it will stand in 1967.

I have recounted what happened from 1917 to 1957 for a reason. The seed sown by our leaders sprouted well and became a flourishing plant. Although many people tried to destroy it in the intervening years, it has continued to grow soundly. In the same way, the growth we possess in 1957 will, by 1967, become so great that we ourselves will marvel at it. No one need doubt this. Because of the conditions now available in 1957 to the DMK and to a Tamil Nadu that lives by listening to the DMK's ideas, within another ten years, and in these very ten years, results of immense value will take shape in our society.

Perhaps anticipating this, Finance Minister Subramaniam said: “I appeal to Annadurai and the members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam: abandon the demand for partition. If you cannot abandon it, at least refrain from pressing it for ten years.”

When I say this, Congressmen may respond that Subramaniam denied making such a statement and that his denial appeared in the newspapers. You must observe when he denied it and why. You must consider why he spoke in the first place if he was then compelled to deny his own words. There is some meaning when we speak hastily; we are inexperienced. But no one can say that Finance Minister Subramaniam lacks experience. He served in the Constituent Assembly and in the previous ministry. He is a man of considerable experience. Only later did he realise that, with all this experience, he ought not to have spoken so hastily that day.

As soon as he spoke, newspapers in Calcutta and the northern newspapers of Bombay and Delhi wrote editorials condemning him. “How can you seek a ten-year agreement? They seek to partition the country. They number fifteen while you number 150. Can you sacrifice the soul of India merely to obtain their cooperation? Is this what you are a minister for?” They wrote whatever came to mind.

I too felt deeply sorry. “Poor man! He has only just begun to speak a little truth. If these wretches had waited until he had spoken five or six more times, it would not have mattered. But they have attacked him after his very first attempt. He will be more guarded from now on,” I thought regretfully.

Finance Minister Subramaniam was then compelled to repair his position.

A member rose and asked: “What is this? You ask them to postpone partition for ten years. Do you accept the partition of the country?”

Before the Finance Minister could answer, the Speaker said, “That concerned something that happened then. Why raise a question about it now? Not now.”

The Finance Minister himself immediately stood and said, “No, no. If the Speaker permits me, I would like to explain one matter.

“I do not accept partition. I did not accept it yesterday, I do not accept it today, and I shall never accept it. India is one and indivisible. I never asked for partition to be postponed for ten years. What I asked them to postpone for ten years was destructive activity.”

Is this reasonable? It makes sense to say, “Do not engage in destructive activity.” But if one says, “Do not engage in destructive activity for ten years,” does that confer the right to do so in the eleventh year? Would anyone tell a scorpion, “Wait until I have eaten and returned, and then sting me”?

If one knows that a scorpion will sting, one must seek protection so that it never stings. If one knows that destructive activity may occur in the country, one must say that it must never occur at all. What can it mean to say that there should be no destructive activity for ten years?

Why was he forced to speak in so meaningless a fashion? At the very first meeting where I spoke, I told our minister that whenever he said anything, those in the North became suspicious of him. I said, “Sir, denounce us a thousand times if you wish. But use us, at least, to obtain authority from the North.” I gave him the example of the Sappers and Miners. They are one among the several divisions of an army. Their task is to prepare the road when the artillery comes behind them.

“Use us like that regiment,” I said.

Finance Minister Subramaniam replied: “What Annadurai says is true. I too would like to act in that way. But how can I trust him? After clearing the enemy's road, what am I to do if he plants a fresh landmine before leaving? I will therefore proceed only with great caution.” Having guarded himself in this way, he added: “He has now said this openly. The North Indian will surely hear it. If he does, he will suspect that Annadurai and I are together. What am I to do about that?”

He thus lamented his predicament in the Legislative Assembly. It was to prevent such suspicion from falling upon him that he later denied in the Assembly that he had sought a ten-year agreement.

You ask what will happen during these ten years. This tendency will continue. Suspicion will fall upon one person after another. We cannot now say who will be suspected next. One day they may suddenly become suspicious even of Kamaraj.

Do not imagine that this will happen because of any method we employ. That is the direction indicated by the times.

Whether they admit it or not, even the great leaders now in Congress inwardly understand that South India can obtain the progress it needs, and its people can be uplifted, only if it receives greater authority than it possesses today. Congressmen themselves recognise that otherwise they cannot succeed in this work.

They abuse us, and we know it. They call us destroyers because we demand partition. Yet, after finishing their abuse, when they are alone, the thought that arises in their own minds is: “What these people say is true. For everything we have to go to the North. All authority is concentrated there. What do we possess here?” This sense of humiliation lives within them.

Four days ago, when Chief Minister Kamaraj visited my constituency, local people asked him for a spinning mill in Kanchipuram. “Why not? It can be established,” he said. But he immediately added, “It can be established, but yarn allocation will not be available. An order for that must come from Delhi.”

Afterwards I asked my friend in Kanchipuram, “Did you hear what Kamaraj said? He is a good man and wishes to act, but he has no authority. Everything rests in Delhi.” The moment he understood this, that man became a member of the DMK. Everyone who understands this truth belongs to the DMK. Their number will grow day by day; it will not diminish.

The North's development is plainly visible. It is also becoming increasingly clear that if the declining South is to advance again, the already developed North will not readily make room for it.

I often tell you that the temple priest knows better than the devotee that the goddess Kali will grant no boon. You think the priest beats his little drum because of divine power. In truth, he beats it because of the cruelty of hunger and the need to earn a living. Ask him privately whether Kali grants boons, and he will first look at what you carry in the fold of your garment. If he sees a coconut and camphor there, he will exclaim, “Ah! She is a visibly proven deity. Kali helped Kandasamy; Kali restored Samikannu's sight.”

But if you have nothing and ask only out of curiosity, the priest himself will retort: “Why are you such a fool? If Kali is powerful, why has she left me in this condition? My father was a priest, I am a priest, and my son too is a priest. That is the extent of Kali's power. If this Kali has not lifted even me up, where is she going to lift you?”

Delhi is likewise the deity. People like us may not know whether it grants boons. But the Congress priests who beat the drum know very well.

They have tried beating the drum three times.

No issue could be more just and untainted for the Tamils than the Devikulam–Peermade question. Our Madras Government itself produced statistics showing that Tamils lived there. The Tamil Nadu Congress Committee passed a resolution declaring that Devikulam and Peermade belonged to the Tamils. Here in Madurai, Kamaraj himself said, “We shall secure them for you.” What did Delhi do?

It said that Kerala demanded them and that they must therefore remain with Kerala. What did the Kamarajs do then? In their evening speeches these esteemed Congress comrades proclaim: “Do not speak of North and South. We are not slaves to the North; we can stand upright. We descend from Nakkeeran, who declared a fault to be a fault even when Shiva opened his third eye.” But when Delhi rejected our spotless claim in the Devikulam–Peermade dispute, what did they do? Did they so much as knit their brows for a moment? Which minister said, “I shall not return without securing Devikulam”? Who said, “If Devikulam is not obtained, I shall cease to be a minister”?

From a thousand platforms they once looked at the Justice Party and asked, “Is it stuck there because someone applied glue?” Today these men remain stuck without anyone applying glue at all. The Justice Party at least had glue: it clung to the adhesive supplied by the white man. Now there is no one even to supply the glue. If they remain attached when no one is there to apply it, what example can I offer except to say that they cling like leeches?

Let that pass. That concerns a national issue. In their own accounts they wrote that the Second Plan required 400 crores. What were they given? What did they receive? In what respect have they escaped northern domination?

Their demand that bordered dhotis and saris be reserved for handlooms was not mere talk. They passed a resolution in the Legislative Assembly and sent it to Delhi. What happened to it? What respect did Delhi show it?

That is why they know even better than we do the many ways in which the North belittles the people of the South.

Why do they not say so openly today? Recall the story I told you. If the priest admits that Kali grants no boons, what will become of the livelihood he earns by beating his drum?

Similarly, if a Congress minister were to say, “We possess no authority; everything rests in the North,” who would respect these ministers afterwards? The drum is heard only because the priest must retain his employment. No boon comes from Kali.

If that boon is to be obtained, it must be obtained in these ten years. The degree to which our work succeeds during this decade will determine whether the next generation enjoys a life of self-respect.

If our endeavour during these ten years fails, you may write it down as clearly as an inscription carved in stone: the ancient Tamil born and living here will meet the same fate as the Black people of Africa and the Native Americans of America. No other kind of progress will be available to him.

At the critical moment when milk rises to boil over, a mother will not leave the stove even if her child cries. A thoughtful mother, seeing the milk rise, decides that she can attend to the crying child a moment later. She first sprinkles a little water upon the boiling milk, takes the pot down, and only then goes to nurse the child crying in its cradle.

These ten years are the moment when the milk is rising. During this decade our ideas will enter through the ears of those who now refuse to hear them, remain in their hearts, and draw them all towards us. This is the critical time.

Fifteen of us from the DMK entered the Legislative Assembly. You may be astonished if I say that, in the three months since we entered it, the demand for the separation of Dravida Nadu has achieved as much growth as it had during the previous ten years. But this is no exaggeration.

Foreign newspapers have only now begun to write: “There is said to be a party called the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam demanding the separation of Dravida Nadu, with ten or fifteen members in the legislature.”

Coming of age is not confined to human beings; parties too come of age. A party reaches political maturity when it enters political activity.

I am not unaware that danger also arrives at the very moment of maturity. That is true of the body and of politics. It is a dangerous stage.

But you may confidently believe one thing. Along with whatever natural intelligence I possess, Periyar Ramasamy has trained me well enough to prevent dangers and avoid those that arise, and to make those who set out intending to kindle danger lament later that they themselves have been caught in it. That training will serve me at the proper time. I will not be easily deceived.

If even he could not make me waver, by whom else in Tamil Nadu could I be deceived?

At this mature stage, therefore, the DMK will never conduct itself in a manner that makes us subject to others or afraid of them. I said in the Assembly, and I say here too, that we did not enter it merely to abuse Congress ministers.

When two or three DMK comrades enter the legislature, they have three duties. First, they must secure whatever benefit they can for the people of the constituency that elected them. Second, they must state the movement's position on every issue debated in the Assembly. Third, as emissaries of their movement, they must work without allowing its fundamental principle, its foundational doctrine, to be impaired in the slightest.

Whoever enters a legislature, and whichever party they belong to, has these three responsibilities. They must not abandon principle in the name of constituency development. Nor may they keep only principle in mind and say, “I do not care what ruin overtakes my constituency.” They must attend to the constituency, participate in legislative debate, and still not surrender the DMK's fundamental ideas.

Carrying all three responsibilities forward together is mature parliamentary conduct. Experienced political observers have told us, and we sincerely believe, that the DMK has maintained this balance properly until now. Whatever issue or law is brought before the House, DMK members do not hesitate to state their views in accordance with its every turn and complexity. In doing so, they also offer alternatives. At the same time, they do not antagonise Congressmen without cause.

If you have watched closely, you will know that although the DMK spoke from the opposition benches in every debate, we demanded a recorded vote on only two issues. We knew they possessed a majority and that we numbered only fifteen. We did not demand a count on every matter. We sought one on these two because they concerned not merely our party but the country. The first was the proposal that this land should be named “Tamil Nadu.”

After every friend had spoken on the cut motion we submitted on this matter, and after Congress members had spoken against it, the Speaker rose and asked whether we would withdraw all our cut motions.

I rose on behalf of the DMK and said: “I withdraw every other cut motion. But I am not prepared to withdraw the motion to name the State ‘Tamil Nadu.’ I demand that it be put to a vote.”

The Communists agreed. The Socialists agreed. The Praja Socialists agreed. Members of the Congress Reform Committee and the independents all agreed. When the vote was taken, every member of the opposition rose in support of the name “Tamil Nadu.” All 150 members on the Congress side rose to say that “Tamil Nadu” was not wanted.

Why did we demand a vote on this matter alone? We knew we would be defeated. But should the country not see who among the Tamils would say that Tamil Nadu should not be called “Tamil Nadu”?

Our friend Sankaran, the member for Madurai city, also stood against us. Only two days earlier, he had offered us advice: “If these DMK men speak so intensely of Tamil and Tamil Nadu, why do they not change their party's name to the Tamil Munnetra Kazhagam?” Yet our friend Sankaran, who made that recommendation to us, also stood with Congress.

I watched him as he rose, to see whether he stood proudly. He stood with his head bowed. He knew that he had been born in Tamil Nadu, that the language he spoke while lying in his cradle was Tamil, and that the language he speaks today while lying in bed is Tamil. In court, if necessary, his speech may be a mixture of English and Tamil. Yet such a man rose under party discipline to say that this land did not need the name Tamil Nadu.

Even that was not so surprising. Our friend Vinayagam labours to prevent even a single English word from entering the bounds of Tamil. Yet he too stood. When he rose, I turned and asked, “Why, sir, you too?” But he would not even turn to look at me.

If a young woman is married to an elderly husband, will she gaze directly into his face? She will look at the ground. In the same way, they bowed their heads, thinking, “These wretches have placed us in such a predicament.” We held our heads high. We rose to demonstrate that a Tamil would naturally name his country “Tamil Nadu.” They rose in shame that so many Tamils were present to say that Tamil Nadu should not bear that name.

While still seated I asked Kamaraj, “You have brought so many Tamils here to say that Tamil Nadu is not wanted. You are indeed a very clever man, sir!”

He replied, “Never mind. We shall see, we shall see.”

You may ask what we achieved merely by forcing a vote on that issue. I hear that even Periyar Ramasamy asks: “Those fellows entered the Assembly without listening to me, though I opposed them so strongly. What have they achieved?”

We achieved nothing immediately. What we did achieve was to write a new chapter in the history of Tamil Nadu. Its meaning is not visible now. Our grandson will read it. On one page he will see that this country was once called Madras. He will turn to a friend beside him and ask, “Look how foolish our elders were! Why was our country called Madras?”

His friend will say, “No, its true name is Tamil Nadu. They named it Madras and later changed the name to Tamil Nadu.”

“Who laboured to change it?”

“These people and those people laboured for it.”

“How did they struggle?”

“They introduced a resolution in the Legislative Assembly.”

“Did it obtain a majority when it was introduced?”

“No.”

“Who wanted the name?”

“Fifteen members wanted it.”

“Who said Tamil Nadu was not wanted?”

“One hundred and fifty members said it was not wanted.”

“Did even a Tamil who did not want Tamil Nadu exist in those days?”

“Yes, sir. Such a Tamil existed.”

“Where is he now?”

“He is no longer alive. Look, his grandson is standing over there.”

Friends, write this down clearly. We fifteen are not writing the history of the present; we are writing the history of the future. We write it with our tears, our blood, and our sweat. It may take ten years to dry; it may take twenty. But every sentence written in that history will become an instrument of awakening for the future generations of our country.

The second issue on which we demanded a vote concerned handloom workers. Congressmen rose against us on that issue too, including Congressmen who had obtained the votes of handloom weavers. Congress members from this southern region spoke of handloom weavers with melting hearts and tear-filled eyes. I appreciate their good intentions and respect their sincere feeling. But because of party discipline, they too rose and voted against the proposal.

We demanded votes only on these two matters and not on the others. From this you may understand that DMK members work in the legislature without forgetting their fundamental principles, while also attending to the affairs of their constituencies.

It was to attend to constituency matters that I took Chief Minister Kamaraj to my constituency. A few days earlier, while we were in the Assembly, I asked him casually, “Will you visit our constituency?”

“Why not? I will come,” he said.

When he agreed, he may have thought: “Let me go there and see who helps this Annadurai and who gives these people their influence. I can meet them directly and seek that influence for myself.” I am not ignorant of the possibility that he came with such a thought. But because the goods I possess are colour-fast, I told him, in effect, “Wash them and test them for yourself.”

Why did I invite him? Neither I nor the party to which I belong forms the majority; ours is a minority party. We cannot issue orders and carry out constituency works ourselves. I am obliged to show the people of my constituency who holds the pen and who possesses the authority to issue orders.

I therefore took him there, seated him before the people, and said: “Look, this is the Chief Minister. He must repair the breached tank bund. He must repair that sluice. He must repair the break in the canal. Your taxes are paid to his government. My task is to ask him to do these things. Now, sir, you tell them what you will do.”

As the representative connected with that constituency, I am duty-bound to act in this way.

If he does good for the constituency, I shall praise him sincerely. If he fails, I shall return to my constituents and say, “What do you ask me to do? I brought him here and showed him the problems directly.” They themselves will reply: “That man spoke properly when he visited. He said he knew the suffering of the poor. Does he still not understand it?” Five years later, they will give their answer in the election. That is their concern, not mine. This is why I participated in that issue.

I likewise participated in the second kind of work: we must state our position on the laws introduced there. In the Assembly session just concluded, no new law was introduced. If a new law capable of stirring the people's hearts were brought forward, the DMK would have an opportunity to state its views upon it.

For now, Kamaraj merely carries on the administration, like the Brahmin cook at a public rest house who cooks for ten people and divides the same quantity equally among twenty. He has still introduced no new legislation. Whether a harmful law like those introduced by Achariyar, or a beneficial law of the kind we all expect, if any measure capable of stirring the people's hearts is introduced, we are duty-bound to state our position clearly upon it.

I have some doubt, however, whether such fundamental laws will appear under Kamaraj's government. He seems to think only of passing the time for as long as he remains in office. He does not appear to possess the desire to point with pride and say, “We achieved this in our time; we fulfilled this in our time.” I see in him only the general wish that good should be done for the people and that some good may be done. I do not see in him a serious view of the fundamental structures that must be changed if the people are truly to benefit.

The year 1967 will bring us to an important stage on the road we now travel. I speak to you with an open heart: within these ten years, conditions will arise for great struggles directed towards the liberation of our country. The enthusiastic young people involved in our movement must begin preparing themselves today for those great struggles.

You need not suppose that we shall sit as members of the Legislative Assembly throughout this decade. It is impossible to predict how long we shall remain in the Assembly or how long the present enthusiasm for legislative work will continue. Before 1967, when these great moral struggles against northern imperialism arise in Tamil Nadu, I shall appeal from this great gathering, from this city of Madurai, to dedicated Dravidian comrades.

I look for many Bhagat Singhs. I call for many Tiruppur Kumarans. I gladly call for many V. O. Chidambaranars. Unless people like them, endowed with a noble spirit of sacrifice, arise in Dravida Nadu, and unless they arise during these ten years, it will become impossible to recover our sacred motherland in accordance with the decision we take at this gathering.

We are ordinary people, but the issue we have undertaken is immense. It is an issue that cannot be destroyed by people of any strength. It demands a true spirit of sacrifice. To acquire that spirit, we must honour those who sacrificed elsewhere.

That is why I have always respected the genuine people of sacrifice within Congress and praised them. I have saluted Congressmen who endured police batons and honoured those who went to prison. They all laboured for the freedom of the country along the path that appeared right to them.

They believe that the chapter of liberation has been completed. They have not yet understood that its final chapter remains unwritten. The last chapter of liberation can be written only when Dravida Nadu gains freedom. You are the people qualified to write that chapter, and that is why the DMK labours among you.

I therefore appeal to all of you to acquire that sacrificial spirit. You and I are steadily attaining the maturity of mind required for it, and I believe that maturity will yield good results within ten years.

In 1957 we have looked into the Legislative Assembly. In 1967, whether we are inside the Assembly or outside it, the rulers in Delhi may summon us there, or they may come here. Perhaps, considering it beneath us to go there and beneath them to come here, both sides may sit somewhere midway and they may ask us, “Why do you demand Dravida Nadu?”

We shall state our reasons.

They may say, “We can accept this reason, but we cannot accept that one.”

We shall argue, “No, you must accept it.”

They may ask, “If Dravida Nadu separates, what will conditions be afterwards?”

We shall assure them, “After separation, there will be greater friendship than there is now.”

They may ask, “Can we believe that?”

We shall reply, “You may believe it completely.”

They may ask, “Upon what do you swear this assurance?”

We shall say, “We swear upon our Tamil heritage.”

If 1967 can open the way for such a conference to assemble and negotiate such an agreement, I believe that the labour many of you perform today will bear fruit on that day.

## Translator's Notes

The complete corrected Tamil source is retained verbatim. The website inserted two unrelated Rajya Sabha pages between Part 1 Images 12 and 15; those scans remain preserved and are documented in `_translation_state/working/1967_quarantined_scans.md`, but are not included in this speech or translation because Image 15 directly continues Image 12.

The title is the year `1967`, used by Anna as a ten-year political horizon from the speech date, 11 August 1957. The editorial footnote observes that the DMK did take office in 1967.

`பார்ப்பனரல்லாதார் இயக்கம்` is rendered historically as “Non-Brahmin movement.” `நீதிக்கட்சி` is rendered as “Justice Party,” while the speech also gives its formal English name, “South Indian Liberal Federation.” `நல உரிமைச் சங்கம்` is retained by sense as a Welfare Rights Association rather than treated as a separate organisation.

`மகரக் கண்டிகை` refers to Lord Varadaraja's ceremonial necklace or collar with a *makara* motif. The military expression `Sappers and Miners` is retained exactly as printed. `மிராசுதாரர்`, `தாசில்தார்`, and `சத்திரம்` are rendered contextually as mirasdar, tahsildar, and public rest house.

The speech's period language for Black Africans and Native Americans is translated descriptively rather than repeated as an English slur. The original Tamil remains visible in the source block.

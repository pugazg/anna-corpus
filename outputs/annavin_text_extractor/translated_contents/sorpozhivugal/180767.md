## English Translation

Debate held on 18 July 1967.

**The Hon. Thiru C. N. Annadurai:** Mr. Speaker, I understand that certain amendments have been moved to this resolution. This Bill concerns a question on which views have been expressed in this same House on more than two occasions.

If my memory is correct, an effort was first made when Kutti Krishnan Nair was Law Minister. Another attempt was made in 1952, and another in 1965. We are engaged in the effort again now.

By reading the debates from the three or four occasions when this question arose, one can see how those familiar with it gradually changed. Many were alarmed when such an amendment was first introduced. Nobody is alarmed now. When the question came a second time, fewer people were alarmed, though some still felt fear. Now even that fear has disappeared.

The present objection is only that our Dr. Hande repeatedly asks: “Was the Hindu the only person you could find? Why do you keep striking him on the head?” If Muslims seek reform, members of the Islamic community will undertake it, and that will be welcome.

Why is it necessary to deal specifically with Hindu society? Some say that, philosophically, Hinduism has omitted nothing found in any other religion and contains every philosophy. Some attribute this to Hinduism's imperishable quality; others to its indeterminate quality. Whichever is true, Dr. Hande need not look at the word “Hindu” and wonder whether this applies only to Hindus and not others.

I recall Swami Vivekananda saying that a true Hindu should not fear reform and should accept the ideas appropriate to the age. Adherents of Hinduism themselves have said that a true Hindu will not accept caste. There is therefore no need to look at the title and worry that this is directed only at the Hindu.

Dr. Hande is a fine friend. When I first heard his name, it did not even occur to me that it was a Hindu name. Friends told me there was a Dr. Hande who spoke very ably on political questions. We did not imagine he would be a Hindu; the name sounded otherwise. Only after meeting him did I remember that he belonged to our own community. At first I thought it an Anglo-Indian name. I do not know its meaning in Kannada.

**Thiru K. Vinayakam:** His name is Venkataramana Hande. Hande is the caste name.

**C. N. Annadurai:** If he had said “Venkataramana” at the beginning, I would have understood immediately…

What must be considered is that, over the past thirty or thirty-five years, a change has been taking place in the manner of conducting marriages. It began amid great opposition, many doubts and public fear. Thousands of marriages have since been conducted in this manner, and this form of marriage has grown old enough for those couples to have produced the next generation.

When the public has followed a particular custom for many years, the function of law is to give that custom legal form.

“Custom by usage becomes law. Custom by usage gets legal sanction.”

We are therefore only giving legal form now to a new practice that has existed in Tamil Nadu for thirty or forty years. This law does not say that, after it is enacted, everyone must enter into a self-respect marriage. In lawyers' language, it is permissive legislation.

A marriage may also be conducted in this manner, and such a marriage will be legally valid. This is not a law compelling everyone in Tamil Nadu to marry only this way, nor one prohibiting other forms of marriage. If it restricted others or prohibited other forms, public opinion would necessarily have to be ascertained. Since it neither restricts nor prohibits other forms, but merely grants permission to those who have married in this manner and those who wish to do so, I believe it is enough to explain it to knowledgeable men such as our friend Ponnappa Nadar. I hope to search the world and offer them a few ideas that may not yet have occurred to them...

I take it that he made the remark because we are about to consider this matter individually, not because necessity compelled him. He too understands marriage. At his age, the pleasurable side of marriage is especially prominent; he is still at that age.

Our Ponnappa Nadar supports him. He has experienced that pleasure to a great extent and also understands legal subtleties. People like him can serve on the Select Committee, contribute valuable views, and make society say not merely that this law is necessary but that it is a well-formed law. They have the opportunity, the right and the ability to do so.

Speaking for myself and my friends, I wish to say one thing and ask the other members of this House to permit me. We do not regard this as an ordinary reform.

For many years this idea lay sleeping in our hearts. Through many nights and many days we wondered whether such a law could ever be created. In times when no possibility appeared that it could be enacted, many thousands nevertheless married through this form of ceremony. To all of them, this news will, in Bharati's poetic phrase, flow as “pure honey.”

There should be no stinginess here, no obstacle placed in their happiness. The old arrangement must be changed. We must recognise that people who marry in this way should not remain outside the law and must give them legal protection. That is why this amendment is introduced.

Our friend, I think Thiru Ponnappa Nadar, mentioned that a court had held a marriage valid only if fire bore witness. The many discussions at self-respect marriages bring all those ideas back to my mind. I have no time to speak at length; I can see the clock.

I do not know how much meaning “with fire as witness” possesses today. I sometimes think about how Agni Bhagavan, considered fit to witness a marriage, torments poor people in the Mylapore area. At this time we must reduce, at least a little, even the greatness accorded to Agni Bhagavan.

Nobody can forget the immense difference between ideas inherited from an age when such things were believed and the ideas held now. Following the reasoning of our friend Thiru Ponnappa Nadar, who spoke of the fire god, I do not imagine he would today also say that the moon is a god.

In these days when Soviet and American scientists compete to determine when they can travel to the moon, the story that the moon is a god, that he went to learn from a rishi, chose another lesson for himself, and was cursed to wane and wax—thereby producing alternating full moons and new moons—was once accepted by everyone. Today everyone treats it as amusing. In the future it may be cast aside as a story unfit even to remain in books.

In such an age, if the law says only a marriage witnessed by fire is valid, and a section of society says, “We do not accept that; we marry in this other manner,” this law merely permits them to do so.

It is not like a law in an authoritarian country commanding society, “From the sixth of the month onward, you must act in this prescribed manner.” Nor is it a rationing law saying each person may receive only so much because of a food shortage. Anyone may marry in whatever manner they wish.

Our Leader of the Opposition, Thiru Karuthiruman, said, “I remain in the marriage chamber for only two minutes.” Young people generally do not remain there long. They finish quickly, go outside to their friends and enjoy asking, “Well? Did you see? Was it all right? I could not see much.”

Sitting for a long time on the wedding platform is something that my friend Thiru M. P. Sivagnanam, or I following him, may do only at a sixtieth-birthday remarriage.

I therefore believe this Select Committee is sufficient to refine the marriage law. I appeal to our friend Thiru Ponnappa Nadar. The public elected and sent us here to make reforms of this kind. We must honour, neither alter nor fail the mandate they gave us.

I ask Thiru Ponnappa Nadar to withdraw his amendment and support an idea we have held for thirty or forty years.

Members have expressed views. My friend Thiru Arangannal said this form of marriage transcends parties. Last week, All India leader Thiru Kamaraj went to Virudhunagar. The marriage conducted there could not have followed the old Vedic form.

So far as I know, for thirty years people around Virudhunagar, Sattur and Sivakasi have followed the practice of inviting an elder of their community to conduct a marriage.

Though our Thiru Ponnappa Nadar belongs to Kanyakumari district, in Kerala State, of which that district was once part, he would be familiar with marriages conducted by elders through the exchange of *mundu*, departing from Vedic ritual.

In Bengal, members of the Brahmo Samaj created such a change. In the Punjab too changes occurred. In Tamil Nadu, members of the Self-Respect Movement and great Tamil scholars such as the late Maraimalai Adigal and Thiru V. Kalyanasundaranar supplied literary evidence and endorsed this form as necessary, harmless, suited to the age and joined with Tamil culture.

The movement led by the respected Thiru M. P. Sivagnanam, President of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam, also supports it.

Even in an Islamic marriage, after the bride and groom are seated, they follow the practice of asking the bride, “Do you consent to this marriage?”

There is therefore nothing repugnant or frightening in this Amendment Bill. The opposition says it should again be circulated for public opinion. Whenever a Bill comes, parliamentary practice requires opposition members to move such an amendment. The same parliamentary practice, when we ask and when it is appropriate, makes withdrawing it the better parliamentary course.

I therefore ask my friend Thiru Ponnappa Nadar, who knows parliamentary practice well, to withdraw his amendment, cooperate with the Select Committee in framing a sound law, and share the pride of having given Tamil Nadu a worthwhile statute. With that, I conclude.

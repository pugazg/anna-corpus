## English Translation

Some friends who have only recently begun to hear about the concerns of our movement ask why we needlessly condemn Brahmins. They ask, "They are such a tiny minority in society; why must we denounce them at all times?"

This is an old question for our movement, a very old question. But many of those asking it are new arrivals to the movement. Their question is therefore genuinely asked in order to clear their doubt. It is our duty to dispel that doubt. First, we wish to tell those friends: please do not imagine that we condemn individual Brahmins out of hostility toward their community. What we condemn is a system called Brahminism.

Brahmins have been its original authors and guardians, while many non-Brahmins are its supporters.

Our condemnation, therefore, is directed at Brahminism. Let us now carefully consider the question: "Are Brahmins not a very small minority? Is it really necessary to condemn them and their system?" The issue cannot be understood merely by looking at the number of people involved. What must chiefly be considered is the extent of the influence that the system called Brahminism possesses in the country. When examined in that way, one can see that the issue does not stop with Brahmins bearing names such as Iyer, Iyengar, and Sharma; it has also become an issue "owned" by many non-Brahmins.

When considering a system, it is not enough merely to count the people who produced it. The extent of the influence that the system has acquired in the country is what must be considered most importantly. Only then will the full shape of the problem become visible.

Nationalist friends often ask, "Why should you fear the Brahmins? Why be jealous of them? They are only three in every hundred, are they not? Are you not ninety-seven in every hundred? Why should the majority community fear a minority community and seek protection from it?" Whenever they ask this, those friends admire their own intellectual ability.

If one looks only at society's numbers, Brahmins are a small group. The very breath of non-Brahmins would be enough to make them gasp; so vastly more numerous are the non-Brahmins. But Brahminism does not depend upon numbers. It depends upon the strength that has so far been supplied to it through many channels.

A warrior may be feared by an entire town, yet in a moment of delusion he fears the little bunch of neem leaves in a sorcerer's hand. The greatness attributed to those leaves and to the sorcerer is what creates a condition in which even the warrior becomes afraid. In the same way, though Brahmins are few in number, innumerable texts have for generations taught that they hold the keys to heaven and hell and the measure by which divine grace is dispensed; that receiving the service of other communities is their birthright; and that satisfying them is equivalent to satisfying God. Because that idea has been mingled with the people's blood over a very long period, a path has been created for that community to develop an immense influence wholly disproportionate to its numbers.

A bridle is small, but everyone knows what a horse suffers under it. A nose-rope is not even as thick as a cow's tail, but once caught by it, the cow submits. In the same way, even a small community will certainly grow influential if all manner of greatness is attributed to it. If a single aeroplane circles over a town, even nine thousand inhabitants become afraid. Why? Because they know that bombs may be dropped from it. This is why the problem called Brahminism must be condemned.

Has Brahminism not acquired enough "power" to make so many non-Brahmins ask, "Why should Brahmins be condemned?" When Brahminism is criticised, even the Ananthacharis may remain quiet; why is it the Avinashis who grow angry? That is the subtle point. Though its originating community is a very small minority, its system has spread so widely and gained such influence. That is why our movement regards this as an important problem. Until now, the rejection of and opposition to the resistance we have raised against Brahminism has arisen more from our own people than from Brahmins. Would it be futile to condemn a system so powerful? Would it be unnecessary for us to work courageously against a system possessing influence of that magnitude? We ask the friends who pose this question to reflect upon it.

## Translator's Notes

The source repeatedly distinguishes individual Brahmins from "Brahminism" as a social and ideological system; the translation preserves that distinction.

The paired references to "Ananthacharis" and "Avinashis" appear to contrast a recognisably Brahmin name with a non-Brahmin public figure or group. They are retained as names because the OCR source alone does not securely establish the intended individuals.

The source's political and social assertions are translated in the author's own rhetorical voice and have not been independently verified.

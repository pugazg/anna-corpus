தியாகராயர் நினைவு மன்ற முதலாண்டு விழாக் கூட்டத்தில்

எப்படி இருந்தது என்பதையும் இடையிலே எப்படி. மாற்றம்
ஏற்பட்டது என்பதையும் இன்றுள்ள தலைமுறையினர்
அவ்வளவாக அறிந்திருக்கமாட்டார்களென்று நான் கருதுகிறேன்.

ஓர் அரசியல் பகுதியாக திராவிடர் கழகம் தோன்றியது; பின்

படித்துக் கொண்டிருந்தார்கள்; அந்த நேரத்தில் நான் முதன்
முதலாக அண்ணாமலைப் பல்கலைக் கழகத்தில் பேச

என்னைப்பற்றித் தன்னுடைய கருத்தைத் தெரியப்படுத்தினார்;

என்று கருதி அவர் சென்னைக்கு வந்துவிட்டார்; அந்த

நம்முடைய நண்பர்கள் நம்முடைய கழகத்தில் ஈடுபாடு

பதவி கிடைத்திருக்கிறது!

நீங்கள் கேள்விப்பட்டால் வேடிக்கையாக இருக்கும் -

அப்பொழுது இராமையாதான்; நம் இயக்கத்திலுள்ள

## English Translation

Speech delivered by Arignar Anna on 24 June 1960 at the first anniversary meeting of the P. Theagaraya Memorial Forum in Madras Corporation’s 89th Division.

Theagaraya was a leader who, at an important stage in Tamil Nadu politics, peacefully carried out a fine revolution and won. I believe the present generation may not fully know the condition of Tamil politics before his time or how it changed thereafter.

When the people of Tamil Nadu could not live with heads held high, and Tamils occupied subordinate positions in government and political parties, Theagaraya founded the Non-Brahmin movement. The Self-Respect Movement began as one of its sharp points; the Dravidar Kazhagam emerged as a political component; and later we carried this long journey forward as the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

Therefore, anyone who knows Theagaraya’s service feels an urge to walk his path when hearing his name. Such a great leader dedicated his time, thought, intellect, strength, and accumulated wealth to the Non-Brahmin movement. Today that family has lost its prosperity.

Compare the position acquired by people who joined Congress with that of those who remained in our movement. When Anbazhagan and Navalar Nedunchezhiyan studied at Annamalai University, two other young men, Ramaiah and Shanmugham, studied there too. When I was first invited to speak at the university, Ramaiah said, “He should not speak among students; he is a communalist.” Once most students decided to invite me, he came to Madras rather than remain to witness my visit. That Ramaiah is today the Electricity Minister. The other man joined Congress and became a senior district official.

Our friends did not become ministers by joining our Kazhagam, but they gained a place in the hearts of Tamil people. Those who joined Congress received titles and office.

There is an amusing detail. Anbazhagan’s name too was Ramaiah then. The Ramaiah in our movement became Anbazhagan; the Ramaiah who entered Congress became a minister.

Congressmen know nothing about Theagaraya. To understand him, one must know that we conducted the Non-Brahmin movement. This forum established in his name carries DMK principles and policies to the people with distinction.

As I arrived, our friend Manivannan said Congressmen were engaging in rowdyism. Their actions are not so frightening that we must fear them. They behave so because they know this movement contains people prepared to accept every hardship and loss for principle.

They try such old plans once every couple of years. In twenty or thirty years of public life, I have often seen people stab with knives or throw stones. I am concerned for the stone-throwers and ask them at least to demand better wages: ten rupees to throw stones at a meeting, one hundred to disperse it, and one thousand and one to kill a leader. If they bargain so, perhaps their economic condition will improve.

Twenty years ago in Tupattunayakkanpettai there was a large wrestler named Muthuvel, who is still alive. He received six annas a day to break up my meeting. After hearing me, he told his employers: “Annadurai is a good man and speaks honestly; he even offered me advice. I shall not disrupt his meeting.” They said he would receive no wages. He replied, “If you do not pay me, I shall disrupt your meetings instead,” and continued collecting his daily wage. We have seen people of that kind.

Friends also said Congressmen speak obscenely. Can they speak more obscenely than Vibhuti Veeramuthu, who said, “Take the slipper and beat the black shirt”? Where is that Veeramuthu now?

They also spoke about the police. Police personnel are not our enemies; they perform a job there. We need not attack them. Our comrades must understand that we conduct the movement expecting all these things. Nowhere has the situation grown to a level requiring fear.

Their anxiety is this: the DMK has captured forty-five seats in the Corporation. What if it captures still more in the next general election? That fear causes the agitation we see.

In Thanjavur, Kamaraj said: “We have guns, and the guns contain bullets.” Just as he has the right to keep a gun ready to fire, we have the right to demonstrate that we can receive the bullet upon our chests. If one hundred people were shot dead at a Marina meeting, Dravida Nadu would arrive the following year.

I read a report yesterday that a motorist accidentally ran over and killed a child and that an enraged crowd killed the driver. If the government imagines that shooting us will destroy this movement, it reveals that it acts without understanding us. The rulers must know that if one drop of blood leaves my body, blood will flow from the eyes of Tamil Nadu’s three crore people.

The relationship among our members is not like the connection Congress creates by paying eight annas and bargaining for election votes. Ours is a relationship made through blood. It did not arise yesterday or within twelve months. Every comrade’s tears are mixed into this movement. I ask Kamaraj to read history and understand the power such affection can create. If he boasts of possessing a gun, he should remember that we have shed blood before.

Achariyar was stronger than Kamaraj and also threatened us with guns and fired upon protesters. Kamaraj wears no dark glasses and may at least see blood as blood. Achariyar wore dark glasses; perhaps the blood we shed did not appear red to him.

During the earlier anti-Hindi agitation, three saffron-clad religious figures stood behind me: Arunagiri Adigalar, Eelathu Sivananda Adigal, and Shanmugananda Adigal. Behind them stood the elderly Dr Dharmambal and many Tamil elder women still alive.

Today those behind me could look like General Thimayya’s troops if given khaki uniforms. They do not tremble before anything. In 1938 I was young and susceptible to attachment. When told I would go to prison, my wife might have wiped her eyes for a moment. Today our position is, “Will you go first, or shall I?” If we do not face the test now, when will we? I waited for the rulers to commit their error. We cannot let this moment pass.

This will not be merely an anti-Hindi agitation; it will also be a liberation struggle. We have acquired the courage to sustain it.

We must give the Anti-Hindi Agitation Committee an account of how many possess the inner strength to stand in the field without a trace of fear. However many are imprisoned, the rulers must know that we are prepared to protect our mother tongue and motherland. The day has come to show that number.

From today, prepare a list strong enough to say, whatever plan the committee gives us: “We come with our younger brothers, with our elder brother, and our mothers come too.” Such people are needed now.

Participants may enter with two legs and return after losing one; they may enter with two eyes and lose one through the government’s cruelty; one or both hands may be lost. We need unwavering hearts prepared to accept such stages. We must show that people exist in this country and in our homes who go beyond stone for stone or word for word and possess the courage to offer life itself.

Read again the speeches delivered at the Anti-Hindi Conference in Tiruvannamalai two years ago. It resolved that if Hindi were introduced, we would undertake a powerful agitation. The issue was subsequently examined at the Mayavaram and Pudukkottai General Councils and repeatedly by the Executive Committee.

President Babu Rajendra Prasad told Parliament that after 1965 English would be removed and Hindi introduced and that arrangements should be made. He is now in Leningrad. Only after learning of this did we decide at Kumarapalayam to fight Hindi. Until then, we had waited to learn the rulers’ decision.

They have now decided and invite us into a needless confrontation. We ask the President to withdraw the order and issue a new one stating that Hindi will neither be introduced nor imposed and that its expansion will occur only after learning the views of non-Hindi-speaking people.

If the President does not alter his course according to this resolution and Tamil opinion, the General Council has resolved to hold protest meetings and processions in every city and town on 31 August, condemning his policy and announcing our agitation.

Previously our General Secretary would say only, “Conduct protest meetings.” This time he has ordered: “Hold the meetings; if the government bans them, defy the ban.”

If we come to attend the Marina meeting on 31 August, I do not think we will be arrested on the way as before. This time they may let us meet you there and pretend to disperse the gathering with batons, but they know we can withstand a baton charge. They may seek a situation in which they can claim that people threw stones and earth and that firing became unavoidable.

On one side are bullets; on the other are you and I, showing our chests to those bullets. Between them stands 31 August. I need to know how many will say, “We shall preserve dignity and display Tamil culture and courage.”

I know there is strength in your arms and compassion in your hearts. Preserving our tradition, we must show compassion when our people suffer and courage before an opponent, and determine how many will participate at each stage.

I respectfully ask you to prepare now the plans for the nonviolent struggle following 31 August. If they fire or beat us, our friends must not run; they must lie down. Let the cruel men climb upon and trample your chests so the world may know how those rulers treat people who demand rights. We must believe that we have grown for such a purpose.

If we cannot protect Tamil, what respect can we possess at home or in the country? Recovering the country means protecting our home. Today we have a golden opportunity to carry forward the feeling and strength our comrades possess.

Like mixing sweetness into medicine refused by a child, the rulers say they do not impose Hindi while steadily imposing it. They still think this country’s people are helpless children in another’s hands.

If any organisations in the Indian subcontinent have fought for language, they are the Dravidar Kazhagam and DMK. Other States struggle to protect political position or religion. Tamil Nadu conducts a struggle for language.

Only if Tamil survives will our people survive. Only if our country belongs to us can we live with heads held high.

We have decided that the anti-Hindi agitation will be the foundation and beginning of a great liberation movement: to remove our humiliation, oppose the Central Government, resist its debt and tax burdens, and challenge the injustices of the Five-Year Plans. During the struggle, I need not speak of other matters.

I cannot meet you frequently. I have repeatedly presented what must be said with evidence. Now there is only one thing you must give me: your courage. You must prepare to give it.

## Translator's Notes

The speech is dated 24 June 1960. Its references to 31 August and the post-1965 official-language transition are translated in the immediate historical context of the proposed agitation.

The source advocates an “அறப்போர்,” rendered as a nonviolent struggle, while vividly anticipating state violence and bodily sacrifice. The rhetoric is preserved as historical speech and not converted into present-day instruction.

The OCR line “ஆகஸ்டு 37/-ந் தேதி” is read contextually as 31 August, which is stated repeatedly immediately before and after it; the source remains unchanged.

Community labels, regional generalisations, and the Marwari comparison appear in the period political source and are translated as historical rhetoric without endorsement.

## English Translation

## 20 February

Presidential address delivered by C. N. Annadurai, M.A., at the general strike on 20 February 1956:

> Whatever the flag, if the people are Tamil,  
> Unite in the alliance!

> Let us forget old enmity!  
> Let us join the ranks!  
> Let us end the foe!

"The Chief Minister of Mysore is consulting many people and asking himself, 'Is a Karnataka united with Mysore alone enough? If Madras too were to come to me, how much more splendid a realm could I occupy?' The Chief Minister of Travancore-Cochin, Panampilly Govinda Menon, is taking stock not only of the Devikulam and Peermedu taluks that have fallen to him, but also of the portion of Shencottah placed in his hands. He is gathering statistics: how many elephants are found in those regions? How much *vengai* timber? How much tea comes from the plantations? How much rubber? How much cardamom and clove?

"But we too have a Chief Minister. What is he doing? Two days ago, he presided over and conducted a little girl's dance performance.

"At this time, when the country is in turmoil, the Ministers of each State are gathering evidence favourable to their claims. They are collecting statistics and arguing their case. They are flinging letters of resignation in Nehru's face. Yet even though he knows that Tamil taluks such as Devikulam and Peermedu are being denied to Tamil Nadu, the Chief Minister of this State is presiding over a dance performance!

"We have no objection to Kamaraj fostering the arts by attending a performance in which a dancer displays her hands and waist, bends like a flowering vine, twists, and dances. Let the Ministers themselves dance too, if they can; we have no objection even to that. But what will Nehru, watching this, think? Will he not suppose: 'We declared that Devikulam and Peermedu would not go to the Tamils; we seized a portion of Shencottah as well and gave it to Kerala. Yet those people are not troubled. There is no turmoil in the country, no agitation. Look, the Chief Minister appointed for the people of that State is fostering the arts'?

"Will Pandit Nehru not also think: 'We said they would not have Devikulam and Peermedu, and the people of this State said nothing. We can therefore give Coimbatore to Karnataka and Madras to Andhra; we can cut Tamil Nadu into pieces and give any part to anyone we please. No one will lift even a little finger'?

"That is precisely why we say that we must resist and fight the injustice inflicted upon the Tamils. We wish to demonstrate that Kamaraj is not the country.

"Kamaraj is certainly not the country! Kamaraj is not the person responsible for the country! Kamaraj is the finial on the temple tower, not the tower itself! Kamaraj is the Chief Minister, not the people of the country.

"On whose side have the people gathered? A great formation stands beside the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam; another great formation stands beside the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam; another beside the Communists; and there are formations of people beside the Socialists and the Praja Socialists. Thus, the overwhelming majority of the country is neither with Kamaraj nor with the Congress.

"What does the Kamaraj side contain? Seven or eight Ministers, about a hundred members of the Legislative Assembly, and a few Congress commission agents. Even ten thousand people in the whole of Tamil Nadu would be a very generous estimate for all of them together! That is why every party has united to fight the injustice inflicted upon the Tamils. On 20 February, the rulers must realise that the people of the country have rallied to their side.

"This is the second in the series of meetings arranged by the Madras District Kazhagam during the past few days. Their purpose is to explain the DMK's policy on two or three major problems that torment and distress the people's hearts, and to invite you to participate in the action the DMK proposes to undertake. Yesterday, our DMK General Secretary spoke on Madras beach and explained the decisions of the party's General Council. Today I speak, and from tomorrow many other friends will continue to speak in various places.

"You must not merely attend all these meetings in large numbers and with enthusiasm. Instead of expecting a great deal of subject matter from them, you must draw enthusiasm from them and participate in the general strike to be held on 20 February. We ask you to give this effort immense support, not so that we may see you taking part in large numbers, but in a manner that makes the Government ruling the country recognise it.

"A great general strike is to take place throughout the country on 20 February. The leaders of all parties have gathered and adopted this decision; the DMK General Council has approved and supported it.

"What is to happen on 20 February is not an undertaking for which the DMK alone is responsible. The DMK has previously assumed sole responsibility and carried out many undertakings. Now, however, it has joined many other parties in a great endeavour.

"Whenever the DMK alone undertakes an action, our comrades conduct themselves with dignity and discipline. I ask them to act with even greater dignity and discipline now. The conduct of Kazhagam comrades in this joint endeavour must bring about that joyful day when all the other participating parties look at them and think, 'Ah! Is this not the splendid place where we ought to be? Why should we all remain separated any longer? Let us unite.'

"To appreciate this all-party struggle, you must understand what a magnificent formation the gathering of all the parties represents, what a great joint endeavour it is, and what a tremendous opportunity it offers.

"Only two parties are absent from this all-party formation. One is the Congress. It cannot join this formation, and so it has not. The other is the Dravidar Kazhagam. The reason given for its absence from the formation is not appropriate. If all of us act with great dignity, I believe that they too will seek to join it.

"There is no benefit in investigating whether this person or that person has joined the formation. Every party flies its own flag and preserves its principles. No one has abandoned anything, surrendered their distinct identity, and joined the alliance. They have come together only to find a path through a common problem."

"The formation of this magnificent alliance is a great diplomatic victory that our General Secretary has secured for the DMK within a year of assuming office. Until now, an accusation had been levelled against the DMK: 'They are like oil poured upon water. They will mix with nothing and allow nothing to mix with them. They will do nothing and allow nothing to be done.' By creating this alliance, our General Secretary has removed that charge against the DMK.

"The parties may have been enemies in the past, and enmity may arise again later. In the interval, however, they have united.

"You did not have the opportunity to witness the conference at which all the parties gathered. I was given that opportunity. Let me bring the scene before your mind's eye so that you may understand what a beautiful and splendid picture it was.

"On one side was M. P. Sivagnanam. Friends, listen closely to my words. I speak of Comrade M. P. Sivagnanam, not M. P. Sivagnana Gramaniar. M. P. Sivagnana Gramaniar is the leader of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam; there was a period of enmity between him and us. But recall the earlier days when he and we worked together. No one can deny that Comrade M. P. Sivagnanam has deep affection for Tamil Nadu and love for us.

"We worked together when we demanded that monastic property be made the property of the people. We worked together for the introduction of the Inam Bill. He and I participated together in a lecture programme at the Tiruchirappalli radio station. Our relationship was once that close. During the interval, however, some sinners and mischief-makers severed that relationship. We have now reunited. Devikulam and Peermedu have brought us together; long may they live! He wishes that the relationship now formed should, 'by the grace of God,' endure and benefit Tamil Nadu. We desire the same; you too must bless it.

"On another side of the alliance were the leaders of our Communist Party, P. Jeevanandam and Manali Kandasami. On another stood the Socialist Party leader Anthony Pillai, leader of nearly fourteen thousand workers, holding all their prosperity and adversity in his hands; O. Subramaniam stood beside him. On another side was Chinnadurai, leader of the Praja Socialist Party, along with Nallasivam. Elsewhere stood Suyamprakasam, leader of the Dravidian Parliamentary Party; P. T. Rajan, leader of the Justice Party; Comrade T. K. Shanmugam representing the actors; and the leaders of organisations such as the Northern Border Protection Committee and the Unification Committee.

"It was as though all the *navagrahas* in a temple, having suddenly faced in different directions, turned and stood with a single face. The many parties gathered together that day, formed ranks, and devised a plan. Was that not a beautiful sight? Those who united the Tamils in this cause and made way for the alliance have done the Tamils immense good; they have performed a noble deed. I wish this organisation to endure so that it may accomplish many different tasks in the future.

"When Cheran Senguttuvan heard that the Tamil language and the Tamil people had been insulted, he vowed, 'Who are those Kanakan and Vijayan? I shall raise an army against the arrogant men who reviled the Tamils and end the foe.' Some who stood beside him are said to have remarked, 'King, Kanaka Vijayan did not insult the Chera or you. He merely insulted some Tamils.' Senguttuvan immediately replied, 'Fools! What difference is there between insulting me and insulting Tamil? Why do you not understand that even an insult to the soil and trees of Tamil Nadu is an insult to me? All Tamils are siblings, born of one people, bearing Tamil blood, and belonging to the Tamil clan. How did you fail to understand that those who belong to the Tamil clan, Tamil race, and Tamil language travel one path?'

"Like that Senguttuvan of old, who destroyed Kanakan and Vijayan for insulting the Tamils in general, our General Secretary today has embraced the other parties and formed a united front to defeat the great peril confronting Tamil Nadu.

"To avert the present danger to Tamil Nadu, everyone who is Tamil must unite, whatever their flag, so long as their people are Tamil, their lineage Tamil, and their blood Tamil blood.

"I earnestly ask the people not to doubt the inner integrity of any party leader engaged in this joint endeavour. People say that I am always suspicious; yet it is I who now say that the people need harbour no doubt about the purity of anyone's heart.

"Everyone who attended the meeting that day was filled with devotion to Tamil. All rejoiced: 'We remained apart for so long; at least now we have united. How fortunate that the opportunity arose.' They looked at one another with affection, glad of the opportunity for unity. No one harboured an evil or deceitful thought. Consider, then, the importance and distinction of the alliance.

"When we reflect upon the situation confronting Tamil Nadu, we understand how great a joint endeavour is needed to protect Tamil honour, enrich Tamil life, and set the future right.

"Leaving Tamil Nadu aside, we see great turmoil arising in the northern part of the Indian subcontinent.

"Trains that ordinarily run have stopped; radios that ordinarily sing have fallen silent; Ministers responsible for administration speak in agitation.

"The Marathas have risen in fury against the injustice inflicted upon the land of Maharashtra, where the hero Shivaji was born, who placed swords in the hands of all who held ploughs, established camps in caves and tents in forests, waged a mighty struggle to blast apart the Mughal Empire, and founded the Maratha Empire. The injustice has been committed by the arrogant rulers in Delhi, by Marwari rulers, and by Gujarati rulers trembling in fear. The Marathas are fighting rulers who scheme to destroy their future.

"In one place a prison has been reduced to rubble and the prisoners released. As far as I have read, prisons have been broken open only twice in history. One was the Bastille in France, a chamber of cruelty: a young man who entered it would return an old man, while one who entered as an old man would be sent out only as a corpse, prey for the vultures. A prison was also attacked during the Russian Revolution. Yet now, in the days in which we live and before our very eyes, a prison has been demolished. It was not a large prison; the people's effort was not immense; nor did enormous crowds gather. Nevertheless, the prison was attacked and the inmates released.

"Elsewhere people went in search of the Chief Minister, crying, 'Where is the Chief Minister? Where is that frog who does not know how to sustain the people?' They gathered at his doorway. The Minister's wife came outside and saw the great crowd at the entrance. 'What do you want?' she asked. 'Why has this crowd assembled?' They cried, 'Look here, madam, at the blood flowing from this man's head. Look there at his severed arm. It is under your husband's rule, madam, that the police have committed this cruelty.' Hearing the matter, the Minister's wife wrung her hands, wiped her eyes, and said with the privilege of a wife, 'This is indeed an atrocity. Let him come home; I shall ask him about it.'

"In another place, the people cornered an official and demanded his signature upon a paper they had prepared. Formerly, people would make way when an official arrived and bow in reverence. They knew that an official's order could cost them their life or property. Yet when an official arrived in his car, a great crowd blocked his path, obtained his signature, and then sent him on his way. The people knew that the official's signature alone was insufficient. The official likewise knew that his signature alone could not make the people's demand happen. Nevertheless, the people felt the need to make someone yield to something.

"In Bombay, it is said, a person wearing khadi could not walk the streets; anyone wearing a cap was in danger; portraits of Gandhi had to be placed somewhere safe. Even the Pandit's portrait was reportedly in grave danger. It is indeed a pitiable event!"

"All the other State Ministers are engaged in political activity concerning the reorganisation of their States.

"Having cultivated hostility with every party in Tamil Nadu, whom will Pandit Nehru trust as he travels through this land? Upon whom will he rely to maintain his authority? On Kamaraj and Kakkan?

"How long can Pandit Nehru succeed by employing the divide-and-rule stratagem left behind by the British? If the alliance formed in Tamil Nadu works effectively for one year, it can make the arrogant Pandit, roaming upon the mountaintop, descend and recognise justice.

"Kamaraj has asked, 'Have you learned no sense even after seeing Bombay?' I wish to tell him, 'Bombay has not frightened us.'

"If we alone launched major struggles against the injustice inflicted upon Tamil Nadu, people would ask: 'They have educated and mature students, students in the process of becoming educated and mature, and members of the middle classes. But do they have factory workers? Do they have the great farming population?'

"If the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam launched a struggle, they would find fault by saying, 'Do they not have only those who have forgotten the consciousness of being Dravidian and think of themselves as Tamil?'

"If only the Communists entered the struggle, they would say, 'They have exhausted working people and farmers wasting away with sunken eyes. But do they have anyone who knows geography? Do they have anyone who has studied history?'

"Likewise, whichever other party began the struggle, people would find some fault.

"But now? All the parties are enclosed within the same fold. Everyone will be responsible for the victory or defeat produced by the struggle. Such a great and rare formation has never before arisen in Tamil Nadu. This is the first time that excellent leaders and remarkable commanders have gathered together.

"During the last general election, the Dravidar Kazhagam formed an alliance with the Communists. At that time, all the others did not unite in that formation; they could not unite.

"But now, everyone who has influence in the people's forum, everyone who maintains an inseparable relationship with the people, and everyone who has both the opportunity and facilities to serve the people, as well as the attachment and desire to do so, has united. They have resolved to work together.

"Each person must take pride in belonging to this alliance and regard it as a great blessing received in life.

"From this very moment, from today onward, everyone must begin working to make the general strike on 20 February a success.

"On 20 February, there must be a single stillness everywhere, throughout the country. Factories must not open. Shops must remain closed; even eating houses must be shut. All means of transport must stand still. All of this must take place peacefully, without the slightest violence.

"DMK comrades should be able to say, 'Victory came because the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam worked with us.' Members of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam should say, 'Victory became complete because the DMK joined.' The Socialists should say, 'Victory became fragrant because the Communists took part in the alliance,' while the Communists should say, 'Victory was beautiful because of the Socialists.' In this manner, enabling each to praise the other and speak words of affection to one another, everyone must stand united and work during the general strike on 20 February.

"DMK comrades in every district must immediately shake hands with members of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam. This is not to test one another's strength; each already knows their own strength. They must shake hands with sincere affection.

"When a member of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam approaches a shopkeeper and asks him to close his shop on 20 February, the shopkeeper should be able to reply, 'A DMK comrade told me just a little while ago.' When a DMK member approaches him, he should say, 'A member of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam told me yesterday.' Or he should be able to say, 'The Communists told me even before either of you.' With the good objective that Devikulam and Peermedu must belong to the Tamils as their goal, comrades must compete with one another in their work.

"Some may ask, 'If the general strike succeeds in this manner, will that secure Devikulam and Peermedu?' What is there that people in this country do not ask? Even Minister Subramaniam asked, 'Will my resignation secure Devikulam and Peermedu?' It is not that Devikulam and Peermedu will be secured merely because Minister Subramaniam resigns. But will he not be removed? How much good would that do the country?

"The purpose of the general strike is to make the Government in Delhi understand that the Tamils will not give up the taluks of Devikulam and Peermedu.

"I submit that if all parties unite and act with discipline, we shall succeed in securing the union of Devikulam and Peermedu with Tamil Nadu.

"Having made enemies of every party in this country, whom does Pandit Nehru trust to govern it? Whom will he trust as he travels through it? How will he maintain his authority? By trusting Kamaraj and Kakkan? Some may say that Kamaraj is the Chief Minister and Kakkan the Tamil Nadu Congress leader. But we knew them even before they assumed those offices; we know them now; and we know what they will become and how they will be after leaving those offices."

"If this alliance works effectively for one year, it can make the arrogant Pandit Nehru, who roams upon the mountaintop, descend.

"Kamaraj is reported to have said at a place called Chengam, 'Some opposition parties have joined together and announced that they are going to conduct a general strike on 20 February. Have they learned no sense even after seeing Bombay?' I tell him, 'Bombay has not frightened us.'

"What does it mean to ask, 'Have you learned no sense even after seeing Bombay?' Have the comrades of the Dravidar Kazhagam considered it?

"The police shot down the people who rose in Bombay. Many were killed; hundreds were grievously injured; countless people were imprisoned and subjected to cruelty. Is Kamaraj saying that he intends to do the same here?

"We possess only one life. Let them take it if they wish and have the courage. That is what we seek to say!

"Demonstrations like those in Bombay will not take place here; the agitation will proceed peacefully. Nevertheless, if Kamaraj intends to practise the tyranny of the gun as in Bombay, let him. Let bloodstained history record that, under a Government with a Tamil as Chief Minister, as many as one hundred Tamils were shot dead for fighting for their rights. Upon whom would that disgrace fall?

"Is it not better to die in a single day without fear or servitude than to die over many days in fear and subjection?

"Kamaraj asks us, 'Have you learned no sense after seeing Bombay?' We do not feel inclined to ask him, 'Do you speak like this even after seeing France?' because Kamaraj does not know that history.

"A great crowd must not gather at the entrance of a shop shouting, 'Close it, close it,' while the shopkeeper mutters feebly, 'I cannot, I cannot'; nor, amid that commotion, must someone enter the shop and lift away the cash box while the shopkeeper devotes his attention to protecting it, followed by further undesirable events. That is not what we wish to see on 20 February.

"A single stillness must prevail throughout the country on 20 February. We desire a condition in which 'there is not the slightest disturbance anywhere and no violent act of any kind.'

"Do you know what kind of stillness must prevail across the country that day?

"An English film was recently released. Those who saw it will understand. The king responsible for ruling a country is a tyrant, and its people suffer and endure hardship under his cruelty. A woman called Lady Godiva sees this and goes to the king. 'Why do you torment the people in this manner, O King? What wrong have they committed?' she asks, demanding that he abandon his tyranny.

"The king replies, 'If you ride naked upon a horse and circle the city, I shall give up my tyranny.' Lady Godiva agrees.

"On the first day, the theatre showing the film overflowed with people, for they expected to see a nude scene.

"The next day, the theatre was empty, because those who saw the film had wept.

"Lady Godiva rode naked around the city on horseback, her long black hair covering what needed to be covered. Yet no one in the city watched her. Everyone shut themselves inside their homes. Not a single door or window was open. There was complete stillness. Students hid their faces behind their books; men bolted their doors and remained inside; women did not leave their hearths.

"One mischievous fellow alone wondered, 'What exactly is happening? What does Lady Godiva look like as she rides naked? Let me see.' He opened the window a little and peered out. Another man seared his eyes with a firebrand. Although that other man too might have wished to see Godiva riding naked, he did not. The town remained immersed in complete stillness.

"We desire that such stillness should prevail throughout the country on 20 February.

"If the Ministers who govern the country come out that day to look at it, the general strike must leave the land so still that they wonder: 'Are we ruling a country or a burial ground? Are all the people still with us? Are they moving about the country, or have they all been imprisoned?'

"I do not mean that, just as the eyes of the wicked man who tried to see Lady Godiva naked were pierced with a firebrand, you must pierce the eyes of the Ministers when they come to see the general strike on 20 February. It is enough to remain peaceful.

"If the Ministers possess a strong sense of honour, let them see that spectacle and run to Pandit Nehru, whom they regard as Mount Meru, and plead: 'The people of our land are enraged by the injustice done to them. They have raised their voices in condemnation and are observing a general strike. Therefore, remove the injustice.'

"If, instead, they possess even a little self-respect, let them resign their offices.

"In a democratic country, a tremendous upheaval need not arise to bring disgrace and blame upon a Government. Government offices need not be attacked, officials beaten, acid thrown in Ministers' faces, or shops looted.

"In democratic countries such as England, a general strike that makes the country look like a cremation ground is considered sufficient to bring disgrace and blame upon a Government.

"Although our country has not attained that high condition, such general strikes must at least make the Government understand that all the people stand united on a particular issue.

"From this very moment, you must prepare the people of the country for the general strike on 20 February.

"If this alliance of all parties continues to work for one year, it can certainly bring the arrogant Pandit Nehru down from the mountaintop.

"What does Pandit Nehru possess other than the divide-and-rule stratagem left behind by the British?

"The British employed this very strategy: 'If the Sikhs oppose us, the Punjabis will support us; if the Punjabis oppose us, the Bengalis will support us; if the Bengalis oppose us, the Marathas will support us; if the Marathas oppose us, the Muslims will support us.'"

"Today the Pandit likewise employs divide and rule: 'If the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam opposes us, the Communists will support us; if the Communists oppose us, the Praja Socialists will support us.' But what of the present? Not even the weakest person within the alliance will abandon Tamil and stand on Nehru's side.

"Will M. P. S. abandon Tamil and go over to Nehru's side?

"Will Jeevanandam abandon the people and go over, even if Bulganin comes and leaves?

"The country knows well that we have never gone over and never will.

"Who, then, will go?

"Some may ask, 'All that is well, but are Devikulam and Peermedu important enough to warrant the formation of such a great alliance and so immense an effort?'

"If a farmer sees a tiger seize and run away with a lamb that belongs to him, he will not remain idle, either saying, 'It is only a small lamb that is being carried away,' or shrinking in fear because it is a tiger that carries it.

"To recover his lamb from the tiger, he takes whatever is at hand, runs faster than the tiger, attacks it, and fights.

"But Devikulam and Peermedu are no lamb. They are taluks spread across mountain ranges rising three to four thousand feet. They are plantation forests covered everywhere in a single expanse of green, lush and verdant as though green silk had been unfurled. Such are Devikulam and Peermedu.

"When I say mountains, I do not mean hills like Pallavaram Hill, St. Thomas Mount, or Tiruvannamalai.

"Tea, rubber, cardamom, clove, pepper, plantain, areca, and coconut grow there. It is a land overflowing with wealth. One can see little streams singing *Sindhu* melodies, see elephants trumpeting, and hear insects humming.

"One can also feel the sighs of exhausted working people.

"Some people imagine that 'the wealth of inhabited land is prosperous, but forest wealth is nothing of the kind.'

"Forest wealth is like the wealth in Alagappa Chettiar's iron safe: it can neither be seen nor touched. The wealth of inhabited land is like the loose change in our pocket; if the pocket has a hole, it slips away.

"They say India needs dollars. Devikulam and Peermedu are fertile mountain regions where the crops that earn those dollars are grown. It was the Tamil who raised the tea and rubber plantations that stand in these mountains today.

"It was the Tamil who enriched that mountain region when it was nothing but scrub and forest.

"The story of how the white man recruited labour to clear the forests and convert them into plantations is a tragic one.

"They are said to have tied ten or twenty sovereigns into bundles and thrown them into the mountains, announcing that those who entered the forest and retrieved them could keep the sovereigns.

"If ten Tamils went in, nine would become corpses. Only one would cross those bodies and obtain the bundle of sovereigns. As all ten climbed the mountain, they cleared the forest along their path. The white man used this device for the very purpose of clearing the forests. Thus, sacrificing their lives, shedding their blood, and labouring, the Tamils converted those mountain regions into prosperous plantation forests rich in tea and rubber.

"These very regions of Devikulam and Peermedu are now about to be lost by Tamil Nadu because those in power do not know how to calculate their value, do not know how to argue for them, and lack the backbone to resist injustice."

"Swindlers intend to seize them, and the Tamils are about to be made fools of. That is why we call: 'Tamils who have risen! Tamils who have awakened! Unite to protect the soil our ancestors laboured to develop and to prevent it from being taken away.'

"Nationalist comrades may ask, 'If that is so, is this struggle motivated by profit?'

"Even if Devikulam and Peermedu were not fertile mountains but an arid desert, could we surrender our right to them for that reason?

"If someone carries off a mother's child, whether he is a drunkard, a madman, or a brute, will she not panic and struggle? Will she not strive to recover the stolen child? In the same way, Tamil Nadu has now set forth like a mother running to save her abducted child, determined to recover Devikulam and Peermedu.

"The Madras Ministers, including the Chief Minister, say, 'What difference does it make whether Devikulam and Peermedu are here or there? What difference does it make whether they are with us or with them?'

"Is this how people bearing responsibility speak?

"Mendicants and *pandaram* ascetics may speak so: 'What difference does it make whether this vessel is with me or with him? What difference does it make whether I sleep in a monastery or in an open field?' But those responsible for government speak in this manner. What are we to think of them? In which group shall we place them, that of the mendicants and *pandarams*?

"Let them show us in Maharashtra a single lump of flesh in human form that wanders about saying, 'What difference does it make whether Bombay belongs to the Marathas or is somewhere else?' Let them show us one Maratha who speaks so. We do not see honourable Marathas speaking in that way. Yet our Ministers remain here saying, 'What difference does it make who possesses Devikulam and Peermedu?'

"One is almost tempted to think that it would be better if these esteemed Ministers simply kept their mouths shut!

"Even a dog understands the value of soil. A dog that defecates wherever it happens to be is a rabid dog. A sensible dog first sniffs the earth and defecates there only if the soil suits it.

"Why, then, have the leaders of the country failed to understand the value of the soil and the wealth of the Devikulam and Peermedu taluks?

"Today only the Ministers remain without feeling for Devikulam and Peermedu. Hearing us speak, even the cows and calves at home cry 'Amma, Amma,' as if declaring, 'Devikulam and Peermedu belong to the Tamils.' Those terrifying forests were transformed into productive tea, rubber, cardamom, and clove plantations, not by magic or incantation, but through Tamil blood, sweat, and labour.

"The Madras Ministers say 'what if they are lost?' about those excellent regions our ancestors cultivated and improved.

"Let Devikulam and Peermedu be deserts if they must. When the question is one of right, what elaborate argument is needed to say, 'My country belongs to me'? Does that not require a human heart? Does it not require patriotism and love of language? Does it not require knowledge of democratic principles?

"Are arguments what one needs to say, 'I want my country'?

"That is why all the opposition parties have united and formed a battlefront to protect the borders of Tamil Nadu. The country has assumed a posture of struggle.

"Kamaraj is now reported to have said in the town of Chengam, 'All the opposition parties are joining together to conduct a struggle. Why should they not come and tell me?'

"I told you once about a small matter. What became of it? When I spoke about Kallakudi, did I not mention it to you? What was the result? Were you able to change the name?

"All the parties have united because they understand that Kamaraj is incapable of securing the union of Devikulam and Peermedu with Tamil Nadu. We have realised that Kamaraj lacks the patriotism required to accomplish that task.

"Does he expect P. T. Rajan, who once ruled the country, to come and see him? Kamaraj himself cannot claim that none of the united party leaders possesses the qualifications needed to sit where he sits and perform that work. Yet all of them are apparently expected to unite and bear a *kavadi* before Kamaraj! Good heavens, when did he become so great a man?

"Therefore, Kamaraj is not the person whom the people of the country must regard as important. What demands attention is the Devikulam issue, not what Kamaraj says.

"The problem now before us is this: in the struggle to be held on 20 February, we must unite in spirit. Though we appear as many in form, the Tamil gathering is a flood. We must make Nehru, who sits in the intoxication of power, swollen with arrogance and dispensing injustice, and our Ministers who hold up his feet, recognise and understand our demand."

## Translator's Notes

`Vengai` retains the Tamil name of the valuable native tree named in the source. `Kazhagam` is retained in organisational names where the period political identity is clearer than a generic rendering such as "association."

`Gramaniar` is retained as the honorific/community designation printed with M. P. Sivagnanam's name. `Navagrahas` refers to the nine planetary deities traditionally arranged in a temple. `Kanakan and Vijayan` preserves the names in Anna's retelling of the Cheran Senguttuvan episode.

Anna's Lady Godiva reference is translated as the political analogy presented in the source. `Mount Meru` renders `மகாமேரு`, the exalted mountain image he uses sarcastically for Nehru.

`M. P. S.` reflects Anna's abbreviated reference to M. P. Sivagnanam. `Bulganin` names Soviet leader Nikolai Bulganin as printed. `Sindhu` is retained as the named Tamil musical/poetic mode in the source. `Sovereigns` renders the period currency term `பவுன்`.

`Pandaram` is retained as the source's period term for a class of religious mendicants. `Kavadi` preserves Anna's image of ceremonially bearing a burden in supplication; it is not reduced to the generic word "petition."

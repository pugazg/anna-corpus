## English Translation

The following is a speech delivered by Arignar Anna at a public meeting in 1964, after his release from prison.

Beloved chairman and friends!

I am truly delighted to have the opportunity to take part in this joyful celebration, where comrades returning from prison are accorded a splendid welcome with a great procession.

But instead of joy overflowing in our hearts, the death of Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru has caused profound sorrow and grief and plunged us into anguish.

I conveyed my condolences on Nehru's death while I was still in prison. Nevertheless, this is the first meeting in which I have participated since coming out. Through it, as one who bears responsibility, I offer my share of grief for the great loss suffered by the country and my respects on the death of a valued democrat.

Pandit Nehru was not merely India's peerless leader; he also stood as a leader who expounded noble ideas to the whole world.

I am proud to say that the fine democratic qualities he possessed encouraged our own democratic ideas and methods.

At this moment some Congressmen may wonder why people who do not belong to Congress, and who oppose it, express condolences on Pandit Nehru's death. I shall tell them one thing.

Members of the DMK did not profit from Pandit Nehru. His name was used to injure the growing strength of the DMK; neither the movement nor any of us belonging to it obtained the slightest personal or political benefit from him.

Yet there are many reasons why the DMK is deeply distressed by Pandit Jawaharlal's sorrowful death. It is not merely that a good man who respected democratic awakening has died. His death is also a personal loss to us because he was the object of one firm hope held by the DMK.

I trust that you remember some of the ideas I expressed when we began the righteous struggle against Hindi.

When friends asked why we intended to continue the anti-Hindi struggle until 1965, I explained the reason through the press and at public meetings. When the Delhi government decided the official-language question in 1965, I said, the DMK's prolonged anti-Hindi struggle would create a new awakening in Pandit Nehru's mind and produce the change we desired.

I had that confidence because Pandit Nehru was a good democrat who could understand the pure purpose of the struggle. But the man in whom we placed that hope died unexpectedly. The DMK is therefore bound to convey its profound condolences and sorrow on his death, regarding it as a loss suffered along our own path. Though we have already expressed these sentiments at a separate meeting, I convey my personal condolences as well.

Friends Mathiazhagan and Anbazhagan spoke here about prison life and its cruelties. I do not wish to say more about them.

While speaking, Anbazhagan mentioned the pain in my shoulder. You need not hear him and fear that Anna is suffering severely. The pain is confined to one shoulder; no one need distress themselves by imagining that it is so great that I cannot raise my arm.

Nor should the opposition miscalculate that Annadurai has lost the use of an arm.

As Anbazhagan said, give me a month of rest. Within that time I shall obtain the best medical treatment and cure the ailment.

If you ask how prison was for us, it was a prison. We did not enter it expecting much, nor did we grumble that they inflicted so many hardships upon us.

What caused me and our dear friends sorrow and pain was not prison suffering. It was that we could not see you, filled with eagerness and enthusiasm; could not delight in your radiant eyes; and were shut away from your stirring voices and majestic stride.

Whatever fatigue or illness afflicts us, you who sit before us are the physicians who cure it.

Your gentle smiles remove our suffering.

Your enthusiasm dispels our weariness.

Your radiant eyes drive away our illness.

I have therefore come here in search of the physicians who will cure me. I say with confidence and happiness that the joy of seeing you will remove my pain and soon restore me to full health.

The greatest suffering of the past six months was my inability to see you.

For those engaged in public life, prison is not a place filled with fear. A person who regrets not receiving customary food or wearing customary clothes is not truly engaged in public life. The greater cruelty is being separated from the public among whom one has lived and being unable to see them.

That was the suffering I experienced, and I rejoice that it has ended today.

Navalar described the hardships our friends and I endured in prison. Next month he himself is to take part in the righteous struggle against Hindi and go to prison. Navalar and the others go there fully aware that these hardships exist.

Very few in our movement have not been imprisoned. Many of our comrades have been seasoned by taking part in struggles conducted by the movement, including the three-front agitation and the agitation to reduce prices.

Not only they but their wives too have become so accustomed to their frequent imprisonment that they no longer weep or worry when their husbands go to prison, but regard it as a routine occurrence.

If our comrades now remain at home for a year or two without going to prison, their wives may well ask, "Why have you been idle for two years? Have you held no agitation? Have you not gone to prison?"

Such maturity is essential to a liberation movement, and I rejoice in it.

You heard those who returned from prison speak on this platform. If someone asks what benefit came from their imprisonment, no lengthy explanation is needed. The heroic fervour visible when they spoke here is answer enough.

Confinement did not change the minds of Mathiazhagan and Anbazhagan. Their enthusiasm did not diminish.

Those imprisoned for work that must be done for the country do not lose enthusiasm; it increases tenfold. The resolve they possessed before prison increases a thousandfold after they return.

I believe it would be more accurate here to say genuine Congressmen rather than merely people who happen to be in Congress. Congressmen will understand this. I do not expect everyone now inside Congress to understand it, for today Congress does not contain Congressmen alone. People in Congress are one thing and Congressmen another. Only true Congressmen will understand what I say.

I wish to ask those Congressmen: when you were confined and tortured in Alipur Jail, did you not endure it without losing heart, without departing even slightly from the ideal of freedom, and emerge determined that white imperialism must end?

Likewise, we who were imprisoned endured hardship and emerged without softening, resolved that Hindi imperialism must end. Prison has hardened our hearts against fear. Its sufferings have become familiar.

When a political movement loses its fear of prison, regards imprisonment as ordinary, and gains firmness, it reaches a stage extremely dangerous to political profit-seekers.

Because we go to prison so often, fear has vanished not only from us but from those already inside. They think, "I too am merely coming to the place these people regularly visit." That is the dangerous stage I mean.

I believe the law must be respected. I am not one who thinks every issue demands breaking it.

But by responding to everything with prison and punishment, these rulers degrade the law until people cease respecting it and regard its penalties lightly.

That is undesirable and must be removed. If the people are to respect law, rulers must use it as a shield, not as a sword for destroying opponents.

Recent events are enough to show how today's government respects law. Karunanidhi announced, not only outside but within the Legislative Assembly, that he would burn the language provision of the Constitution at Thanjavur. N. V. Natarajan was to burn it in Madras in 1965. Was arresting them during the constitutional-burning agitation at Madurai an act of respect for law? What is the secret behind arresting people who had gone merely to preserve the discipline of the agitation and maintain peace?

No one should mistakenly imagine that Hindi alone causes me to oppose Congress.

I am not a mendicant driven by famine but one born into a hereditary calling. My opposition did not begin today or yesterday. For many years I have had one thousand and eight sound reasons besides Hindi for opposing Congress.

It does no credit to mature democracy that a single party has ruled this country for seventeen years.

Prolonged one-party rule causes many harms. It makes a party lose the heart that listens to popular feeling and leads it toward authoritarian conduct.

Ayub Khan rules next door. Though democracy is spoken of there, dictatorship sits concealed behind it.

If one person alone must rule the country and one party alone must operate the government, democracy may be discussed as theory but will never enter practice.

Taxes will continually be imposed in the confidence that there is no opposition and the rulers alone command. Cruelties will continue in the assurance that no one can remove them from office.

I will never accept this. I hold that democracy means also entrusting governing responsibility to opposition parties and allowing the people to judge changed views and new ideas. As an opposition man, as one who respects democracy, and as a responsible member of a great movement that has won the confidence of Tamil hearts, I oppose Congress's one-party rule.

The purpose of the righteous struggle against Hindi is to remove imposed Hindi. For that to happen, the arrogance of the rulers must be reduced. If we labour well in 1967, I believe our struggle and work will yield a good result.

Those who ask what the DMK would accomplish in office should read the election manifesto it announced at the Madurai conference.

Private ownership continues to grow, but where does the capital invested in public ownership go? The country's economy deteriorates because economics is not understood.

Where have the results of seventeen years of rule gone? Do the Five-Year Plans yield benefit or not? If they do, where has that benefit become immobilised? A committee is appointed to discover it.

Unable even to understand what the Mahalanobis Committee report says, people suffer the misfortune of repeatedly turning its pages to discover whether the cause of the deterioration appears on page ninety-two or page one hundred.

Pandit Nehru at least possessed the courage and democratic disposition to appoint a committee to investigate the results of his government.

Unlike other Congressmen who dismissively declare that Congress rule has accumulated benefits, filled the country with progress, built dams and constructed factories, Pandit Nehru wished to examine the actual results. We are obliged to honour this democratic quality.

Just as the DMK opposed Nehru many times, it also praised him many times.

I am not trained in the extreme habit of treating an opponent lightly and speaking of him with contempt.

I am moderate in every matter. Even the shirt I wear and the food I eat are mild. I generally wear a white shirt. When I wear a coloured one, its colour is pale; a strongly coloured shirt becomes pale after two washes.

It is appropriate to mention a friend at this point. If you learn how far he has moved from a once extremely vigorous position, you will understand how even "fast colours" fade.

At the Virudhunagar conference, that friend said, though I did not participate in that debate, "A human being can progress only after completely casting the feeling of God out of his heart."

Today that same friend is in Durban, South Africa, proclaiming the greatness of the *Tiruvacakam* and the glory of God.

I name him in the confidence that he will not be angry with me: he is my valued friend Tiruvacakamani K. M. Balasubramaniam. I could name several others. The fervour they displayed and the speeches they delivered have disappeared somewhere; they have changed in one way or another. I do not wish to name them.

Though I often opposed Pandit Nehru's political decisions, I also repeatedly praised his democratic qualities.

I wish to recount one example I personally witnessed.

When ministers come to Parliament, most hand the files they must bring to their attendants and enter with heads held high. As they arrive they offer greetings, pat friends on the back to recruit them into their faction, and seem to ask with each pat whether the person remains their friend or belongs to the rival faction, and whether he will support or oppose them at the next election. I have seen them stride in with puffed chests and ostentatious bearing.

Pandit Nehru was different. When he stepped out of his car, many people ran toward him. One opened the car door; another greeted him with folded hands; others offered to carry what he held.

Despite all this reception and respect, Nehru never handed his file to anyone. He carried it himself, paid no attention to who saluted him or withheld respect, walked rapidly with bowed head, and sat in his chair.

Pandit Nehru was the only minister who went directly to his place, mindful that speaking to one person nearby might cause hurt or resentment in others.

I shall offer another example of the respect and welcome he gave democracy.

It occurred during the war with China. Vajpayee of the Jana Sangh asked, "How many thousand Indian troops are on the Ladakh front?"

No responsible minister would answer. Ministers know that disclosing military secrets is not statesmanship. Opposition parties nevertheless ask such questions to trouble the ruling party, knowing there will be no reply.

Had Nehru answered, he would not have been a good Prime Minister. Yet he did not respond like other ministers by saying, "I do not wish to state it here," or "Military secrets must not be revealed," nor did he merely spread his hands while seated to indicate that he could not answer.

When Vajpayee asked, Nehru thought for a moment and turned over several papers on his desk as though searching for something.

My seat was just in front of the ministers, so I could see what lay on their desks. I saw what Pandit Nehru was searching: there were only blank sheets on his desk. Yet he spent five minutes appearing to search.

Then he answered Vajpayee: "I have not brought the answer with me."

Vajpayee immediately said, "The Prime Minister should not come to Parliament without bringing the answer."

Nehru heard him, smiled, and remained silent.

Like other ministers, Nehru could have said, "I cannot answer." But asking a question is a democratic right. Without wishing to deny that right, he behaved both as one who allowed it and as a responsible Prime Minister.

About ten days ago, a letter appeared in the *Mail*. Many may not have read it. It was written by our friend Rengasamy, who writes in that newspaper under the name "Pertinax."

One statement in it is entirely true: "Among Congressmen who wholeheartedly welcomed and rejoiced at the DMK's abandonment of the demand for separation, no one could equal Pandit Nehru."

I too can understand this truth. I wish to illustrate it with an incident in the Rajya Sabha.

I said there, "India is not one nation. It is a subcontinent containing many peoples."

Pandit Nehru replied, "I agree that India is not one nation. But we must try to make it one."

This shows his magnanimity. When we voiced the same idea here, people asked, "Ah! What do these youngsters know?" Pandit Nehru did not say that. He acknowledged the point.

The death of such a good democrat is the death of a great man capable of understanding the strength of our struggle.

Even so, I believe those who have announced that they will follow his path will understand at least to some extent the awakening produced by the DMK's struggle and the anguish in the hearts of Tamil people.

At this moment I am bound to make an appeal to the parties of Tamil Nadu.

If you truly oppose Hindi, show it openly. If you stand aside because doing so would strengthen the DMK's agitation, you commit a great betrayal of the country.

Our friends here answered the observations made by ministers and opponents concerning the anti-Hindi struggle. They explained that it will not wither, falter, or be stopped; it has become inseparable from popular feeling and cannot perish.

Sir, you say the anti-Hindi struggle has withered away. Let us assume it has. Who suffers the loss?

Is the anti-Hindi struggle only for Anbazhagan's children? Will the children of Congress leaders not be affected by Hindi imposition? Please recognise this danger.

Do not sink so low that in the future one child can say, "My grandfather took part in the anti-Hindi struggle," while another of your grandchildren must say, "My grandfather slipped away to the other side."

Our struggle is not for ourselves alone. It is conducted for the future welfare of Tamil Nadu and all non-Hindi-speaking states.

I earnestly ask those who support it to come forward openly.

I am next obliged to thank our friendly allies, the Swatantra Party and the Muslim League.

United in opposition to Congress, let us strengthen our front further.

A five-rupee note has no value if torn into separate pieces. Likewise, only Congress profits when opposition votes are scattered and wasted.

Friend Mathiazhagan mentioned the view Acharya expressed while I was in prison.

He said that the seat of power must change hands in 1967, for otherwise there would be no deliverance from the sufferings that continually bite at us.

He also said, "The DMK has attained the standing of a party fit to govern," and expressed the desire that it be placed in office.

With the conviction that the DMK will attain that qualification in 1967 in accordance with the confidence it enjoys among the people, I thank the friends who welcomed us and all of you assembled here. I take my leave. Vanakkam.

## Translator's Notes

`அறப்போர்` is rendered as “righteous struggle”: in this political context it denotes a principled, nonviolent agitation rather than literal warfare.

Anna distinguishes genuine `காங்கிரஸ்காரர்கள்`, veterans formed by the freedom movement, from people merely occupying positions inside the contemporary Congress organisation. The repetition is retained because it drives his argument.

`பஞ்சத்துக்கு ஆண்டி அல்ல, பரம்பரை ஆண்டி` is a proverbial contrast between one forced into mendicancy by famine and one belonging to a hereditary order. Anna uses it humorously to say that his Congress opposition is longstanding, not opportunistic.

The Mahalanobis Committee reference concerns official investigation of the distribution and concentration of income and wealth during the planning era. The source calls `மெயில்` a newspaper and identifies its correspondent by the pen name “Pertinax”; those forms are retained conservatively.

`ஆச்சாரியார்` refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji); `நாவலர்` here refers to V. R. Nedunchezhiyan. `திருவாசகமணி` is K. M. Balasubramaniam's literary honorific. `வணக்கம்` is retained at the close because it is the speaker's formal leave-taking.

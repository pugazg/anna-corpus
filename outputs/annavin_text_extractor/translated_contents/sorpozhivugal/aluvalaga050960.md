## English Translation

Speech No. 40, 5 September 1960

**C. N. Annadurai:** Mr Speaker, although the language question has been debated once or twice before in our Legislative Assembly, the exciting events that have now occurred, their repercussions in the country, the political controversies that have arisen, and their spread through the public mind make it appropriate for the Assembly to consider the question now.

I feared until the last moment that we might be denied permission to take part in so appropriate a debate. This morning, however, the Finance Minister said, "I welcome their participation." Yet he spoke as though expressing our view here might indicate some alteration in our policy of separation or support for a united India. I gratefully welcome and appreciate his new concern for our party.

We possess enough ability to express the views of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam without injuring our fundamental principles. If that ability should ever fail us, I ask the Finance Minister, though he belongs to another party, to tell us as a friend that we are wrong and thereby strengthen our party. *(Laughter.)* At least when speaking upon this language question, everyone should speak without concealment.

We must examine over many years the actions of the Congress, now the ruling party, and genuinely examine the concern shown by our parties in the Dravidian movement. Long before Congress became the ruling party, its leaders entered Hindi propaganda, declared Hindi the national language and insisted that making it India's common language would be the first task after self-government. Throughout that period the Tamil and Dravidian movements consistently opposed Hindi.

Congress too has shown consistency over twenty-five years, though its position has changed from time to time according to the opposition it encountered. No one should think I use "change" as an accusation. Change is a sign of growth. I cite it as evidence that they have learned about the world.

After Congress became the ruling party, the Hindi question was considered by two or three bodies: the Constituent Assembly; the Congress Working Committee; and the Hindi Sahitya Sammelan. I ask the Finance Minister to recall what was said in the Constituent Assembly, of which he was a member. I eagerly studied its record, not only because it contains interesting matters but because I wanted to discover what he had said about the use of Hindi.

I was disappointed. When this difficult question came up and members stated their positions clearly, the Finance Minister offered no position. One might say it would be unfair to accuse him of slipping away, but an important page in the history of the language question remained unwritten. Apart from an explanation in the middle of the proceedings, there is nothing showing what he said about putting a policy into practice.

T. A. Ramalingam Chettiar of Coimbatore district and Dr P. Subbarayan, who was once Chief Minister here and is now a minister in the Government of India, spoke. I was deeply disappointed that our Finance Minister did not. *(Interruption.)* I said he did not speak with distinction. The member for Lalgudi says he had no chance; he need not offer that defence.

At the debate approximately 300 amendments were submitted to the official resolution moved by Gopalaswami Ayyangar. They were not considered one by one, as in our Assembly. All were taken together and members expressed whatever views they wished, speaking also upon their own resolutions. The result was confusion. The enthusiasm, care and skill demanded by the question of which language should be the official language of the Indian subcontinent were not displayed. The Finance Minister will realise this even if he reads the record now. I have mentioned it once before in this Assembly.

Those who assembled there had won the title of liberators because the subcontinent had become free, and believed responsibility for governing it had been entrusted to them. While exchanging affectionate counsel, they failed to consider the future. Their decision to make Hindi the official language, embodied in Part XVII of the Constitution, was made in that state of mind.

Until that constitutional provision is properly amended, examined from a new perspective and adapted to changing times, these complications, disputes and confusions will repeatedly raise their heads. Tamil conduct may not yet have reached the condition seen in Assam, and there is no need now to ask whether it will remain confined to debate. When linguistic fanaticism and domination create turmoil, no one can stop every consequence. Undesirable results will certainly occur.

The decision that Hindi should be India's official language was therefore not reached through sound discussion under proper circumstances. Many members of the Constituent Assembly itself said so. One stated plainly that the 315 people gathered there need not devise the answer and that it should be left to the people of the country.

Although many continued the debate, a closure motion was eventually moved on the ground that enough had been said. When another member asked that it continue a little longer, our Finance Minister reportedly rose and said the question was unnecessary then, that a new amendment would come and the matter could be raised at that time. The President accepted this, allowed closure and put only one or two amendments to a vote.

When those amendments were declared defeated, T. A. Ramalingam Chettiar demanded a vote upon his amendment. Six hands were raised. The Constituent Assembly record does not say whether our Finance Minister's hand was among them. If it was, it was a good hand for Tamil Nadu. If it was not, his hand truly failed to serve us. *(Laughter.)*

Numerous amendments were defeated in this manner. The demand that Part XVII of the Constitution be wholly revised is not the DMK's alone; many across the Indian subcontinent share it.

The argument that only those who desire a united India may consider this issue, while others have no right to speak, is mistaken and inappropriate. India has attained self-government. Before it did, Congress conducted satyagraha and said that after freedom it would abolish the salt tax. Under British rule it conducted the Salt Satyagraha and called for the expulsion of British imperialists. The British imperialists were expelled. National leaders, or their second generation, now sit in government. The imperialists have gone, but imperialism remains.

Foreign imperialism has gone and domestic imperialism has arrived. The British handed their imperial authority to others and departed.

Every citizen has the right to describe present conditions, whether or not that person took part in the freedom struggle and whether or not, after freedom, that person cultivated the ruling party. Every citizen of this subcontinent may speak about its current conditions, laws and inequalities and express personal views. I do not think the Finance Minister will deny this.

I welcome what he said this morning. He stated clearly that continued talk of partition would damage national unity. But seeking separation does not mean that hostility must arise between southerners and northerners. Separation does not necessarily create enmity. Our Prime Minister Nehru travels from our state to Karachi in separated Pakistan. We should consider why.

Congressmen may imagine that by saying this Annadurai raises himself to Pandit Nehru's stature. I do not place myself in so exalted a position. I cite the example only to show that bitterness caused by separation can pass away. There is therefore certainly room to debate this question.

The Indian subcontinent needs an official language of its own. That is necessary, and a decision had to be made after independence. The Constituent Assembly accordingly considered this fundamental issue. Our Finance Minister was present, as were Hindi zealots; I do not think he will deny it.

No one there opposed making Hindi the official language. The controversy concerned numerals: whether Hindi-script numerals or Arabic numerals should be adopted. Many comments and rebuttals concerned that issue. One Hindi zealot said Hindi must be the national language. When another member replied that it was not a national language but an official and administrative language, the zealot declared:

> You may call it official language but I call it national language. You may belong to another nation but I belong to the Indian Nation. You may oppose and walk out but I do not approve of your view.

I do not know how much attention that speech received; the Finance Minister will know. The debate concerned Hindi or Arabic numerals. Hindi itself had already been accepted as official language. A further decision was later reached at the Gauhati conference.

It is said that without one official language the country will lose unity. We must examine history and seek evidence. Switzerland has four official languages, including one spoken by only one person in a hundred, yet its unity has not collapsed. The Finance Minister, who will soon travel abroad, may investigate Switzerland and Canada. Has Canada's unity collapsed because English and French are both official languages? If multilingual administration does not destroy unity elsewhere, why should it do so only here?

A language may lack richness, literature or grammar. Making it official merely because some people speak it would be farcical. Hindi is an Indian language, and Hindi zealots and supporters demand its acceptance.

By "Hindi zealots" I mean those coming from Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and similar regions who insist upon imposing it. By "Hindi supporters" I mean people here who support it, such as Sardar A. Vedarathinam.

When asked what grammar or literary richness Hindi possesses, advocates search and display two epics: Tulsidas's *Ramayana* and the *All India Railway Guide*. *(Laughter.)* These are offered as its finest literature.

**C. Subramaniam:** We may praise our literary wealth as highly as we wish, but I ask that we not lower another language for that purpose.

**C. N. Annadurai:** I do not speak to degrade or humiliate others. I point out that we are being degraded and humiliated. Could our Finance Minister not have asked, even on one day, that Tamil, our mother tongue with two thousand years of life, be made an official language?

Even if his request were rejected, he would possess the consolation of having insisted that Tamil, our mother tongue, become an Indian national official language. Instead, adopting what he perhaps regards as breadth of mind or a Vedantic outlook, he says: "Anyone can make a demand. I will speak only if it can happen. Why speak vainly of something that cannot?"

Two months ago, when the West Bengal Legislative Assembly debated language, Bengali leaders who demanded Bengali as an official language said Tamil too was a very fine language and ought to be accepted. I do not know whether to praise the fact that we hear this from Bengal instead of from a minister of our own Kongu country, or how to praise it.

We do not show the desire to make Tamil an official language; they have shown it. We fear even to say Tamil ought to be one, while they say it must be one. Why do members of our ministry and ruling party fear to say so? The answer lies in their political philosophy.

When people ask why ministers sat listening while speakers like me talked in this manner, the Finance Minister immediately creates an opportunity to reply, "Did I not intervene and say: praise yourselves, but do not insult others?"

Let everything be discussed openly today. Why are you afraid? Do you think no one will support you? We are here to support you. Must you alone remain permanently in office? A section of our party, the Dravidar Kazhagam, already supports you. We too are ready to protect you if you perform this task. But you must not endanger Tamil Nadu's future by bowing to the North. Hindi has neither the qualification nor the necessity to be the official language.

Another question should have been asked this morning. We keep saying "official language," but no clear explanation has been given of what the expression means: for which purposes it will be used and how. Does it mean appearing upon cards and envelopes, being spoken in legislatures, or being used in correspondence with foreign governments? We have no complete explanation.

That absence follows from the Constitution's failure to define administration itself. When state administration, central administration and their shared fields are separated, confusion remains over what belongs to us, what belongs to them and what belongs to both.

When we say "our official language," Finance Minister C. Subramaniam should explain precisely whom it affects. If it concerns only correspondence from the Madras Government to the Central Government or from Madras to Bengal, there is no need to search minutely for words or spend crores inventing terminology. Why do they search so relentlessly? Whatever their underlying purpose in enriching Hindi, northern leaders now lack the courage to declare it. Repeated opposition has produced that fear.

You may say, "We are 150 and you are only fifteen." Yet why has fear arisen among northerners? The claim that Hindi is the national language has retreated so far that people such as Vedarathinam now argue only that it should not be treated below English. The compulsory demand and their grip have weakened, and they know it. Why should our ministry not firmly oppose Hindi at this moment?

The honourable Kalyanasundaram described Rajagopalachari's former and present views. I do not say one must never change a mistaken opinion. Rajagopalachari once said, "Our policy from A to Z is to abolish the Communist Party." Now he says, "My policy from A to Z is to abolish the Congress Party." Whenever something appears bad to him, he follows a policy of removing it and preserving what is good.

Even if we ourselves commit an act, greatness lies in admitting the error when we learn it was harmful. We opposed Hindi when Acharya compulsorily introduced it and still oppose it; whether to praise or condemn his present position is therefore unnecessary for us.

A curious fact comes to mind. Just as Rajagopalachari introduced compulsory Hindi and later opposed it, Prafulla Chandra Ghosh, former Education Minister of West Bengal, now says:

> I have committed a mistake and I admit it.

"I committed an error and acknowledge it," he says. Ministers may err. The greatness required of a ministry lies in admitting and correcting the error. No one need say, "Because I did this, I shall remain with it to the end."

Some friends said Andhra and Kerala do not oppose Hindi as Tamil Nadu does. Yet when we began our agitation, I received letters from people in Andhra saying, "You fought on our behalf too and won; we thank you." Those writers may not possess the status of people seated beside the Finance Minister. They may be ordinary people like me, but people in Andhra carefully watch and appreciate what happens here.

Every newspaper in the Indian subcontinent has relaxed its former rigidity on Hindi. That relaxation was caused by the agitation arising in the South. Now newspapers fear that the agitation may proceed to separation and appeal to us: "Do not separate; remain together." Thus our group's demand is that the Constitution must be amended.

The Finance Minister described the difficulties of amendment. Difficulties certainly exist in everything, but capable people resolve them. He has often told this Assembly that he is such a capable man, and I have sometimes agreed. He should therefore stop saying the matter is difficult and say instead, "I shall accomplish it." We do not fear that he will promise and fail, because the fortress is already collapsing.

The rigidity prevailing today is not what existed in 1945–50. In the North today the concern is merely that previous labour for Hindi should not be wasted and that it should at least remain in the statute book. The former flag-flying dominance has gone; the fortress built then has broken into a hundred pieces.

We may therefore boldly attempt constitutional amendment. I would even take pride if the Government adopted Anbazhagan's resolution as its own. In both houses of the West Bengal legislature, all party leaders unanimously supported a similar resolution moved by someone else. Adopting this resolution will not injure the ministry or Congress's honour, nor need anyone fear that it will give the DMK immense influence. There are already enough people trying to destroy our influence, so why should you fear?

By accepting the resolution without fear, Parliament should reconsider Part XVII, remove the provision making Hindi in Devanagari script India's official language, and provide that English shall continue until one or more languages of the Indian subcontinent are considered qualified to become official languages.

Some misunderstand this as advocacy of English. They say people educated in English fear losing privileges when Tamil comes. Anyone who devoted to Tamil the intelligence devoted to English could earn equal distinction in Tamil. A language does not destroy intelligence; intelligent people can obtain good from any language. There is no reason to imagine that opposition to Hindi arises from fear of losing English-derived privilege, nor to treat Tamil as inferior to English.

The Finance Minister has urged that when a language of two thousand years gradually comes into administration, students should also study "any one of the Indian languages." If so, why are facilities and routes provided only for studying Hindi?

When Sardar Vedarathinam spoke this morning, he said Hindi had been made compulsory and accepted from the eighth to the eleventh class. That system should be replaced with one under which students may choose Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Gujarati, Marathi or another Indian language.

Tell us the minimum number of students required. Establish a rule that when that many students ask, a teacher of that language will be appointed. When parents and students express their preference, provide facilities for study in the relevant language.

We have achieved a noteworthy and honourable victory in the language question. For it to become complete, a great, even fundamental, change must be made in Part XVII of the Constitution. The idea that only one language may be official must be removed, and every language qualified for official use must be given a path to that status.

There is no need to fear confusion. A proposal to provide interpretation when one language is spoken in this Assembly was once defeated because 150 members stood on one side and fifteen on the other. Parliament has now accepted that principle and is making rapid arrangements at a cost of six or seven lakhs to translate speech from one language into another. A good idea echoes in many places.

I therefore ask the Government to accept our well-intentioned resolution, strengthen its hands, argue and struggle with the Government of India, and secure a permanent and honourable solution to the Hindi language question, one that does no injury to Tamil culture. With that request, I conclude my speech.

## English Translation

Speech delivered in 1948 emphasising the necessity of “a library in every home.”

We live in days when an event occurring for some reason in one place anywhere in the world affects other parts of the world. War in Palestine produces argument and counterargument in Palayamkottai. An election in Italy produces debate here over which party should win there. We live in a time when connections across the world have increased and continue to grow.

When I say that we live in such a time, I do not mean that we have entrusted responsibility for enriching our lives and the life of our country to people who came from a thousand miles away. Nor have we handed government to whoever wears a glittering crown or holds a flashing sword.

The people have assumed responsibility for government and proudly declare, “We are the masters of the country.” The world knows this well.

We have spoken in song, perhaps a little excessively, about our country's distinction and our strength, turning the world's attention toward us. We sang praises: “My country is a golden land; what can equal it?” We sang in delight that here are mountains touching the sky, inexhaustible rivers, fertile fields and groves heavy with fruit; that our sea contains pearls and our earth gold.

All that is well. But if asked, “Tell us the condition of education in your country,” we must bow our heads in shame.

We may say that our country has Vedic and Vedantic scholars, crowns among philosophers, and people blessed with divine grace. We do say so; some still continue saying it. But ask Kannan to sign his name and he shakes his head. Muthan says he has forgotten. Muniyan signs, but only the first and last letters are correct; everything between is unrelated.

Most of our people possess a sharp sword, a strong shield and a fearless heart, but the warrior's eyesight is defective. Such a condition brings no distinction to the country and opens no path to good government.

The country's condition must develop in keeping with the world. For that, the condition of the home must change. The ideal of “a library in every home” is the foundation of a plan to create a sound condition for the country.

The world does not respect a country because it sees mountains, rivers or great treasure. The world respects it by seeing the intellectual wealth of its people. We need intellectual wealth, very quickly and in very great measure.

There are enormous numbers of illiterate people in this country. That is a great evil. But do even all educated people possess intellectual wealth? Are their homes farms where the good qualities that distinguish a country flourish, or groves in which ideas that bring the country strength and beauty bloom? If we refuse to conceal reality, we must answer with a sigh: no.

If the world is to respect our national condition, this environment must change. By turning the ideal of a library in every home into a practical programme and accepting a little difficulty, we can certainly acquire intellectual wealth.

We possess more facilities than our ancestors did. In their age, children sat in a forest, by a riverbank, near a hermitage or beneath a banyan tree, while the teacher completed his morning duties and came to give lessons. It was an age of palm-leaf manuscript and stylus.

Our age can bring the world into our homes and show it to us. It is an age when ordinary people govern Parliament, with more means than before for increasing intellectual wealth.

Here I speak and you listen, though many miles lie between us. Neither you nor I are ascetics. The sound reaches you not by divine grace but through a device made by a scientist using knowledge. Our ancestors lived in days without such facilities.

With so many facilities, why is our intellectual wealth still so poor? We make no effort to increase or preserve it in our homes and seek no means to do so.

Look at the arrangement of most houses. There will be a hall for special occasions and two rooms adjoining it. One remains locked, with the key held by the head of the household. In the other there is a cradle or swinging cot. One child troubles the baby sleeping there; an older child goes to the kitchen and reports it to the mother.

The mother says, “Father is in the prayer room; go and tell him.” He is not there. He is lying on the raised platform near the cattle shed.

Our homes are broadly like this: hall, secure room, bedroom, kitchen and prayer room. All are present. Try to find a place for books or a room set aside for reading; it is extremely difficult.

In many homes, a few books lie on pillars, in window crevices or in the niche for Pillaiyar. Ask whether there is a library and nobody answers. A smile appears, meaning: “Madman! This is a house. Why have you come here asking for a library?”

Homes contain tables, chairs and sofas. There are cupboards holding silver trays, vessels of many kinds and rosewater pots. There are brass utensils. There may be enough clothing for a small shop and medicines enough for a little dispensary. Yet even in such well-appointed homes there is no library, no thought that one should exist, and no sense of its necessity.

People point to many household objects and say: “We bought this copper pot in Srirangam. We bought this during Deva's wedding at Tirupati. We bought this tray when we went to Bellary to see a prospective bride. We bought this gem-coloured carpet at Tiruvannamalai during Karthigai Deepam. We bought this at Chidambaram during the Arudra festival.”

They narrate a history for every object. But they do not display ten good books and say on what occasions they bought them.

As we attend to the decoration of a home and the equipment needed for special occasions, we must certainly take care and show concern to establish at least a small home library. We live in days when knowledge has become a weapon. Continuing to neglect this matter is an indirect act of treachery toward the country.

There must certainly be a library in every home.

After life's basic needs are met, the place now given to decoration and luxury must be given to the library. Food and clothing are fundamental needs. Once those are met, the first place must go to the library.

People ask: “What will it cost? Where is so much money?” Many cannot buy all the books they need. To remedy that deficiency, the Government, municipalities and public-welfare associations must operate public libraries.

Yet every home should at least collect and use some books necessary for basic knowledge.

Books are needed not only to learn many new things but also to make our people forget many things they already know.

Our people know visions of Kailasa, the glory of Vaikuntha, the merit of the Varalakshmi fast, Narada's tambura, Nandi's drum, Chitragupta's ledger, hell, pits of leeches and other infernal pits, the enchantment of salvation, the decoration of Indra's court, and the beauty of Menaka who sings and dances there.

They know Arumugan and the peacock he rides. They know of the quarrel between Mother Valli and beautiful Deivanai. They know these and countless similar things.

Where is Nandidurga hill? They do not know. Who is the Finance Minister? They say they do not know. Where does the Cauvery rise? They do not care. Why is there no water in the Palar? They cannot say. How many textile mills exist? They do not know the count.

In what direction is Dharapuram? They do not know. For how many miles does the Tamiraparani flow? They stand baffled without an answer. What is the area of the district in which they live? They do not know. What is the Province's revenue? They neither know nor try to learn.

They know the glory of Hanuman and can tell what benefit comes from circling a peepal tree. They can narrate stories of ghosts, sorcery and witchcraft. They do not know who invented the train they ride. Yet they can recount the story that water touched by the feet of Karunananda Swami, whom they travel by that train to see, cures diseases caused by karma.

This is our people's intellectual wealth. They form the majority.

Can our country, composed of such people, properly belong to a world that produces atomic bombs as a force of destruction and conducts research into travelling to the lunar sphere as a constructive force?

Nobody concerned with the country's future will say this condition is right. People ask, “It is not right, but what can be done?”

We must establish a library in every home. We must open a way for knowledge of the world to enter people's minds. They need books to understand their country and the world—not necessarily highly specialised works, but at least a few books explaining basic truths.

If households with modest means establish, for some time, the habit of buying books during family celebrations, upon returning from journeys, and when giving gifts, they can easily create a small library.

When establishing it, they must not include works that perpetuate meaningless, unnecessary and even harmful ideas already soaked into our people's minds. If such works are included, intellectual wealth will not grow. The mind will become a swamp in which the worms of antiquated ideas writhe.

If, under the plan of creating a library, one stacks three kinds of New Year almanac, a bundle of old almanacs, *Pavalakkodi Malai*, *Panchamirtha Chindu*, a large-print story of Vikramaditya, a purana narrated by a ghost, *Nallathangal's Lament*, and the cremation-ground episode of *Harishchandra*, the intellectual wealth we seek will not arise.

Such a collection will make our country a hunting ground for deceivers. It will be like gathering matchsticks.

We must collect books that provide knowledge of the world and knowledge useful for worthwhile action. Collecting works that further nourish old methods and ideas and calling it a library will become a comic performance, like assembling blind people and naming the place where they sit “the dwelling of guides.”

In the library we intend to establish, the history of our country and books describing conditions in the world's nations must take first place.

It should generally contain books that give clarity to intelligence, nourishment to courage and strength to morality. It should not contain works declaring this living world worthless and pointing instead to a road through the heavens; works that promote illusion and mental bewilderment, destroy self-confidence, overemphasise fate, and degrade and condemn women.

Not an almanac but an atlas—a map of the world—must be in the library.

We must show honesty and courage on this fundamental question. Only then will a library in every home create intellectual clarity, produce intellectual wealth and sustain the country.

One cannot invite a tiger to string a flower garland. One should not expect sandalwood fragrance from mud.

Our old geographical knowledge showed fourteen worlds. In those days, our religious knowledge said even human sacrifice was necessary. Our historical knowledge said one king ruled for ten thousand years.

Our understanding of women's rights told of a chaste wife who carried her husband in a basket to the house of a courtesan. Our scientific knowledge reported a river of fire with a bridge of hair across it.

Including in today's home library the works that supported such ideas will certainly harm the country's welfare.

There must be books on geography and history to show us the real world. To show morality and the paths of life, every home must necessarily have a *Tirukkural*.

There must be books presenting the essence of Sangam literature—which may rightly be called Tamil Nadu's special distinction—in a form ordinary people can understand and read.

At a minimum, there should be a book containing important information about scientific discoveries and arts that assist human progress and the conveniences of life.

There must be biographies of those who laboured for national liberation, those who cleansed the stains from people's minds, explorers of distant lands, heroes and wise people.

If a library in every home is established on this foundation, the country will attain a sound condition.

We need a library in every home, but it must contain works that provide ways to remove harm.

## Translator's Notes

The source is a complete eight-image 1948 speech. “Intellectual wealth” renders மனவளம், which combines breadth of knowledge, critical capacity and cultivated mind rather than mere literacy. The list of disfavoured books is retained as Anna's argument in its historical register and not presented as a neutral modern collection policy. The OCR phrase about world science appears to mean research into travel to the lunar sphere; translation follows that context conservatively. The Tamil source remains verbatim.

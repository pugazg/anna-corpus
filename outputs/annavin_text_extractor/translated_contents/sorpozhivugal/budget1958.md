பொருட்டு எல்லாக் கட்சிகளுக்கும் இருப்பதைப் போலவே, திராவிட
முன்னேற்றக் கழகத்திற்கும் அக்கறை இருப்பதிலேயும், அதிலே கலந்து
கொள்ளுகிற வாய்ப்பு எனக்கு ஏற்பட்டதிலேயும் உண்மையிலேயே
மகிழ்ச்சி எய்துகிறேன்.

“இந்த வரவு-செலவுத் திட்டத்தை ஆராய்வதற்கு முன்னால்
இந்த வரவு-செலவுத் திட்டம் எந்த நேரத்திலே நம்மிடத்தில்
தரப்படுகிறது என்பதை ஆராய்வது, அதனுடைய தன்மைகளை
உண்மையில் புரிந்துகொள்வதற்கு மிகுந்த உதவியாக இருக்கும் என்று
கருதுகிறேன். அப்படி ஆராய்வதில் நமக்குச் சாதகமான சூழ்நிலைகள்

நிதியமைச்சர் அவர்கள் மகிழ்ச்சியோடு தெரிவித்துள்ளார்கள்.

“இந்த நாட்டு மக்களிடையே எத்தனையோ மாதிரியான

கேட்கின்ற வரிகளை எல்லாம் தருகிறார்கள்! தீட்டப்படுகின்ற
சட்டங்களுக்கு மதிப்பளிக்கிறார்கள்! இது நல்ல சூழ்நிலைதான்.
அதேபோன்று, நம் நாட்டிலே இருக்கிற பத்திரிகைகளில்

- கொள்கைகளிலே எந்த மாதிரி குறைபாடுகள் இருந்தாலும்,

## English Translation

## Address 13 - 1958

C. N. Annadurai: "Like every other party, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam has a concern in examining the budget that our State's Finance Minister has placed before this House for discussion. I am genuinely glad that I have received the opportunity to take part in it.

"Before examining this budget, I believe that considering the particular time at which it is being presented to us will greatly help us understand its character. How many circumstances favourable to us emerge from such an examination?

"The Finance Minister has happily informed us that nature has helped us this year.

"However many kinds of anguish may exist among the people of this country, to this very moment they have remained responsible, law-abiding citizens and paid every tax demanded of them. They respect the laws that are enacted. That is certainly a favourable circumstance. Likewise, even when there are deficiencies in the Government's administration, schemes, or policies, and even when these lack strength or lustre, most newspapers in our country lend their support today by coating them in bright colours so that they appear attractive precisely when their lustre has faded.

"That is not all. We have been entrusted with a capable administrative machinery from which the entire Indian subcontinent might observe and learn.

"I did not go looking for the distasteful expression, 'But... but.' It pursues me of its own accord.

"But despite all these favourable circumstances, prices have risen. Unemployment is rampant. Scarcity pursues us in every field. Those employed in Government offices and Government-aided offices are all living with inadequacy today. No favourable condition has been created to relieve it. The budget states that those in the office sector have received Government assistance. The Minister, I am sure, also knows that no one in that sector is wholly satisfied. I remind him of this only to increase the affection and regard I bear for him, not merely to find fault.

"I respectfully wish to point out that many matters deserving criticism have already been explained in this House.

"This House has not been convened so that each person may display his oratorical skill. That is unnecessary. Each person's standing has already been established before the people's forum in the country. Here, one person raises each matter and another gives the answer.

"Members of the Opposition make accusations properly and provide reasons. They follow a policy and, standing by it, possess the qualification to present their case with appropriate evidence, restraint, and dignity. That qualification exists among Opposition members, particularly among members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

"I wish to draw this House's attention to what is happening in the other Legislative Assemblies of the Indian subcontinent. A few days ago we read in the newspapers that a man who had been defeated in an election was appointed Governor, and that great disorder broke out in the Assembly in opposition to the address he came there to deliver.

"Governor Prakasa, who administered our State with distinction and ability, went to deliver his address in the Bombay Legislature. On the ground that they did not want a bilingual State, members created a disturbance in the House.

"More striking than all this, Trilok Singh was elected Speaker of the Rajasthan Legislative Assembly. Opposing it, one member waited until the Speaker had gone out, climbed into the Speaker's chair, sat there obstinately, and declared, 'I will not move.'

"At a time when such incidents are taking place in other Assemblies, you refuse to recognise that we conduct ourselves here with restraint and dignity, affection and modesty, and consideration. If, beyond that, you censure us and find fault with us, it can only mean that you are driving us towards the path I mentioned earlier.

"We have always worked on the foundations of democracy.

"We did not enter this House with the intention of remaining Opposition members forever. We conduct ourselves with a sense of responsibility because we believe that, if not today, the people will one day entrust us with the responsibility of government. I therefore wish to make clear that we will not behave irresponsibly.

"Next, while many members spoke here, they described deficiencies in their constituencies. I too have gathered and noted all those deficiencies in a form that may assist our Finance Minister. I have not recorded which person said what; I have compiled the nature of the shortcomings that were raised. Each member studies the condition of his constituency and brings its deficiencies forward. 'This has not been attended to; that has not been attended to; will our constituency receive attention? We need to know,' many members of the ruling party asked. I am not concerned here with members of the other parties.

"When Congress members themselves face such distress, there is no need to describe the condition of everyone else. One honourable Congress member, a respected lady, said: 'We brought so many Ministers and placed our grievances before them. We welcomed them and garlanded them. We even brought the Honourable Minister Kakkan and told him our grievances. They have still not been remedied. Our suffering and tears have not been wiped away.' She wept in anguish as she spoke.

"At a time when some people think, 'We are poor; we belong to the Opposition; is this even a party, and what standing do these people have?', why is the opinion of members of the ruling party so dismal even after the Congress Government has introduced fine reforms and excellent schemes? My innocent heart is moved to ask. How much money has been spent in the name of these schemes, and yet what has been gained?"

"Several other members, while speaking, described how poor cultivators continued to suffer at the hands of landlords even after the 60-40 law was enacted.

"Merely passing laws is not enough. The administrative machinery that implements them must be kept in proper working order. Many members complained that in numerous places the administrative machinery was not functioning properly. They pointedly drew the Government's attention to the fact that cultivators who had received monastery lands ten or fifteen years ago were still being subjected to many hardships. Hearing all this, one is almost driven to wonder, 'Alas, are these people really supposed to be on our side, or did they part from us at the election?'

"Besides these matters, members have said since this morning that hospitals have not been provided, villages have not received proper roads and transport, electricity restrictions are severe, and hardships arise from Ceylon. They have asked for remedies to all these problems.

"When we place these matters before you, why should you become angry, distressed, or agitated? When members of your own party state these deficiencies, why should we alone, as members of the Opposition, remain silent? We did not come here to sing lullabies to the ruling party. We came precisely to point out its faults. That is the responsibility of democracy. We have not behaved without a sense of propriety.

"Members pointed out that unemployment had not disappeared, irrigation facilities were unavailable, and drinking-water facilities were unavailable. Congress members, I wish to tell you something through this House. Members of the Opposition at least have grievances of their own, and must state them if those grievances are to be remedied. But as members of the ruling party, you have many committees. Through them, you could inform the Government and secure remedies for their grievances. Why, then, do you raise them here?

"Have you grown weary of repeating them? Do your words fail to enter their ears? Have you concluded that speaking produces no result? Or is your purpose to bring them to our notice?

"If you had not spoken at such length about these deficiencies, we in the Opposition would have had more time to discuss the basic policy of the budget. All the accusations made here amount to this: 'The administrative machinery is not working properly.' Our accusation is different. We find fault at the level of policy. If democracy is to grow, administration must be examined on the basis of policy.

"With your permission, I wish at this point to cite a passage from the Governor's address to this House:

> The Finance Minister, in his budget speech, will deal with the policies of the new Ministry.

"Everyone therefore expected the Finance Minister's address to contain a declaration of policy. I searched it minutely and found none. Two possible explanations occur to me for the absence of a policy statement.

"The first is that the Finance Minister may have thought, 'Do you not already know our Congress policy?' The second is his saying, 'Look, I have combined economics and political science and given you statistics.' At college I studied those two subjects together as one. Even so, I regret to say that I have been unable to discover any policy explanation here. In truth, as far as I can determine, every detail supplied in this budget has been prepared under a committee. Only by examining the three matters considered by that committee can the relative merit of this budget be properly understood.

"For example, if the ruling party in Canada were to present a budget like this today, we may suppose that it would genuinely do so with apprehension, because the difference in membership between the ruling party and the Opposition there is only ten or twelve.

"As for my party today, members of the ruling party will say that there are only fifteen of us. That is not all. Even if every member of the Opposition were added together - and even the adding and joining are difficult - members of the ruling party would still outnumber them by more than three to one. They therefore know with certainty that however many deficiencies we point out, however many irregularities we expose, and whatever policy is criticised, 'They will speak only today. Let them speak. What will their speech accomplish? What could possibly happen?' Such a thought may have arisen. I believe this budget was prepared on the strength of that confidence. I ask you not merely to consider our number, but to consider our ideas.

"Many members praised the Finance Minister. Their praise was so abundant that one might fear it would give him indigestion. My praise, however, will contain a little spice with the sweetness. It may therefore cure the indigestion he has acquired and restore his digestion.

"I greatly praise the Finance Minister for conducting himself in such a way that we cannot determine who he is. Perhaps he believed it unwise to reveal who he was before we discovered it; perhaps he did not want us to discover who he was. I do not know. He has not revealed himself. I say this from the standpoint of economic policy.

"Does he support increasing economic resources through heavy taxation? Is he a traditional economist who carries on the country's administration with the income available? Is he an economist who believes a deficit budget benefits the country? Or does he believe that a surplus budget is good for the country? His words do not enable us to tell. Speaking of a deficit budget, some members praised it by saying, 'Deficit budget is not bad. It is a virtue also.' The Honourable Muthiah Chettiar hurriedly declared, 'This is an excellent economic scheme; only now have you accepted the proposal I have advocated for many years.'

"Did the Finance Minister intend through this deficit budget to adjust matters for the country's welfare? Or has the budget reached this position because it escaped the Finance Minister's control? I wish to know. Everyone understands that there is a fundamental difference between the two."

"Hitler, who ruled Germany, regarded the deficit budget as a mighty weapon in the Government's possession. Seizing unrestrained authority, he printed vast quantities of currency, distributed it with the support of the gun, and stabilised and protected his economy.

"The Finance Minister has not explained the significance of the deficit budget. To determine whether he considers a deficit budget inherently proper, I carefully studied the speeches delivered by many members on last year's budget. The curious thing is that, just as he is now praised for presenting a deficit budget, he was praised in this same House last year for imposing heavy taxation and bringing forward a surplus budget. I believe the Finance Minister prepares each budget according to the situation in which he finds himself.

"Praise is normal in a democracy, but it is my humble and candid view that it must not become excessive.

"Some members welcomed the fact that he had imposed no new taxes while preparing a deficit budget.

"So far as I and my party are concerned, we do not believe that a country's hardships and losses are determined merely by whether taxation is high or low. The elementary economics I learned taught that the basic purpose of a sound plan must be to tax those able to bear it and reduce the taxes borne by those unable to bear them. Perhaps the Finance Minister has studied beyond that elementary economics and was taught otherwise; I do not know. Or perhaps he has acquired some new administrative experience. I cannot understand it.

"Those who explained economic theory identified two systems of taxation as 'Progressive taxation - Regressive taxation.' Put differently, they may also be described as 'Direct taxation - Indirect taxation.'

"If we examine our budgets up to this moment, a substantial share of Government revenue comes from the sales tax paid by poor people. Great cries have arisen across the country that many sectors remain neglected at this time. We have repeatedly and emphatically explained at many stages that sales tax truly torments the people. What we therefore expected now was that our Finance Minister would at least exempt foodstuffs from sales tax.

"For a long time, the traders and chambers of commerce in our country have convened various conferences, invited Ministers to attend, submitted memorials to them, and explained their grievances. They said, 'We would be satisfied if the sales tax now imposed at several stages were collected at even one stage alone.'

"We thought the Chief Minister might do so because he belongs to Virudhunagar, a town crowded with traders. I sometimes wonder whether they remain strict on sales tax because they fear that we would accuse them of granting concessions to traders. We are not such people, and we would make no such accusation. Do not be afraid on our account. If you acted so for other reasons, let that pass; but if you acted so because of us, please change course. Come forward to impose a single-point sales tax, and exempt foodstuffs from sales tax. I state this firmly.

"If the Finance Minister were to change 'food grains' to 'food stuffs,' the country would experience a measure of genuine satisfaction.

"His smile seems to say, 'Changing words and changing the names of towns - is that not the whole programme of your party?'

"But the schemes we propose possess a special quality. We propose only schemes that do not impose heavy expenditure on the Government. If we offered costly schemes, what could they do, and where could they go? We already know. They go north, only to return disappointed when nothing is available there.

"If the expression 'food grains' were changed to 'foodstuffs' and sales tax on them removed, Government revenue would fall by only a few lakh rupees.

"But whenever people ate, they would bless the Government wholeheartedly. At mealtime they would say, 'A good Government prevails in the country; a good Cabinet governs it; they have removed the tax on foodstuffs,' and bless it from their hearts.

"Many people come and go in the Legislative Assembly. They would value this as a gift of democracy. With the first mouthful they would bless the Chief Minister, and with the second they would bless the Finance Minister." At this point the Finance Minister remarked, "With the third mouthful they will bless the man who proposed it," and loud laughter arose in the House.

Continuing, Anna said:

"At mealtime, the public would unanimously give thanks because foodstuffs bore no tax. Orthodox believers too say that it is good to offer blessings while eating. Although I differ from orthodoxy on other matters, I agree with them on this one.

"Many Congress members who spoke here examined why no new tax had been imposed in the budget and offered their explanations. The Finance Minister's reason for imposing no new tax, in my view, is that the welt from the blow already received - the Central Government's taxation - has not yet faded, and he may therefore have refrained from striking it again.

"Finance Ministers always suffer an itch of the mind. Once a person is installed in the Finance Minister's seat, the thought of 'tax' remains with him in dream and waking memory alike.

"I read in a humorous magazine that a Finance Minister looked at the ordinary sari his wife was wearing and asked, 'Is there no tax on this?' Whatever he saw and wherever he looked, his attention never left taxation. I refer to an imaginary Finance Minister, not this Finance Minister.

"I did not say that he had refrained from taxation because he feared us; no one among us said it. But because a few members of their own party gave that explanation, members of our Kazhagam replied, 'Yes, it was indeed out of fear of us.' In truth, I have no confidence that the Finance Minister will continue without imposing taxes. Members praised him for writing his sentence in excellent Tamil. Tamil never lies. The Tamil sentence says: 'We have concluded that it is proper to leave the remaining deficit uncovered temporarily at this stage.' They have used two or three dangerous words with great elegance and skill.

"In direct contrast, when the Finance Minister of Bombay State presented a similar deficit budget, he gave this reason: 'The people of my State have already been taxed heavily. I fear that a major share - up to forty percent - of the new tax imposed by the Central Government will be collected from my State. I have therefore imposed no new tax proposals to make good the deficit in the budget.'"

"Here, however, our Finance Minister offered no reason. More than that, I eagerly analysed the plan to discover why no reason was given. The omission itself aroused a fear. At last I found the statement: 'We have concluded that it is proper to leave the remaining deficit uncovered temporarily at this stage.' Seeing the word 'temporarily,' I am driven to think that he is making the people wait until next year, saying: 'The blow from Delhi fell only this year, and its welt has not yet faded. I will give you another blow next year; wait until then.'

"When we speak of taxation, people can determine the tendency of a ruling party or Government only by examining the methods of taxation it uses. That is why various progressive parties lamented: 'Those who govern the country today had their schemes described as socialist. Are they not acting accordingly?' The reason for that sorrow is understandable.

"Our rulers keep saying 'socialism, socialism,' but what they practise today is not *samadharmam*, social justice; it is only *samaya-dharmam*, expedient morality. They maintain different forms of 'dharma' to suit the occasion: 'Enough, alas; this is not a good time to tax, so let us impose no tax now.' The Government has calculated only in that manner. That is why I described their policy as *samaya-dharmam*.

"If we consider the period in which this budget appears, the First Five-Year Plan has ended and we stand at the beginning of the Second. The first plan has improved our country to some extent, and the second is to improve it further. I cannot accept the argument that, with a sense of responsibility to complete that plan, the people must endure even new taxation. I could understand such an argument only if statistics demonstrated that the First Five-Year Plan had truly benefited the poor, the humble, and the ordinary people; if the people of the country accepted that demonstration; and if the strength they gained through the first plan made them strong enough to bear the second. In that event I could genuinely commend the argument.

"Fortunately, as a man of medicine seated in the Speaker's chair, you would not, I trust, administer a powerful tonic to someone who is already rather weak. Before taking up the Second Five-Year Plan, let us ask whether the people have obtained their three fundamental necessities: food, clothing, and shelter. In the *Economic Review*, Congress General Secretary Shriman Narayan writes:

> The people feel that while the poorer section cannot escape taxation of an indirect nature in the form of sales tax and excise duties on various necessaries of life, the richer classes have become experts in managing evasion and avoidence of taxation.

"That appears in the *Economic Review*, an almost official publication conducted by Congressmen. If it says so, what error is there in our saying the same? Nor is that all. The internationally known economist Professor Kaldor has stated:

> Evasion of direct taxes in India is of the order of Rs. 200 to 300 crores per year.

"According to Kaldor's estimate, the tax evaded annually by wealthy people in the Indian subcontinent amounts to two hundred or three hundred crore rupees. I also know that, after examining this estimate and concluding that it was exaggerated, the Government of India itself declared, 'However it is calculated, it cannot exceed sixty crore.' Nevertheless, remember that Shriman Narayan has shown that taxation on this immense scale remains uncollected and is lost.

"Speaking of the Five-Year Plan, he also makes this clear:

> The common man has not been able to feel the glow of freedom during the last few years in accordance with his expectation.

"When many people ask this Government, 'You have not done this; you have not done that,' those holding office may complain, 'What is this? Whenever we look, they are finding fault.' People ask in that manner today precisely because they have immense confidence in the Government. Those in office ought to take pride in that fact. Had the British still ruled this country, the people would not have asked them.

"Do not congratulate yourselves by saying, 'Under the white man there was only an ordinary hurricane lamp; now, in our time, a great tube light has been installed.' If you praise yourselves in that fashion, you will only deceive yourselves. The white man said the same thing: 'I showed the railway to people who had never even seen one, and now you demand that I myself be put aboard and sent away. Why?'

"It is from the concern that Congress democracy must not change from white imperialism into brown imperialism that Congress General Secretary Shriman Narayan has issued his warning.

"I wish to mention something else. Referring to the Community Projects under the administration of our Chief Minister, Shriman Narayan says that they are not in a satisfactory condition. In other words:

> Even in the Community Projects National Extension Services, it is now being admitted that lowest sections of rural population have not been benefitted to any considerable extent. In fact, all the available statistics tend to show that the difference between the living standard of the richer and poorer sections, instead of being narrowed down has increased further during the last nine or ten years.

"What I now ask before this House, with the Honourable Speaker's permission, is this: when the First Five-Year Plan has disappointed the people to this degree, what is gained by asking those same poor people to pay taxes? We must decide our view of this budget by considering upon whom the tax is imposed and how it is spent."

Continuing, Anna read the fifth paragraph of the Finance Minister's statement, which said, "Rising prices and a shortage of foreign exchange are the two great difficulties affecting the country's economic condition today," and said:

"No one can deny this. Those who deny it would only be concealing it.

"These two great deficiencies truly exist. What I regret is that the Finance Minister has not shown that he possesses the ability to seek a sound remedy for them. Yet neither matter has been entrusted to his direct supervision, his direct authority, or his direct administration. The two difficulties can be resolved only in relation to conditions across India. Since he understands this well, he has not claimed that we can settle them here; instead, he has described the few things that lie within his power."

"A few members wished to praise him for those measures. One member asked movingly, 'Will you not say that even one thing is good?' Why should we stand in your way when you gain without our losing? I do not praise him because I know that our Finance Minister is not a man of such a fickle mind that these things alone would satisfy him.

"Some praised him out of affection. Such speech is good for them too. That is a different matter.

"The arrangements by which our Finance Minister has apportioned expenditure within his means genuinely deserve welcome to some extent. But while welcoming them, we must divide them into three and then deliver our judgment. They are: first, the scheme proposed; second, the money allotted to it; and third, the administrative method by which that money is spent. Only when all three are sound will our schemes truly yield good results. For example, the Finance Minister said, 'Thirty-eight seed farms were opened last year, and we have decided to open 136 this year.'

"This is genuinely a just and necessary scheme, worthy of public welcome. But when it is implemented, we must examine how these farms are developed, from whom the land selected for them is to be purchased, and how the price of the purchased land will be fixed.

"Instead of purchasing that land, the landlords could be summoned and legally required to set aside at least a portion of their extensive holdings for seed farms. But since there are many landed magnates who enjoy the affection of the ruling party, if you appeal to them gently and ask them to reserve a portion for a seed farm, they too will give it. The farms would then require no capital outlay, the Government would retain more money, and the scheme could be implemented easily.

"A member also said that seeds supplied by the Government do not reach the poor cultivator. Drawing on experience, he clearly described the rural condition: the seeds fail to arrive at sowing time, come only at harvest, and are therefore sold along with the crop in the market.

"A Government does not become worthy of praise merely by displaying schemes on paper. It must calculate the extent to which each scheme on paper has placed one additional mouthful of rice in every home, one additional spoonful of oil in the curry, and one additional moment of peace in life. The smile of a poor person mingled with contentment is the reward and fame a Finance Minister should seek.

"It will yield no real benefit if members of your party and one or two members of the Opposition praise you in speeches. I wish to state this emphatically.

"Last year's budget promised, 'We will open fifty seed farms this year,' yet only thirty-eight were opened. If a promise of fifty produced thirty-eight, what are we to do if a promise of 136 now stops at thirty-six? I ask that every farm be opened this year.

"The budget states that irrigation schemes will continue this year at a cost of Rs. 55 lakh. Last year's budget figures show expenditure of Rs. 1,23,00,000 for this purpose. The Finance Minister is bound to explain why a smaller amount has been allotted to irrigation this year.

"Two major questions arose in the debate. Our Chief Minister has been telling the people, 'There are no great rivers here, so there is no scope for building great dams. They are in the north, and the great dams are therefore built there.'

"He explains politely that there truly are no great rivers in the south on which to build dams. Some members of his party propose, 'There are no rivers to dam; it is Annadurai's mouth that needs a dam.' Build as many dams as you can. I have not asked you to refrain.

"When small irrigation schemes are discussed, there remain many opportunities in our country to restrain mountain streams that still run to waste and create minor irrigation works. Why are they postponed?

"We are told that they are set aside solely because the revenue obtained from the money spent would be very small. They consider only the direct profit secured by carrying out a small irrigation scheme. They do not consider the happiness it creates in the people's minds or the satisfaction it gives them. If those benefits too were converted into rupees, I believe they would accept an arrangement under which every such scheme ought to be completed.

"Everyone complained about his own constituency. If I did not similarly speak of mine, neither we nor the people of my constituency would think, 'Annadurai spoke of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam but said nothing about his own constituency'; but Opposition members here would say so.

"I wish to bring a dry river in my constituency to the Chief Minister's compassionate heart. I remember that when I was a schoolboy, the teacher rang the bell and said, 'Children, you may go home; water has arrived in the Palar.'

"In our region, the arrival of water in the Palar was an event for which the school declared a holiday and celebrated. The Palar is a fine and a great river. Once a year, when rain falls for three or four days, water comes down in flood. It is held in small reservoirs here and there, fills the great lakes and tanks, and supports irrigation in Chengalpattu. But the silt carried by the flood settles in those lakes. Their depth diminishes, their drainage structures eventually break, and even when a flood arrives the water no longer remains but rushes out.

"Just as we sometimes read a subject, fail to understand its true meaning, and automatically spew it out, the tanks spew out the water that surges into them.

"When we ask what stage has been reached in the various negotiations conducted by the Madras and Mysore Governments, they simply tell us, 'It is over.' Instead of treating any administrative problem as 'over,' I ask the Chief Minister of our State to speak again with the Chief Minister of Mysore about the Palar and take the necessary action.

"Experts were consulted earlier. Our Chief Minister is not an expert, and it is precisely because he is not one that the people place their confidence in him today. Experts trust statistics. This non-expert understands the people's condition by looking at their hungry stomachs, sun-scorched heads, and careworn faces. I therefore ask that negotiations with the Mysore Government be reopened immediately so that water may flow into the Palar.

"There is another possible route. In Russia, even where no water remains above ground in a river, groundwater is brought up at various places with electricity and used for irrigation. Our engineering department may be able to say more about this. I wish to urge that, by that method at least, water be brought to the Palar.

"Do that, and then go to any corner of Chengalpattu district and denounce our Kazhagam. The people will garland you. Denounce us as much as you wish; they will even praise you by saying, 'Our sovereign has brought us water.' There are two gains in this: first, you secure the people's support; second, you find a way to restrain us. By whatever means, let water come to the Palar."

Anna next read from the address delivered by the Finance Minister when presenting the previous year's budget and said:

"If the Government genuinely desires the cooperation of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in developing southern industry, the Kazhagam is ready today to offer it. If industrial progress can sustain workers without harming them, increase the country's wealth, and at the same time transform the capitalist class, the cooperation of a responsible Opposition will always be available.

"But it must be determined at what time and at what stage the rulers seek that cooperation. They say only that the Opposition must cooperate, without making clear when or at what stage it is asked to do so. In my view, it would be best to ask for cooperation while the plan is being framed. We are ready to cooperate.

"I cite the Ramayana in the thought that members here will be pleased by it. Did not even the squirrel assist Rama in building the bridge? In that same manner, this Annadurai too may be put to use. If asked while the plan is being framed, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam will certainly cooperate."

He concluded: "I 'temporarily' praise the Finance Minister who has 'temporarily' imposed no tax in the budget."

Salutations.

## Translator's Notes

`Governor Prakasa` and `Trilok Singh` follow the forms printed in the Tamil source. `Kakkan` refers to the Congress minister named in the member's quoted complaint.

The printed `60-40 law` is retained without expansion because the speech does not identify the enactment more precisely. The Governor's English sentence and the phrase `Deficit budget is not bad. It is a virtue also` are reproduced from the scan before being discussed in translation.

`Single-point sales tax` renders the source proposal that sales tax be collected at one stage rather than at multiple stages. `Temporarily` is kept prominent because Anna's closing argument returns to the same word.

The source makes a Tamil pun between `samadharmam` (social equality or socialism) and `samaya-dharmam` (conduct adjusted to the occasion); both forms are retained and glossed. The printed English source spells `avoidance` as `avoidence`; that spelling remains visible in the quoted source and is not silently corrected.

The Palar is the river running through Annadurai's constituency and Chengalpattu. The source's comparison `136 ... முப்பத்தாறு` is translated literally as a warning that a promise of 136 farms might stop at thirty-six; the printed figure is retained rather than normalised.

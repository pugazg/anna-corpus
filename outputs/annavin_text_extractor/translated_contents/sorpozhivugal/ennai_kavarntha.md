## English Translation

A speech delivered in 1948 on the subject “Books That Captivated Me”:

Aladdin stood sobbing in that dark cave and leaned against the wall. The lamp in his hand rubbed against it. At once, a sound arose as though the whole cave were shaking violently. Aladdin’s head spun. He cried, “Alas!” A terrifying genie appeared before him.

There was a stage, not only for me but for many of you, when we read *Aladdin’s Wonderful Lamp*, filled with such descriptions, astonishing incidents, and startling information, became enchanted by it, and believed no finer book existed. In childhood the story certainly captured our hearts. We cried when Aladdin cried and laughed when he laughed. We hated the magician and rejoiced when told that he had died. Forgetting evening play, morning food, night study, household arithmetic, and other duties, we read and relished the story. Every old lamp reminded our young minds of that magic lamp; every pair of rolling eyes and menacing moustaches recalled the magician. The story had settled so deeply within us and given us such delight.

School lessons about the breadth and length of Africa, the islands in the Pacific, dividing one thousand rupees among sixty-six people, or why a banyan tree has aerial roots seemed bitter. Stories of Aladdin, of leaving one body and entering another, and of what Kali told the ghoul tasted sweet and captivated us.

At that age, in those bounding school days, these were the books that captivated me. Was it only me? They captivated you too. Think carefully and you will remember the scene: a dim earthen lamp burns upon the ledge of a street veranda, and a child leans against the wall reading, unaware of everything nearby and unconcerned even when a tiny ant bites.

We read to gain benefits such as delight, new ideas, change in old beliefs, refinement, and the ability to act. Each kind of book provides each benefit in a different degree and helps shape our minds. We cannot say that a delightful book must necessarily give the power to act, or that a work sowing new ideas must invariably possess a sweet and pleasurable style.

Reading an attractively written account of Narada’s music or a scene in the celestial realm certainly produces one kind of pleasure. But it does not awaken new thought, awareness, or action. Even those who read such books in devotional ecstasy do not set out saying that they must visit Narada in person.

A history of Malaya may lack literary sweetness. Yet readers who obtain the means may think of travelling there and act upon that thought.

At the stage when we read only for pleasure, stories filled with unbelievable events and impossible deeds attract the heart. Tales like those of Vikramaditya, whose narrative pattern enters the mind easily and rapidly, belong to this class.

After passing that stage, the mind seeks useful books. Among works that gave both recreation and benefit, together with literary pleasure, I mention *Prathapa Mudaliar Charithram*. It is one of the books that captivated my heart.

People who accept this world and the next as two separate realms begin to value books said to assist the afterlife. They force ideas incompatible with rational inquiry into their minds and become burdened by anxiety and fear.

I have read such books. At times they produced fear and disgust but did not captivate me. When I encountered puranic and epic stories during school, I thought either that they were unnecessary or that they contained things we could not accomplish. Descriptions of hell, a head exploding into a thousand fragments, or a mountain snake swallowing someone sometimes shocked the heart while one read. Yet neither those books nor their ideas remained in my mind for long. Like birds in a millet field, the ideas arrive in flocks and touch the mind; but as the warning cry called intellectual clarity continues to sound, they fly away. This is true not only for me but for anyone of average intelligence.

Books possessing musical cadence and richness of meaning that transport us while we read have the power to captivate the mind. Among such works that drew me most strongly, I consider *Kalingathu Parani* important. Such books certainly give literary pleasure. But once we reach a stage of reading for practical benefit, neither time nor inclination naturally arises to read works offering pleasure alone, such as *Kalingathu Parani* and *Kuttrala Kuravanji*.

This does not mean we possess an intelligence too blind to perceive the pleasure in epics that paint scenes through sweet style and beautiful description: the little stream singing a chindu, the mountain wearing clouds as a garland, the spring where a deer and its fawn drink, farmers ploughing wet fields, a conch thrown at fruit said to have been plucked, or banana plants rising like eagles with grain only a cubit high. Nor are we rustic people incapable of drinking in such beauty. Many among you who share my condition do not seek such books today because our purpose directs us toward others. The works that now captivate us are those explaining the problems that stand before our mind’s eye and command us to work.

The mind grows through thought, through waves of ideas moving across the world, and through lessons taught by the teacher called life. New ideals continually arise. A thirst for knowledge appropriate to them develops, and we seek books that can quench it.

Stories of beauty, pleasure, love, and meeting a fine young woman in a mango grove give enjoyment and sometimes benefit. Yet the reader’s whole ideal is not found within them. The stage of life changes.

The scenes in *Silappathikaram* captivated the heart: Kovalan fondly addressing Kannagi, “O flawless gold! O right-spiralled pearl!”; the sea festival; and the Pandya king trembling and dying as he cries, “Am I a king who destroyed justice? I am the thief! I am the thief!” Many other heart-stealing scenes did the same. But life’s stage changes, and the list of books changes with it.

When one reaches an age when strength surges, books about heroic deeds, battle scenes, and the difficult work of establishing governments become deeply attractive. Accounts of Alexander’s power, Julius Caesar’s courage, Napoleon’s passion for war, and the ways empires arose and fell captivated my mind. They also awakened a desire to learn about comparable great heroes in my motherland of Tamil Nadu: crowned kings and rulers who crossed the sea and planted flags of victory. I wished to understand the distinctive greatness of the sacred motherland and proclaim it to the world. I began to search Tamil records closely and desired lessons from every Tamil scholar.

I found scattered brief references, sweet poems about courage reaching the Himalayas, the tale of placing a stone upon Kanakavijaya’s head, inscriptions about conquering the Ganges and Kadaram, and lists of names such as Karikala, Rajaraja, Kulothunga, Nedunchezhian who overcame an Aryan army, the king of dance, and Attanathi. But a coherent history comparable with those of heroes from other countries has still not been obtained. Because it is unavailable, we have lost a magnificent instrument capable of captivating heroic young minds ready for action.

Two books greatly captivated me: Abbott’s life of Napoleon and Gibbon’s *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*.

When reading the histories of countries that fought heroic wars to escape slavery and the lives of powerful figures within them, even a coward becomes brave and a hunchback stands a little straighter. These people conducted struggles for liberation without fearing danger, without panic or weariness, even when the scorpion of poverty stung their private lives and the snake of blame bit them. Books about Simón Bolívar and Garibaldi captivated me greatly.

Though not written in the same manner and available only in scattered form, the stories of Kattabomman and Desingh Raja also attracted me. So did brief references to the great power of Paranjothi, who in the time of Narasimhavarman Pallava led an army from Kanchipuram to Vatapi near Bombay and defeated the Chalukya king Pulakesi.

While a country remains enslaved, stories of freedom fighters provide a feast that captivates the mind. After that feast has been consumed, more books of the same kind cease to attract us; it becomes like eating a second or third mango.

In general, we pass beyond the stage of merely admiring courage and enter one in which we calculate what courage produced. The book list changes again.

At that stage, histories of other kinds of people captivate the mind more than those of warriors: people who fought for justice, stood against the arrogance of the privileged, freed herds of bewildered deer from tigers roaming beneath sacred coverings, extended a hand to the oppressed, fearlessly confronted palaces and monasteries that served as seats of domination, waged moral struggles, and laboured for the people’s well-being.

In this class I consider important the history and teachings of Voltaire, the flame of knowledge who tore apart the darkness of ignorance, the general of reason who shattered the fortress of domination, and the enemy of crooked orthodoxy. I also value Émile Zola, the literary hero who fought and won after proclaiming: “The government may try to destroy me; military force may descend upon me; the whole world may mock me. Yet I will establish justice and wipe away the poor person’s tears.” Likewise, Ingersoll’s rationalist prose-poems stirred and captivated me.

At the present stage, the lives and research of scientific masters who discover nature’s subtleties, remove the sufferings of human society, provide comfort, and make the world new and convenient are excellent books capable of giving both pleasure and benefit and of captivating people’s minds.

Not only an individual but the whole world accepts books suited to each age in this manner.

Though books change with the age, not every work from the past dies.

The Buddha’s renunciation, Socrates speaking before the court, Jesus being nailed to the cross, Ilango Adigal’s renunciation, Manimekalai’s strength of mind, Kannagi’s arrows of questioning, Madhavi’s tenderness of heart, Ashoka’s grief, and Shakuntala’s sorrow are chains of events that will always move the heart.

Some people enthusiastically name particular books because they believe the world will regard them as pure-hearted and learned only if they claim to have read them.

“One who does not melt for *Thiruvasagam* will melt for no utterance.”

“Those who read *Thiruppugazh*, through the strength of their thought, value no one except through your grace.”

“The banyan and acacia strengthen teeth; four and two strengthen speech.” “*Naidatham* is medicine for poets.” “Do not debate with one who has studied *Kalladam*.”

Today’s world is not prepared to compile its book list solely from such sayings of praise.

The frontiers of knowledge are now multiplying from one into many. Today we possess one great work that combines clarity about problems in many fields, an explanation of culture, and reflection upon social organisation, government, and morality, and that can make people knowledgeable, capable, and ethical: the *Tirukkural*. It not only captivates our heart but helps correct us.

Yet knowledge is a continuing story. Its teachers continue to appear and will appear in the future. Many more books that captivate the heart will continue to emerge.

## English Translation

The following is a summary of the speech delivered by Arignar Anna at the “Victory Celebration” meeting held on the Marina in Chennai on 7 August 1960:

I have come here today to meet all of you and convey my greetings and gratitude. You will receive only my good wishes. How would the leaders of the other political parties congratulate you or us? If they did congratulate us, it would mean that somewhere we had truly strayed from our path. It is because we have not strayed that we feel happy.

Several friends here have explained that others speak differently about the victory we have won today. What they say is not important to us; what our own hearts say is what matters.

We did not draw up our plans believing that victory would come to us so quickly. When we resolved to show black flags, we thought earnestly about all the forms of repression that might be unleashed. Only then did all of us reach a firm decision at Kodambakkam. When I took leave of many of you that day, I left Kodambakkam with tears in my eyes. I returned from there believing that the great, valiant warriors of Tamil would triumph in the field.

I never expected that victory would seek us out so swiftly, without our having to endure such hardships and losses. To those who have never won a victory of this kind, it may appear ordinary.

If a young man remains unmarried, many will say, “Where is he ever going to get married?” When at last the wedding takes place, they will say, “What is so remarkable about a wedding? Their troubles are only beginning now!”

While we were assembled at Arivagam, a senior police officer came and spoke with great warmth. He asked me, not in the manner of an official but as an elder statesman, “What do you expect to achieve by showing a black flag to the President of the Republic?” I told him plainly: “Through this, the comrades of the Kazhagam will demonstrate that there are people in Chennai with the strength to compel a change in the declaration imposing Hindi. They will uphold Tamil Nadu’s honour.” He replied, “Even so, is it right to show a black flag to the President?”

A government that had never before climbed down, a Delhi authority that boasted, “We have dealt with the strike of the Central Government employees,” has now descended a little from its arrogance.

I firmly believe that the assurances and the letter given to us are not things to be ridiculed in political circles. They are political documents worthy of being inscribed in letters of gold in the history of Dravidam!

The fact that some people are examining them so closely shows that this is no trivial matter.

A very important Congress member spoke to me over the telephone and asked, “Have you read the Finance Minister’s assurance? Is there any change after reading it?” I immediately replied, “Merely pointing to various arguments will not extinguish the anger that has risen within us.” He asked, “What exactly do you want?” I said, “We need an assurance from Nehru.” “From Nehru?” he asked. “Yes,” I replied, adding that I hoped it would arrive within another day or two.

By then I had sent Sampath to Delhi. When I told him that he had to go to Delhi, his face fell. He was distressed, wondering why I was sending him there at that moment. I could see in his expression the anxiety that I might tell him, once he reached Delhi, that the agitation was unnecessary.

I have known Sampath since he was young. He has never been able to conceal his emotions. I asked him, “What is it? You seem afraid,” and explained why he had to go before sending him. I told him: “We have passed a resolution to show black flags. Therefore, when they see Sampath in person, every Member of Parliament and every minister in Delhi will watch him closely. I know that ministers will come to Sampath, hold his hand respectfully, and speak to him. It is with all this in mind that I am sending you.”

That is exactly what happened. After Sampath reached Delhi, fifteen Members of Parliament assembled and presented a statement to the minister. When Sampath telephoned me to ask whether he could sign it, I told him, “You may freely sign it. Let it go to Nehru; there is nothing to fear.”

I will say this generously: Nehru is the last democrat left in Congress circles. Though he knew the arithmetic, that the DMK had only two members in that great Parliament of five hundred, he sent Sampath the assurance we sought within four hours.

Pandit Pant, too, gave us an assurance; Nehru wrote us a letter. If we had shown black flags even after receiving these, we would have exposed ourselves to the accusation that we had forgotten our duty and violated discipline.

Earlier, a political party once took a letter received from a secretary and announced, “The assurance has been obtained; the war has ended.” But the person who wrote to us was Nehru. The same hand that writes to Khrushchev and Eisenhower has written and sent a letter to us! This alone is enough to show how far we have grown today.

This same hand will one day sign the charter of liberation for Dravida Nadu.

To put it colloquially: this is the hand; all the rest are mere pestles!

The reason this letter deserves respect is that it reveals a true understanding of democracy.

If anyone says, “This is not a letter worthy of respect,” the headquarters of his political party must be in Kilpauk.

Let me give an example. Suppose a toy falls onto the roof of Karunanidhi’s house. If it lies beyond reach, Karunanidhi will ask his wife to bring a ladder so that he can retrieve it. In the meantime, if the wind blows the toy down, what will he say? He will say, “The toy is now within reach, so there is no need for the ladder.”

He will not say, “No, no! I first asked for a ladder. Therefore I shall throw the toy back onto the roof and then retrieve it with the ladder!”

The President is staying here and resting because of ill health. I very much wish for him to recover.

But he is staying here neither by the grace of the police nor through the strength of the army. Our dignity serves as his police; our discipline affords him protection. The great Tamil people are grounded in such dignity.

The rulers had believed that Hindi could be forcibly imposed upon us. That confidence was shaken when we passed the resolution declaring that we would show black flags. One after another, they immediately offered assurances. The faces of the Congressmen here were at once darkened! I meet such Congressmen every day.

Pandit Pant, reputed to be the most unyielding of the ministers in Delhi, has given us an assurance. Some regard this as a humiliation and a disgrace to themselves, and therefore try to belittle our victory.

No comrade of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam need imagine that this magnificent victory has been stained or covered with dust.

Nehru, with political integrity, replied within four hours to the letter written by Sampath. But Subramaniam here knows only how to summon newspaper reporters and address them; the thought of inviting me for a discussion never occurred to him! Perhaps he fears that speaking to me would help the DMK grow.

Nehru, however, gives Comrade Sampath a letter and says, “Take this and show it to your Anna; ask him to stop the agitation.” This does not mean that I do not respect Subramaniam. I respect every human being. What I am saying is that we respect that letter.

On the fourth, the younger comrades gathered in front of Arivagam and demanded, “The agitation must be conducted; it must not be stopped.” We possess a force capable of raising impassioned slogans. We have an excellent formation ready to carry on the agitation. Herein lies our pride; herein lies our glory.

Then why did we not show the black flag? Was it because there was no one to hold it? No. We did not show it because the assurance we demanded had been obtained.

Even so, I ask the comrades of the Kazhagam to multiply this emotion and enthusiasm tenfold. When you see Congressmen, walk past them majestically with your heads held high. If they ask, “Was the agitation a victory?” say, “Yes, we have the letter.” If they ask, “Did you not show the black flag?” say, “Alas, what a pity; we did not!” I believe no other political explanation is necessary.

If industrial prosperity is lacking, Tamil Nadu may grieve a little and wring its hands. But if it is announced that Tamil is to be destroyed, even the coward here will become a warrior. The blind will gain sight; even the deaf will hear! The whole state will boil over, rise, and fight.

They have resolved: “If we live, we shall live with Tamil; otherwise, we shall all die!”

Minister Subramaniam made an appeal at a press conference. The most important part was the invitation he extended to us, and I appreciate it. He said, “A situation has not yet arisen in which only one party must fight on this issue. Tell us the shortcomings; we too shall join the struggle.” Today offers a good opportunity for him to act on that invitation.

All I wish to tell the minister is this: “At this moment, when we have demonstrated our dignity, please do not forget what you said. Undertake the effort to amend the Constitution.” In this effort, the Government of Madras will always have the support of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. They must convey to those in Delhi this awakening among us, the sight witnessed at Kodambakkam, and the determination to struggle. With the support of those who have resolved to live with Tamil, they must abandon their servile mentality. Our formation must grow in that manner.

As I look at all of you now, I am reminded of an incident during the French Revolution. In the frenzy of the French government, many fighters were imprisoned. The prison in which they were confined was called the Bastille. No one sent to that prison ever returned alive; not even a corpse came back. Those who died were buried there. If one dug afterward, all one would find was a skeleton.

At the height of the revolution, the revolutionaries resolved to demolish that prison. A great force was assembling and marching to destroy it. A smaller group approached from the opposite direction. Looking at the great force, they asked, “Where are you going?” The marchers replied, “We are going to break down the Bastille prison.” The smaller group immediately said, “We have already accomplished the task you have set out to perform. The Bastille has been reduced to rubble!”

In the same way, we, the smaller force, have shattered the arrogance of the North and today meet you, the great force. We have won a victory so significant that they are compelled to heed the voice we raise. Therefore, go home with enthusiasm. Strengthen the formation. Believe that this formation will soon attain Dravida Nadu.

## Translator's Notes

“Arivagam” was the DMK headquarters. Anna’s Kilpauk remark is a satirical suggestion that anyone dismissing Nehru’s letter would belong in the area associated with Chennai’s mental-health institution. The source prints the Russian leader’s name in an OCR-corrupted form; the contextual reference is to Nikita Khrushchev.

## English Translation

Speech delivered by Anna at a Madras district victory meeting on Triplicane Beach on 15 January 1959.

Comrades,

Anna looked upon the sea of people and asked, "Should the DMK assume responsibility for administering the Madras Corporation? Only those who wish it to do so should raise their hands and say yes."

The faces and hearts of roughly three lakh people brightened. A cry of "Yes" reached the sky and every hand rose.

I am glad that you have declared the DMK should assume responsibility. A Latin proverb says the voice of the people is the voice of God. You have delivered judgment; Congress now bears the responsibility of submitting to it.

When I recently spoke at Tiruvarur, my comments on the Madras victory were reported inaccurately, and *The Mail* wrote an editorial saying Annadurai was trying to evade administrative responsibility. It urged the DMK, as the party with the largest number, to come forward.

I offer respectful thanks for the editorial's goodwill and its confidence that DMK members can administer efficiently. If administration is entrusted to us, forty-five capable people stand ready to justify that confidence.

We will not run from responsibility; I have not run even from danger. But if Congress truly wants us not to evade responsibility, *The Mail* should first have instructed Congress. It should have said that preventing the DMK from gaining office by combining independents and others would be an uncivilised political betrayal.

In genuine democracy, the largest body of voters placed its first confidence in the DMK, its next in Congress, and then in other parties. If Congress approaches independents, strokes their desires and promises favours to prevent the DMK from taking office, no more refined betrayal of democracy can be imagined.

If Congress sincerely wants us to accept the mayoralty, it should issue a public statement, as Tamil Nadu Congress president Rajaram Naidu said, that because Congress is in the minority it will not seek office and that the majority DMK must govern. It should state unequivocally that it will form no coalition.

Friends warn that a manoeuvre may hide inside the phrase "we will not form a coalition." Congress might gather eight or ten independents, ask one to stand for Mayor, give that candidate its thirty-seven votes, purchase a few more, and support the arrangement while claiming it did not itself enter office.

Congress leaders should remove that suspicion tomorrow. Let them say: "Congress will sit in Opposition. Let the majority DMK administer, and we shall see whether it does so efficiently." I would accept the challenge without hesitation and point out from among those present a Mayor, Deputy Mayor, committee members and people capable of improving segregated settlements.

When our Mayor shakes the hand of an American ambassador or Russian diplomat, the visitor may ask, "Is he the Mayor of Madras?" He will answer, "Yes, and I belong to the DMK." Congress fears that message reaching Moscow, America or Britain.

We have no English newspaper; our speeches are not broadcast on radio; our journals do not carry our message to England; and we cannot freely travel abroad. But every foreign visitor could meet a DMK Mayor in Madras and carry our ideas away. Kamaraj and Rajaram Naidu understand that. They may therefore use many devices to keep us from the seat, and we must overcome them.

I ask you once more: even if they employ such manoeuvres, do you want us to overcome them and place a DMK comrade in the Mayor's chair? Those who do should raise their hands and say yes.

The voices cried yes and hands rose.

The people have judged. Congress leaders must accept that decision and refrain from obstructing us. Given the opportunity, our comrades possess the ability to accomplish good work for this city.

The taxes available in Madras are substantial. Many wealthy people evade them, while many poor people carry excessive burdens. One DMK policy will be to reduce as far as possible taxes upon poor people who struggle to pay, while collecting proper and sufficient taxes from those able to bear them, without fear, favour or misplaced indulgence.

Another policy is to remove filth and unhealthy conditions from poor neighbourhoods, obtain Government assistance, gather public support, bring some light to places now dark, and relieve at least part of the anxiety under which poor residents live.

Corporations spend heavily and open many hospitals, yet the source rhetorically claims that ninety of every hundred poor patients entering them proceed not home cured but toward the cremation ground. We will reform hospitals so that sick people enter and emerge healthy and strong.

Madras has long-standing Krishna and Pennar drinking-water schemes that remain only on paper. The DMK will argue and struggle with the State Government to implement them and provide the city with a sound water supply.

For these plans, securing the mayoralty alone is insufficient. Our comrades must sit in the Corporation, while DMK legislators remain in the Assembly to ask whether their schemes have been approved. Fortunately we are there, and now these councillors are arriving.

I hear political figures say, "Let them sit there and frame any schemes they please. Will they not be implemented only if our Kamaraj signs what they send?" I hesitate even to call such people politicians. A friend reports that a responsible minister said the DMK may entice poor people with promises of water, but that he is the person whose signature is required. What political clarity, generosity and democratic training!

If their position is that proposals bearing the DMK name will not receive signatures, let them extend that generosity and declare that they will no longer collect taxes from DMK supporters. They cannot take our money and taxes but refuse our public proposals.

If ministers have courage, let them announce to the world: "The DMK has captured the Corporation, so we will no longer send water through its pipes." Within twenty-four hours each could be brought before the Madras High Court.

Ministers exist to treat equally whoever occupies each public institution. They are not merely Congress ministers; Congressmen were made ministers by the people. My heart therefore still says no minister could have declared that schemes would be rejected merely because DMK members entered the Corporation. Yet friends insist they heard Kakkan himself say it.

If requests are ignored, fifteen DMK members sit in the Assembly. Do you think Karunanidhi will remain silent, Asaithambi will not rise, or Anbazhagan will not argue? If the Corporation is ignored, we will respond in the Assembly.

If Congress creates every kind of obstruction for you in the Corporation, tell us the next morning and fourteen legislators will answer the following day. I alone may abstain because I lack both training and taste for that style. Indeed, these younger brothers may tell me to stay home so they can go to the Assembly. Only I know the effort required over two years to restrain them. Congress will be disappointed if it expects obstruction to go unanswered.

With political generosity, civility and respect for democratic principle, Congress should not stand in the way of DMK responsibility.

I respectfully ask successful independents to cooperate and stand with us. I ask the Praja Socialists to respect the people's voice and support the DMK, which received the largest public backing.

I would like to ask the Communists too, though they may first seek direction from their higher leadership. If cooperation seems right, offer it; otherwise stand aside. If you oppose us, we shall manage.

I say this because in several municipal elections Congress would have lost the chairmanship had Communists cooperated with us. The same happened in Kanchipuram. Two Communist members entered that council. We met them repeatedly and explained that our thirteen could become sixteen; their two votes could prevent a Congress victory.

One asked whether we would accept Communist programmes. I replied that they had not yet learned to devise a programme frightening enough for me to reject, so they could submit whatever they wished. They asked whether our candidate would accept it. I said he possessed no independent programme of his own and would therefore follow the agreed one. They asked for time to consider, postponed the answer, and ultimately helped Congress win indirectly.

Tamil has many words for such conduct: betrayal, joining in order to ruin, or drawing close in order to destroy. We have no time to dwell upon them. We have far to travel and many thorns lie along the path. If one pierces the right foot, remove it with the left hand and continue.

I therefore do not insist that Communists cooperate. Kanchipuram taught me that lesson. If they choose to come, I welcome them, but I do not invite anyone to join the DMK. Let there be no misunderstanding.

I never seek borrowed goods for my organisation. I am patient in politics. I wait until fruit ripens and make sure it does not fall into another's hand; once ripe, it will fall into my lap. I do not ask people to join merely for this mayoral election. I ask them, for the public's sake, to support the party that has obtained such influence among the people.

The DMK will examine their proposals and accept those worthy of acceptance. Independents, anti-Congress candidates and Congressmen elected without Congress tickets should all help the DMK assume responsibility.

Though the DMK continues as Opposition at State level, give it responsibility in this smaller seat of government and test its work. If its administration is clean, retain it. If it is not, prove that to me. If we cannot perform, we shall hand responsibility to others.

I seek support under those conditions. You may ask how I appeal to people not present. You will meet them. Their workers and supporters should tell them: "Do not become accomplices in restoring Congress rule or place an independent without broad public support upon the seat. Help the DMK accept responsibility."

I know no other politically civil method of inviting them, so I appeal in goodwill. Let people of goodwill join the forty-five DMK councillors and one supporter, making forty-six, and enable the DMK to assume public responsibility.

The printed account adds that, after the Corporation victory under Karunanidhi's leadership and the emergence of the first DMK Mayor, Anna personally purchased a gold ring fitted to Karunanidhi's finger and presented it at the celebration.

## Translator's Notes

The speech was delivered on 15 January 1959 after the DMK won forty-five seats in the Madras Corporation. The prose introduction reports a crowd response and the later presentation of a ring; these editorial portions are distinguished from Anna's direct speech in translation.

The Latin maxim referred to is *vox populi, vox Dei*, rendered by its meaning, “the voice of the people is the voice of God.”

The hospital mortality statement is translated as campaign rhetoric from the source and should not be treated as a verified medical statistic.

An English verse printed near image 10 is illegible after Tamil OCR and appears only as an editorial footnote comparing later words attributed to Nehru. It is not reconstructed because it is not part of the recoverable speech argument.

“Borrowed goods” and the ripening-fruit image describe Anna's refusal to recruit temporary allies merely for office; they are retained as political metaphors.

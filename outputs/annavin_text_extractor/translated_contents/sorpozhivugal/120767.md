## English Translation

Speech 24, delivered in the Legislative Assembly on 12 July 1967

Honourable Speaker, while listening today to the many members who expressed their views on the grant for district administration, I wondered whether we might reorganise these proceedings more usefully. When members speak from the standpoint of their political parties, an immediate reply may be possible. But when they say that administration needs particular reforms, that institutions must be reorganised, or that defects in specified places must be corrected, ministers need not answer at once. Members could first be allowed to present their views. The minister concerned could then consult the appropriate officials, examine each suggestion and reply the next day. That would make the discussion more fruitful. When our friend Thiru D. N. Adhimoolam referred to a notice issued by a Collector, an instant answer from me could only have been the conventional government reply that it was said in jest and was not really so. Given another day, a minister could obtain the facts and answer properly, though this method might require the Assembly to sit for more days.

After the general budget debate, the demands for grants must first be disposed of within a fixed and shorter period. We could vote within two days, allow perhaps one speaker from each party, and complete the financial business. Thereafter members could discuss worthwhile ideas under each subject. Ministers could listen each day, collect information from their departments and reply on the following day. Otherwise, as happened during today's discussion of the District Development Council, those in office state one view, others state another, an hour passes, and everyone grows anxious to finish. We merely exchange political opinions without producing change on important questions. The Speaker and the party leaders should consider suitable changes to the rules and to the allocation of time for budget discussions.

Members have stressed one important truth: whoever governs, administration at the village foundation must work well. Only when it satisfies the public can government schemes be implemented and the government earn a good name. Just as a human body can perform its functions only when its nerves and blood vessels work properly, district administration must function through an ordered chain of villages, firkas, taluks and districts. Government plans alone cannot succeed when this administrative circulation fails. Any reorganisation must therefore be considered deeply. We must not make a hurried change because of dissatisfaction in one place and then discover that the former arrangement was better.

I differ from the claim that the entire arrangement of villages, firkas, taluks and districts was merely left behind by the British and is therefore unacceptable. In Tamil Nadu, the Chola kings organised villages into *kurrams*, several kurrams into *kottams*, and kottams into *mandalams*, which together formed the country. Historians praise Chola rule because these administrative stages were linked and kept in order. They also established boards for distinct functions: the wet-field board, garden board, water board, sluice board, gold board, accounts board and highways board. We sometimes imagine that irrigation and highways arrived only under the British, although specialised committees existed under the Cholas.

Comparable administrative structures exist today, but not their former vitality. Village people once participated directly in local administration. Twenty or thirty years ago, people joined together to carry out local work rather than waiting for the Collector or a legislator. Today almost every undertaking depends on government grants, assistance or loans; even when a sluice needs repair, people ask who will come and attend to it. I appeal to members to restore among villagers the feeling that they themselves must notice and care for such matters.

In the financial statement I spoke about the *Seerani*. Some imagined it as a Red Guard or another kind of army. My purpose was to stimulate this civic spirit through a service corps. It should exist not merely in every district but in every village, where it could accomplish much useful work. Difficult tasks requiring administrative expertise must, of course, remain with government, and government intervention in such work is proper and sometimes indispensable. But members should help revive the public spirit that existed twenty or thirty years ago.

Consider the District Development Council. Every village was given a panchayat in order to bring self-government to people's doorsteps. Yet many village panchayats are too small to obtain enough revenue to perform their duties. The Leader of the Opposition suggested reorganising small villages on a revenue basis. When government undertakes such reform, all parties must accept together that it is necessary; only then can any governing party act boldly. If, for illustration, 5,000 panchayats were reorganised into 3,000, the same opposition leader might later speak from a Congress platform and say, “Our villages had self-government, but after these people came they abolished 2,000 panchayats, all of them Congress panchayats.” Party leaders must instead agree that administrative reform will not be used for partisan rivalry. Political parties sometimes prevent an action and later complain that it was not done. Governments, in turn, fear the opposition such reforms may provoke.

Members said that these reforms have been requested for twenty or twenty-two years. One reason they were not carried out may be the Congress fear that we would exploit the change politically; if we too fail, a similar fear may be responsible. A broad agreement among all political parties would therefore be valuable. I do not say that government will act only if an agreement is reached. We are prepared to seek agreement, and I assure the House that the government will engage in the steps needed to carry out reform.

When I was a legislator, I attended a Chengalpattu District Development Council meeting only once in five years. The Collector presided, while those present sat waiting for his glance to fall upon them. I felt even that single visit had been unnecessary. The Collector asked me something in English, and after another English prompt, both of which the OCR has destroyed, I replied that I had nothing to say and sat down. I warmly welcome the proposal to turn the Council into a living institution and shall gather details about its reorganisation and the new circulation of life it needs.

Merely replacing the Collector as chair with a non-official will not transform it. The Collector and officials could sit as the governing side, while all members, whatever their party, sit opposite as in a legislature. Members could question them; officials could provide information; proposals could be offered and considered. But the Council cannot issue every requested order immediately without consulting government. If someone asks for a sluice or bridge, an order can be made only after determining whether funds have been provided. A former Chief Minister tried an experiment at Pagalmedu, granting on the spot whatever the public requested, whether a high school or a bridge. Officials wrote everything down, but the financial position could not support all those promises; Pagalmedu became both the first such conference and the last.

Congress members later undertook walking tours and pilgrimages on foot, saying they would hear grievances directly. Thiru K. Vinayagam interjected that he did not go because he represented Madras; his party evidently knew he would not join a foot march and left him out. Those tours too produced little result. Many petitions were submitted, more than ministers could even examine. Between leaving home in the morning and reaching my office, petitions accumulate in such numbers that there is scarcely time to identify the department concerned. People generally come with real and necessary public problems. Yet there is no interval in which to read each petition fairly, decide whether action is possible, send it to the proper department and follow up whether the work was completed. That is why the earlier proposal for deputy ministers has a practical basis: they could route petitions and follow them through. I mention the need here so that no one may later ask why the number of ministers was increased.

Members raised many points about village officers. Two months ago, a village officer spoke to me with striking candour. He said, “In the Assembly and at public meetings, people call the five or ten rupees we receive a large bribe. It is our lawful, customary right. Why call it bribery?” Shocked, I questioned him. He argued that government had long tolerated these payments because officers served for such low salaries, and he was ready to defend that position even in court. His attitude could only be called sincere. He writes an application for a land assignment and receives two rupees; if the applicant obtains land worth a thousand rupees, he asks what is wrong in paying two rupees for the petition. I do not cite this to decide whether his reasoning is right, but to show the frame of mind created among village officers by very low pay.

Simply adding three rupees to every village officer's salary, multiplied by the number of villages, is not a viable answer. The fragmented system under which each tiny village requires a separate officer should be reorganised. If several small units were combined so that officers could be paid a decent salary, work would proceed properly, the complaint of inadequate pay would diminish, and administration would become more efficient. Since every party is concerned, I intend soon to invite party leaders, hear their views and consider how far reform can proceed. Such measures would substantially remove the stagnation visible in rural areas.

Members also spoke about Collectors. A few days ago, wanting to know whether they were too old for strenuous work or young and physically capable, I asked about their ages. Apart from one or two, they are energetic younger people eager to accomplish something. Youth does not mean absence of experience; it is the age at which experience is acquired, not merely recounted. Older people may possess experience without always having the physical or mental vigour to apply it. Where Collectors are between forty and fifty, one can see work proceeding briskly.

In earlier times it was difficult even to see a Collector, which was why *jamabandi* was instituted. Many present Collectors, seen in the evening, would not be recognised as such unless a liveried attendant announced their arrival. They look as simple as college students or teachers and possess youthful energy. They embody society's new consciousness rather than reincarnating British authority. They seek satisfaction in asking what they accomplished during their tenure and how far their district improved. Legislators should give them greater encouragement, beginning by taking fewer private petitions to them while carrying public problems to them fully and seriously.

A District Development Council may need two days, as I think Thiru Sankarayya suggested; even two days may not suffice if it is empowered to discuss all the necessary issues. Such a forum would help Collectors do still more useful work. Thiru Hande observed that any government earns a bad name because of petty officers at the lower levels. Their narrow authority may tempt them to exercise it merely for personal satisfaction. On behalf of the people, not of political parties, we must ask that this tendency be removed. From petty officers to the District Collector, officials operate the machinery of government, but the poor villagers whose blood and sweat fall upon the soil provide the means by which that machinery runs. Every official must therefore come forward to give them the respect they deserve.

A welcome change is already occurring. Members themselves recognise it when they say *jamabandi* is no longer needed. Until twenty years ago, people could see officials only at jamabandi; now we visit them every day or they come to us. As a boy, I heard villagers say, “Jamabandi is next month; this goat is for it, and these six chickens have been bought for it.” Members speak from that memory, but the situation has changed. Once, when officials came, even the goat might seem to walk forward saying, “Take me.” As Thiru Karuthiruman said, people are no longer so submissive. If he were told today that the Collector had come for jamabandi, he would say he had no need to see him. His father might instead have gone around the village proudly proclaiming, “Sir called me; the Collector called me.”

The old generation has passed and a new one is emerging. Officials too are adapting themselves to it. Give them further encouragement and they will behave still better. One immediate way to encourage them is to withdraw the cut motions moved against this grant. I therefore ask the House to pass the grant so that district administration may function well.

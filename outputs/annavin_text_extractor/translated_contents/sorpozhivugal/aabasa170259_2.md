## English Translation

Speech 26

Date: 17 February 1959

Mr. C. N. Annadurai: Mr. Deputy Speaker, for two days, however many arguments we on this side have advanced and however earnestly we have appealed in various ways, the Honourable Minister has repeatedly given only one reply. He asked, “There is nothing in this Bill that you need fear. Why are you so afraid of it?”

As I considered whether that was a sign of his good intention or of a lack of clarity, I accepted that the words of a man possessing such sound knowledge and experience could not signify a lack of clarity and must instead signify good intention. Yet everyone acquainted with the subtleties of law, and every legal expert, ought to know that it is better for such a law not to be enacted at all than for a law enacted in this country to be administered according to one person’s good intentions.

While the Minister interrupts us and speaks angrily in the manner of saying, “We have the one reason needed to enact the law. No matter how you speak about this legislation, call it injustice; call it an atrocity too; say as much as you please,” and hastens to bring the law into force somehow, I wish to offer just one explanation. I wish to present it in the form of a story.

There was a wealthy gentleman. For his own protection, he employed a rough fellow. He told the man, “See that no one lays a hand upon me. It is your responsibility to ensure that not even a mosquito lands on me. For this I shall pay you a salary of one thousand five hundred rupees a month.”

From that day, the rough fellow began guarding the gentleman with a great cudgel in his hand. After all, he was being paid one thousand five hundred rupees. He guarded him without closing his eyes. But no one came. One day, a fly landed on the gentleman. Thinking, “Ah! Can this fly be allowed to sit on him? Am I to remain idle after receiving one thousand five hundred rupees?” the rough fellow struck at the fly with the great cudgel in his hand. But it was the gentleman who died. That is the story that now comes to my mind.

We are going to safeguard morality in the motherland. Henceforth, no obscene advertisement or repulsive poster must meet the eye. In order to say that these must be eradicated, you are creating a cudgel, this Bill. But this cudgel is to be entrusted to the police. Although the rough fellow swung his cudgel to kill the fly, it was the gentleman who died. Likewise, in order to eradicate obscene advertisements that threaten morality, you are about to entrust a great cudgel to the police regime. Whether morality is protected or not, you are going to beat democracy to death.

All my friends said that this is a measure you are undertaking to suppress the opposition. I shall say only this to them: “We accept the report you are issuing today. If you secretly imagine that through this law you can suppress all the propaganda of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and live forever, your intention will not be fulfilled.”

In South Africa, numerous kinds of devices have been employed to suppress non-white people, Black Africans in particular. A law was introduced under which they could walk along public roads only if they carried a permit in their hands; they had to take that permit with them merely to walk in the street. Justification was offered for that law too. Lawyers argued for it too. It was through numerical strength that the law was enacted in that country at the time.

If a non-white person went outside without a permit, a severe sentence of five or six years was imposed. What was the result of making punishment so cheap? People began committing very grave crimes, saying, “If we are to be punished anyway, let us at least commit a proper offence before receiving the punishment.”

Likewise, by continually inventing new acts for which the people of this country may be punished, you are today destroying respect for the law. You are removing the fear of punishment from people’s minds. Through new methods of repression, you are creating new offenders. Therefore, when this Bill is debated for the third time and passed, we do not wish to remain in this House. We are walking out.

## Translator's Notes

The Tamil source is a three-page legislative speech numbered 26 and dated 17 February 1959. All three scans were inspected to resolve OCR damage; the preserved Tamil source itself has not been altered.

The source title literally describes a “draft law for the prevention of obscenity.” “Obscene Publications Bill” is used as concise legislative English without claiming an independently verified official English title.

The fly-and-cudgel story is translated closely because it supplies the speech’s governing analogy: expansive police power may destroy democracy while purportedly protecting morality.

The damaged OCR phrase after “எதிர்க்கட்சியை ஒடுக்குவதற்கு” was checked against the scan. The scan reads “இது என்பது எனது மதிப்பிற்குரிய நண்பர்கள் எல்லாம் சொன்னார்கள்,” followed by a warning that the law cannot permanently suppress the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam’s propaganda.

The historical terminology used by the source for Black South Africans is rendered as “Black Africans” in current English. The discriminatory permit-law example and all political and historical assertions are translated in the speaker’s voice and have not been independently verified.

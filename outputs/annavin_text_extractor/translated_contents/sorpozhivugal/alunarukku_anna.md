## English Translation

## Welcome to Speaker U. Krishna Rao, 29 April 1957

Dr U. Krishna Rao was elected Speaker of the Legislative Assembly on 29 April 1957. Anna, as Leader of the Opposition, offered the following congratulations.

Mr Speaker, I am delighted at your election as presiding officer of this House and offer my heartfelt congratulations.

You ought to have been elected unopposed, but a contest became an unavoidable democratic duty. Even so, it ended in a manner that enhanced your distinction.

I have known your intellectual ability from my student days through all the years in which I have observed political activity. We are confident that your integrity will protect the dignity and honour of this House.

You come from a distinguished family and are a doctor of unrivalled reputation in your region. We know your learning and ability in medicine. Just as you give medicine to strengthen those who are physically weak, I ask you to show affection toward the politically weak and become a good political physician as well.

Democracy in our country is still in childhood. We in particular are new to the proceedings of parliamentary democratic institutions and lack experience. When a child errs or behaves mischievously, a mother does not beat it excessively; she corrects it and embraces it. Please treat us in the same way. Correct us with commands accompanied by your smile and make us useful democrats.

Political magnanimity is greatly needed in this country today. Such magnanimity must be given and received; it is not a one-way trade. Coming from a generous family and practising a generous profession, you cannot be unaware of that.

You now occupy the authority from which small errors can be corrected. Our being the Opposition does not mean that we oppose each and everything the Government does.

The Opposition may be compared to the bridegroom's attendant. At times even the bridegroom becomes irritated and shakes off his companion's hand. Yet the companion continues to hold it because he sincerely wishes the ceremony to proceed properly. In the same way, we sit in Opposition because we want government affairs to proceed in good order.

It is true that we are new and inexperienced in this Assembly. But we merely failed to obtain that experience at the proper time; we are not incapable of acquiring it.

You and this House know that we belong to an unusual political body. The unusual conditions prevailing in the country are what made us such an unusual political body.

As a good physician, you are not satisfied by treating the outward appearance of disease. You identify its root cause and prescribe accordingly. Likewise, it is not enough to see only the symptoms of the country's problems; their causes must be understood.

On our trains, those who occupy a seat first often tell later passengers that there is no room. Politics has similar arrangements. Somehow, stumbling and struggling, we have boarded this train called the Legislative Assembly.

Before we must ask those sprawled across the seats to rise and make room, please direct them with your very glance to draw in their arms and legs and acknowledge that we too are qualified to travel. Secure a place for us.

If we are given even a small part in protecting the honour and dignity of this House, I assure you that we shall discharge it properly.

## Reply to the Governor's Address, 7 February 1957

Now that people holding many shades of opinion have been permitted to express their views upon the Governor's address, I am proud to present the position of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

During this debate we heard members of one party offer good counsel and advice to another. But political parties cannot develop differences in the morning and blossom into love by evening. Even of love, Valluvar said, "This malady buds in the morning, grows all day, and blossoms in the evening." So it is in politics too.

I welcome replies from one party criticising another. By listening to varied views, we shall refine ourselves.

Some here said that our party should change its name. There are not only such people but others who wish us to disappear altogether, and still others who ask why we cannot stand shoulder to shoulder and cooperate with them.

How can I stand shoulder to shoulder with the Hon. Kamaraj? We could work hand in hand if necessary. I say that with reference both to bodily build and cast of mind. In working hand in hand, however, the rulers bear greater responsibility than those of us seated here.

The Governor's address contains no exposition of Government policy; we are told that policy and budgets are reserved for later debate. But a budget ought to distribute funds among schemes after policies have been announced and debated. Instead, the address offers no policy explanation and reads rather like a description of certain parties in the country.

No one denies the address's statement that two thousand breeding bulls will be supplied, nor do I say that 725,000 pump sets are unnecessary. But the address contains no plan or policy explaining how those bulls and pumps will produce rural development.

Our Finance Minister is minister for finance, justice and education. This arrangement may testify to his extraordinary ability and the rulers' confidence in him. Yet democratic convention abroad keeps departments that spend money away from finance ministers.

I do not suggest dividing his portfolios because he may lavish funds upon his own departments. On the contrary, fearing that people might accuse him of doing so, he may starve them. One member proposed enlarging the cabinet. The ministers may not relish the suggestion, though ruling-party members probably did. However flattering these responsibilities may be to his ability and even his youth, they should be divided. All three departments shape the people in different ways and are better entrusted to separate ministers.

Budgets must rest upon policy. If policy is discussed only after a budget has been prepared, how can that budget be altered to reflect the views expressed?

When Kamaraj became Chief Minister, I read that he answered a request for policy by saying, "Why explain policy? The old policies will continue."

At this point Kamaraj rose and said, "Yes."

I fear that the Governor omitted policy precisely because the old policies are to continue. We are therefore compelled to discuss those old policies.

Several speakers found fault with the act of finding fault. They resembled the cruel mother-in-law who calls back a beggar after her daughter-in-law has dismissed him, asks, "Who gave you that right? Is it not mine?" and then herself cries, "No alms; go away!" Ruling-party members spoke as though the Opposition had no right to criticise because that right belonged to them.

I hope the democratic habit of criticism grows within the ruling party. If it does, our own workload will diminish.

Some said we raise defects only so that we may later tell voters, "We heard, saw and reported all this, but the Government refused to listen." I reply that we did not win votes by promising to do this and that if elected. We won them by calling for the recovery of our motherland.

The address mentions Vinoba's Bhoodan movement, while the Government keeps postponing land-reform legislation. I fear it uses Vinoba as a curtain behind which to conceal that delay. If it truly believes in land reform, why hesitate to enact it immediately? Whom does it fear? I charge the Government with evading its responsibility to settle the land question.

Our position on industrial development is clear. A lady who spoke here asked, "Only if an aunt grows a moustache can she become an uncle, can she not?" I tell her that the aunt has grown a moustache and is being called uncle in neighbouring Kerala. She should understand that.

The ruling party must also keep in mind that the Opposition may become the Government at any time.

Not everyone who climbed Gingee Fort was Raja Desingh. Likewise, not everyone in Congress is a great martyr. When they forget this and call all of us servants of imperialism, it is not our faces but those of several people seated on their side that I see shrink.

Congressmen gain nothing from reciting past glory. As the age of zamindars and petty princes rapidly disappears, I warn that if they seek to practise zamindari in politics, that too will soon be ended.

The rulers need not even trouble themselves about what we say on industry. But they could tell the Central Government that a body such as ours exists and use that fact to fight for justice for Tamil Nadu.

Roach Victoria, formerly a member of this House and for a time a minister, has described from experience Delhi's attitude toward our industrial development. He says Delhi determines what may and may not be done in this State, and that our State Government is tied to the apron string of the Central Government. All we ask is that the string be loosened a little.

Many members complained that their allotted speaking time was insufficient. However experienced they may be, members cannot present every view broadly within the Assembly. When each department is debated, knowledgeable members from Government and Opposition should therefore be selected for an advisory committee. Debate would then be more effective. The ruling party should accept this suggestion, not for my sake but for democracy's.

When we say, "The North grows while the South declines," it is not mere alliterative ornament. However clever speakers may be, alliteration alone cannot deceive the people. Some who recognised this used absurd pairs of words such as *anda-gunda*, only demonstrating the disorder produced by misusing rhetorical alliteration.

Government statistics themselves disclose income in northern and southern agriculture and industry. They say both the income and number of working days available to northern agricultural labourers exceed those available in the South. Likewise, average annual industrial earnings are greater in the North. Should these disparities not be removed?

If necessary, we can act like the army's Sappers and Miners, clearing cactus and weeds from the path; you may then travel along it and find a way for the South to develop. The Government should use people like us to secure from the Centre the transfer of powers needed for Tamil Nadu's industrial growth.

I welcome the recent vigorous enforcement of prohibition and the discovery of many offences. But my innocent heart asks how so many offences remained hidden for so long.

The success of community-welfare programmes depends upon the self-confidence they create among people and the better life they bring to villages. Such programmes operate not only here; with American assistance, similar schemes are being implemented in about forty-five countries. Even if we cannot surpass their success, only an equal degree of success can justify the crores we spend.

I draw attention to the warning given by Bernard, an assistant director brought from America, about how Government should implement these schemes. He described their operation here: many participating officials do not understand the programme; some have no faith in it; and village-level workers who go among rural people behave like officials exercising authority.

My one reason for hoping that future implementation will not continue in the same fashion is that the Chief Minister himself has assumed responsibility for the department. I say so not because he is already doing good, but because he possesses a concern to do it.

Finally, I bring to the Government's attention the Planning Commission Deputy Chairman's recent severe criticism of its agricultural and food programmes. Great public dissatisfaction surrounds the Government's handling of food. It must understand that allowing that dissatisfaction to grow is not good.

## Translator's Notes

Despite the generated filename and printed heading, this source contains two speeches in reverse chronological order: the 29 April 1957 welcome to Speaker U. Krishna Rao and the 7 February 1957 reply to the Governor's address. They are separated explicitly in translation.

The source heading “ஆளுநருக்கு அண்ணாவின் பாராட்டு” appears inconsistent with the first speech, which praises the newly elected Speaker rather than the Governor. It remains unchanged in the preserved Tamil source.

The cited Tirukkural is Kural 1227. Its compact botanical metaphor is translated directly within the political analogy.

“அத்தைக்கு மீசை முளைத்தால் சித்தப்பா” is a proverb for a condition presumed impossible. The response alludes to the Communist government formed in Kerala in 1957.

The historical terms “Sappers and Miners,” “Bhoodan,” and “village-level workers” are retained in their period administrative and political senses.

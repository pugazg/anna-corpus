## English Translation

“Organisational Unity”—the organisation, the association and unity are indispensable: this was the purpose of Arignar Anna’s 1948 address.

The labour question has grown into one of the most important issues on the world stage and one capable of bewildering many minds. No one who desires justice, honesty, social peace and prosperity can neglect it or imagine that it can be destroyed through repression. The problem will grow stronger by the day and reveal ever newer forms. It will not fade by itself or collapse under attack. As common understanding and democratic feeling grow, the problem will only gain strength.

It has therefore become essential for every responsible person to think about labour organisation. The idea and practice of considering and deciding problems institutionally have now spread everywhere. Any organisation will possess strength and produce benefit only if it has unity. We must therefore attend to organisational unity.

An organisation is a common space, a camp, in which the thoughts of many are gathered and their powers brought together. A military camp requires different weapons and warriors. The number and variety of the weapons must keep growing if the camp’s usefulness is to increase. What use is there in heaping up swords without seeking shields, piling bows like a hill without arrows, or filling warehouses with rifles while neglecting ammunition? Even if all these exist in proper order and sufficient quantity, what use is the camp if there are no warriors capable of using them skilfully, or warriors exist but no commander can lead them? It may resemble a beautiful statue, but it cannot be a useful human being.

When an organisation is built upon certain definite principles, its benefit depends upon acquiring the strength to make those principles succeed. Principles arise in order to remove existing defects, transform conditions or attain some new good. If a workers’ organisation is built upon fundamental principles, it is formed to remove the workers’ deficiencies and hardships.

After the eighteenth century, the mode of industry changed. Before then, countries throughout the world had cottage production and made goods only for immediate need. During the eighteenth century, the Industrial Revolution displaced cottage production. Machinery developed; a few people employed many, expanded production, sold goods and accumulated profit.

Workers gathered in large numbers at one place and began to understand that the share of industrial profit remaining after their wages went elsewhere. When this awareness first arose, workers consoled themselves by thinking of fate, past deeds and the employer’s merit, believing that if they behaved with fear and devotion they would receive God’s grace. Some displayed their empty stomachs and dutiful labour, appealed to the good feeling and generosity of those who established the enterprise, and tried to obtain relief.

This method did not help for long. The generosity of a few cannot remove the growing troubles of a great multitude. Nor did workers’ lives gain any notable prosperity. Poverty poured down and suffering increased. Meanwhile, industrial change increased production and wealth, multiplied the conveniences of life and raised civilisation. A small number enjoyed all its benefits, while the great majority who formed the defensive foundation of that civilisation received none. Shock and turmoil arose in their minds.

As their condition worsened, the fact that not a drop of the conveniences created by their labour reached them produced an unquenchable fire. They realised that their life resembled bees deprived of the honey they had gathered. Their hearts ached. They discussed their grievances with one another, every day and in repeated gatherings.

The conversation began:

“What remedy can there be? Let us wait and see. Will his heart never soften? We must receive God’s grace; He will not abandon us.”

After some time it changed:

“How many times must we ask? How long must we keep begging? Is one who has no compassion for the poor even human? Is his heart stone or iron? Has he no pity at all?”

Then it changed again:

“They say it is impossible now. They say there is no profit as before.”

The conversation became sharper:

“He grew fat through our labour. Profit is heaped like a mountain. We wither in poverty while he lives in royal luxury. Is it not the profit produced by our labour that makes him a millionaire?”

“Kandan asked for at least a quarter-rupee increase in wages, and they refused.”

“Muthan went to speak, but they scowled and told him to go away without a word.”

“When Muniyan entered, they set a dog upon him.”

At last Kandan, Muniyan, Muthan and Kaathan concluded that there was no benefit in going individually and presenting their grievances. All of them must collectively state their common grievances and seek a remedy together. That was the proper path. Organisations arose.

It took nearly half a century for workers to reach this conclusion.

They recognised their hardships, debated their causes, discovered the real causes, attempted individual remedies and failed. Only then did they conclude that they must present their grievances institutionally and seek relief. They saw that their mode of life, mode of labour and lack of necessities were the same for all workers. Since everyone suffered the same illness, they decided everyone must seek the same medicine and formed organisations.

An organisation is not merely necessary but useful only while this common character remains unspoiled. Organisational unity is possible only when that shared feeling is protected. Kandan seeks justice for Murugan as well as himself. Muthan asks for justice for all three. Each seeks justice on behalf of all the others. This is the organisation’s defining quality.

Unity can exist only when it is clear that everyone belonging to the organisation benefits through it. If Murugan acts without Kandan’s knowledge and Muthan acts without Murugan knowing, each proceeding separately, the organisation will break. Commonality is the organisation’s fortress.

For this shared character to remain strong, the organisation must be built upon principles of the highest justice.

From a family to a Government wielding the sceptre, all are organisations. They contain a form of common character, common work, shared responsibilities binding everyone and common benefits. If any of these is damaged, organisational unity will certainly suffer.

A younger brother may spend ten rupees without his elder brother’s knowledge. Their affection may prevent it from harming the family for a few days, but only for a while, not forever. If the younger brother makes a habit of acting selfishly without the elder knowing, no amount of talk about affection, old family stories or appeals to Rama and Lakshmana can maintain family discipline. The family will split and the brothers separate.

If that can occur between brothers in a family, an organisation cannot be protected from breaking until the end by affection alone between a leader and deputy leader, or between the leader and other members. Organisational unity depends upon all its members sharing one purpose and working for the same definite principle. Nor can that unity be false. If it is, even a very ordinary difficulty will become a major obstruction.

An organisation requires principles just as a building requires appropriate materials. Just as the difficult work of construction requires skilled builders, forming an organisation upon specific principles requires organisers with ability and determination. They need the capacity to identify the principles that form its foundation and a vision of how the organisation should be structured.

Strong iron girders, different kinds of timber, high-grade cement, handsome brass fittings and well-mixed lime mortar may all be required. But organisers must know where, in what measure and by what method to use each so that the building has excellent structure, strength and usefulness. Only such people can establish sound organisations. After construction, they must also possess the skill and concern to prevent the structure from deteriorating.

A small window may not seem very important in a great mansion. Even without it, the mansion remains. As long as the foundation, cross walls and upper structure are sound, whether the window exists, is removed or becomes damaged may not immediately harm the mansion. Yet those who care about the mansion will look after even that small window and prevent its decay.

They will worry not only about the window but even if one of its bars is damaged. They foresee that the bar may fail, then the window may be removed, and a thief, wind or rain may enter and cause loss and hardship to the mansion. Though a window bar is not the mansion’s foundation, its owner attends to it carefully.

It is the same with organisations. If the unity and strength of workers’ organisations are to remain undamaged, every member must be watched over like the mansion owner attending even to a window bar, and the structure must be preserved. Failure to follow this method has sometimes weakened unity in labour organisations and scattered their fighting strength.

It is not enough to install great timbers as pillars in the hall. One must also prevent termites from entering the smallest holes in ordinary wood. Once termites begin eating, both redwood and any other timber will decay, and the organisation with them.

An organisation needs both strength to support and strength to endure. It must be structured accordingly and contain people capable of gathering both kinds of strength and using what has been gathered. The conviction that one person’s presence strengthens another must never diminish. Only that conviction can preserve organisational unity.

Changes in principles and conditions may endanger unity. Questions also arise during practice—who should perform which task and how it should be done—and may damage it. But as a mansion remains while its foundation is strong, an organisation will survive while its principles remain strong. Even so, one must not be indifferent when circumstances arise that could destroy unity.

Workers’ organisations sometimes arise merely from grievance and the anger naturally produced by it. These are not sound foundations. We labour in order to live, yet there is no life. Others labour and live without obstruction. The proper foundation is: all of us shall live; we shall labour in order to live; we shall abolish the injustice by which one person’s life is built upon another person’s labour.

An organisation built upon these principles must keep its noble ideal foremost. Its practical work should be arranged so that bitterness and inner fury do not arise among members, and no one feels that another’s task or conduct is wrong. Everyone should receive work appropriate to their ability, without injury to the organisation’s founding principles. Conducted this way, unity will not be damaged and results will certainly follow.

The difficulties arising after an organisation becomes strong are greater than those encountered while its strength is growing. The unity present during growth may face danger once growth is achieved. Questions then arise: What shall be done with this strength? How? Through whom? When? These can threaten unity.

At every such critical moment, the organisation’s founding idea must receive first place. Only then will the organisation avoid disintegration.

Organisational unity is not an achievement one obtains through a single effort and then admires while saying there is nothing more to worry about. It is a wondrous living force that can be protected only by remaining constantly alert, repairing defects whenever they arise and ensuring at every moment that the founding idea is not damaged.

An organisation’s strength and usefulness depend upon its unity, its feeling of oneness. A veena may be of the highest quality and its musician highly accomplished, but if a single string is loose at one point, the instrument’s tone will be spoiled and the musician’s music ruined. Organisational unity is like knowledge of *sruti* in music. It must be watched with the greatest care.

## Translator's Notes

The historical term “ஸ்தாபனம்” is translated as “organisation” and refers primarily to trade unions and collective institutions. The source’s “வாள் மட்டும் போரென” appears OCR-damaged; the surrounding paired weapon examples establish the intended contrast between swords and shields. “அணைக்க முடியாத தயை” is contextually likely “தீ,” an unquenchable fire, rather than compassion; the source remains verbatim and the contextual reading is logged. The final comparison uses *sruti*, accurate pitch and tonal alignment in Indian music, as the governing metaphor for institutional unity.

## English Translation

The Honourable C. N. Annadurai:

Mr Speaker, since the President, under Article 3 of the Constitution of India, has referred to this Assembly for its views the Parliamentary Bill described in the Schedule and entitled the “Madras State (Alteration of Name) Bill, 1968,” I move the following resolution:

“This Assembly expresses its considered view that all the provisions contained in the said Bill be accepted exactly in word and substance.

“This Assembly further desires that the Bill be introduced in Parliament and enacted into law as early as possible.”

Honourable members have moved certain amendments to this resolution. In particular, the honourable members Mr M. P. Sivagnanam and Mr K. Ramamurthi have submitted amendments. After discussing the matter with them and accepting the substance of their amendments, the government moves the following amendment: substitute the following for the second portion of the resolution:

“Recommending that wherever the words ‘Tamil Nad’ occur in this Bill, they be replaced by the words ‘Tamil Nadu,’ this Assembly expresses its considered view that, subject to this amendment, it accepts the provisions contained in the said Bill.”

Mr Speaker, although the honourable members advanced many good reasons for their amendments, I first thank them for generously accepting the amended resolution now brought forward by the government. Since this matter has already been discussed in this House at great length and with great clarity, I made no speech when I moved the resolution. Even now, I do not intend to speak at length.

There was indeed a time when we had to search for evidence, cite authorities, and argue the case for the name Tamil Nadu. Today, however, we should take great pride in announcing that everyone in this House has joined together to accept it without bringing all those former circumstances back to mind.

The honourable Mr Adhimoolam spoke in detail about occasions in the past when the wish that this state should bear the name Tamil Nadu was rejected. At this joyful moment, we should forget all those situations that arose in the past. We should be very happy that everyone has now united and welcomed this in a manner that will convey to the Union of India that this entire House stands united on behalf of Tamil Nadu in support of the Bill.

Even while I say that we should not recall every incident of the past, I believe it is necessary to remember one event alone, an event that can make the heart ache.

An elderly man from Virudhunagar, Sankaralinga Nadar, fasted for seventy-five days insisting that we must secure the name Tamil Nadu, and he sacrificed his life. About fifteen days before his death, I had an opportunity to visit him. I looked at the condition in which he lay and pleaded, “Give up your fast. We shall look after the campaign for the change of name. Do not continue this fast.” He replied, “No good will come from my remaining alive; perhaps some benefit may arise if I die.”

Through that determination, the elder sacrificed himself and created a great awakening in the country. It is only fitting that we remember him at this moment, and I am duty-bound to pay tribute to his steadfastness through this resolution.

My friend M. P. Sivagnanam feels as deeply about retaining the final sound in “Tamil” as I do. But I ask him to write it in English and see how it appears, and to ask a few people to read it. Whichever English letters are used, they cannot pronounce the “ழ” sound. For example, people in the North pronounce “Dravida Kazhagam” as “Dravida Kajagam,” because the final “ழ” sound is unavailable to them.

Not only that: one of the very great leaders of the Indian Union once advised us, “You keep saying Tamil Nadu. When you say it, call it Tamil for now. We shall practise the ‘ழ’ sound and consider the rest afterward.” Taking their practical difficulty into account, the name has been written in English in this form because the Tamil “ழ” cannot be represented accurately there. If M. P. Sivagnanam reflects upon it, he will realise that this is a great distinction for our Tamil.

No matter how many letters other languages employ, they cannot reproduce the sound of this one Tamil letter. This is a distinction of our Tamil in which we may all rejoice. The “ழ” sound should not remain only with us; Tamils must interact with others in such a way that people of every country may acquire it. I have even demonstrated to some people how the tongue must be curled and how it must touch and withdraw without quite touching. When I demonstrate, they imitate it. But when asked to do it again, the “ழ” sound refuses to come.

When we write the name, we write only “தமிழ் நாடு,” Tamil Nadu. When it comes into English, I too feel some dissatisfaction because the “ழ” sound cannot be represented. Yet what we must chiefly recognise now is the profound change we have achieved by replacing “Madras State” in the Constitution with “Tamil Nadu.”

With that sense of fulfilment, I thank everyone for the unanimous support received from every section and every party. I request everyone to accept this resolution.

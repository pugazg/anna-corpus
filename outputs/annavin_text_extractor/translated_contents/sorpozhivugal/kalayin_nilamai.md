கலையின் நிலைமை

11-2-1944-ல் ஈரேஈட்டில் நடைபெற்ற தமிழ் மாகாண நாடகக்
கலை அபிவிருத்தி மாநாட்டில் கலந்துகொண்டு அறிஞர்
அண்ணா அவர்கள் நிகழ்த்திய சொற்பெருக்கு.

நாடக அபிமானிகளும், நாடகக் கலையிலே ஈடுபட்டுள்ள
தோழர்களும், இசைவாணர்களும், கலா ரசிகர்களும் நிரம்பியுள்ள
இந்த மாநாட்டிலே, எனக்குப் பேச ஒரு சந்தர்ப்பம் கிடைத்தது
பற்றி மிக்க மகிழ்ச்சியடைகிறேன். நாடகக் கலையின்
முன்னேற்றத்திற்காக இந்த மாநாடு நடைபெறுகிறது. காலையிலே
தலைவர் சர். சண்முகம் அவர்கள் சொற்பொழிவாற்றினார். வேறு
பல அன்பர்களும் பேசினர். இயலை வளர்க்கவும், இசையை
வளர்க்கவும் அமைப்புகள் இருக்கின்றன. நாடகக் கலை
வளர்ச்சிக்கான அமைப்பும் முயற்சியும் இல்லை. எனவே, என்
நண்பர் தோழர் சிவதாணு, இதற்கான ஓர் தனி மாநாட்டை, முதன்
முறையாகக் கூட்டியது பற்றி நான் அவரைப் பாராட்டுகிறேன்.

நகரசபைகளில் நாடக அரங்கு :

நாடகக் கலை முன்னேற்றத்திற்குப் பல காரியங்கள்
நடைபெற வேண்டி. இருக்கின்றன. முதலிலே ஊருக்குத்
தியேட்டர் வசதி வேண்டும். இப்போது எல்லாம் சினிமாக்
கொட்டகைகளாகி விட்டன. நாடகத்திற்கெனத் தனி
தியேட்டர்கள் இல்லை. திருச்சி நகரசபை ஒன்றில் மட்டுமே தனி
தியேட்டர் அமைக்கப்பட்டி ருக்கிறது. அதுபோல ஒவ்வொரு நகர
சபையும் செய்ய முயற்சி எடுத்துக்கொள்ள வேண்டும். சர்க்காரும்,
செல்வவான்களும், நாடகக் கலை அபிவிருத்திக்கு உதவி அளிக்க
வேண்டும். இவைகளுக்காகப் பொது மக்கள் முயல வேண்டும்.

நடிகர்களுக்கும் தொடர்பு இருப்பதில்லை. இப்பொழுது நடிகர்
களிடம் நன்மதிப்பும், தொடர்பும் ஏற்பட்டிருக்கிறது. இத்
தொடர்பும் நீடிக்க வேண்டும், இதனால் தக்க பயனும் ஏற்பட

அவருடைய நடிப்பு எனக்குப் பிடித்ததால். என் பாராட்டுதலுக்கு
அவர் பதில் கூறும்போது சொன்னார், “என்னமோ, நாங்கள்

ஒரு நடிகர் இவ்வாறு கூறவேண்டுமென்று நான் யோசித்தேன்.
அவருடைய வேதனையான வாழ்வு அவரை அப்படிக் கூறச்
செய்தது என்பதை உணர்ந்தேன்.

நாடகக் கலைஞரின் வாழ்க்கை :

பெரும்பாலான நடிகர்களின், நாடகத் தொழிலாளரின்

வாழ்விலே, நடிகர்கள் இருந்து கொண்டி ருந்தால் நாடகக் கலை

சம்பளம், வாழ்க்கை வசதி முதலியன கிடைக்க வேண்டும்.
இலாபப் பங்கீடும் தரப்பட வேண்டும். இவைகளைச் செய்யப்
பெரிய கம்பெனிகள் முன்வருமா என்பது சந்தேகம். அவைகள்
கலை அபிவிருத்தி என்றால், சீனுக்கு மேல் சன், கலர் விளக்குக்கு
மேல் கலர் விளக்கு அமைப்பது என்ற முறையிலே வருமானத்தை
மேலும் மேலும் நாடக இயந்திரத்திற்கே செலவிடுகிறார்கள். அந்த
இயந்திரம் பிரமாண்டமாகி விடுகிறது. அதன் பசியை அடக்கவே
அவர்கள் முயலுகிறார்கள். ஆகவே அவர்கள் நடிகர்களின்,
நாடகத் தொழிலாளரின் வாழ்க்கை அபிவிருத்திக்கான வழிவகை

## English Translation

Speech delivered by Arignar Anna at the Tamil Provincial Conference for the Development of Dramatic Art, held at Erode on 11 February 1944.

I am very happy to have been given an opportunity to speak at this conference, filled as it is with lovers of drama, friends engaged in dramatic art, musicians, and connoisseurs of the arts. This conference is being held for the advancement of dramatic art. This morning its president, Sir Shanmugham, delivered an address, and several other friends also spoke. Organisations exist to develop literature and music, but there has been neither an organisation nor a concerted effort for the development of drama. I therefore congratulate my friend Comrade Sivadhanu for convening, for the first time, a conference devoted specifically to this purpose.

## Theatres in Municipalities

Many things must be done for the advancement of dramatic art. First, every town needs proper theatre facilities. Existing halls have now largely become cinema sheds; there are no theatres reserved for stage drama. Only the Tiruchirappalli Municipality has established a separate theatre. Every municipality should attempt something similar. The government and wealthy people must assist the development of dramatic art, and the public must press for these measures.

## Respect for Actors

In former times, actors generally received no respect and were mocked. There was little connection between the public and performers. Actors have now gained goodwill and a relationship with society. This relationship must continue and yield proper benefit.

This morning many spoke enthusiastically about the greatness of dramatic art and the service of actors. A week ago, I attended a play in a village. Because I liked the performance of one actor, I spoke in his praise. In replying, he said: “Well, we remain actors. But I swear that we shall not make our children actors.” I wondered why an actor should say this. I then realised that his painful life had driven him to it.

## The Life of a Theatre Artist

This is the condition of most actors and theatre workers. How can dramatic art advance while its performers endure lives filled with suffering? My friend N. S. Krishnan, who spoke earlier, explained this beautifully. If dramatic art is to develop, the actors who constitute its essential element must receive good wages, decent living conditions, and a share of the profits.

It is doubtful whether the large companies will undertake these measures. For them, “developing art” means adding scene upon scene and coloured light upon coloured light. They pour an ever-growing share of their income into the machinery of theatre. That machine becomes enormous, and all their efforts go toward satisfying its hunger. They therefore make no provision for improving the lives of actors and theatre workers. Their annual account books do not encourage development of that kind.

## A Cooperative Company

A limited company with shareholders should be formed to stage reformist plays. Its income should be distributed among theatre workers as bonuses and profit shares. Only then will actors possess contented minds, performances acquire refinement, and dramatic art develop.

Even the raga Kambhoji sung by an artist whose life is filled with misery will sound like Mukhari. In the same way, an actor needs peace of mind, security in life, and certainty in the profession. I have seen many fine actors lose their voices or violate a company rule and then, despite their former position, struggle in the streets. I have also seen “former actors” who were famous ten years ago but today possess no means of livelihood. Their only remaining pleasure is to stroke their moustaches and say, “I too once played the royal lead.”

If actors’ lives collapse in this manner, how can educated and cultured people enter the profession? I have already told you that an actor friend said, “I will not make my son an actor.” This is the reason. Those concerned with developing dramatic art must therefore attend first and foremost to the welfare of actors and theatre workers. Only then can drama advance.

## The Government and Theatre Companies

I said that the government and the public should assist dramatic art. If theatre artists are to receive such help, they must offer useful work in return. Only then will assistance come. Most plays staged today are mythological. What benefit do people receive from watching them? Do these plays not serve merely to cultivate superstition? If plays that promote knowledge are staged, then, as I said earlier, the public, government, and wealthy citizens must support theatre artists.

The companies should therefore stage mainly dramas of social reform. That is true artistic development. This feeling has spread everywhere, even among theatre proprietors.

## The Desire for Reformist Drama

This morning I spoke with Nawab T. S. Rajamanickam. Referring to his play *Ayyappan*, he said: “Although it is mythological, I have introduced many changes and reformed it as far as possible.” Why did my friend Nawab Rajamanickam feel compelled to say that he had reformed it “as far as possible”? Because he has recognised that the country wishes to see reformist plays. His statement means that he is trying to reshape dramatic art accordingly.

This feeling is bound to spread. Very soon, public enthusiasm for reform will grow to the point that mythological plays cannot be staged. It is easy to bring that about. I ask the public to support reformist drama so firmly that companies can no longer complain that such plays produce less revenue.

Sir Shanmugham said: “Whichever play one watches, Narada keeps coming and going! I do not care to watch such mythological plays. Thousands of plays containing reformist ideas could be created.” There are three types of people regarding reform. One group says that reform must never occur. They are worthy of our worship! A second says reform is needed but laments that it cannot be achieved. They deserve our sympathy. A third says reform is needed and must therefore be carried out. They are our people.

I believe the first kind cannot exist in this country. Even if one or two such persons remain, they may reveal themselves privately tomorrow, but after witnessing the feeling present here today, they will not publicly say that reform is unnecessary. Enthusiasm for reform now fills the country. Theatre artists must recognise it. Continuing to stage more and more mythological plays will not benefit society.

Sir Shanmugham said that superstitious customs must be abolished. Yet the very superstitions that connoisseurs say they wish to eliminate, and the caste differences that artists claim they want to remove, are planted among the people through mythological drama. That is why we say such plays should not be staged.

## A Difference of Opinion About Artistic Beauty

This morning Sir Shanmugham said that although the *Ramayana*, the *Mahabharata*, and the *Periya Puranam* are fabrications, they contain art, and that burning them would not abolish superstition. There is a difference between his view of art and the view held by me and my associates. I had suspected this before; after hearing his morning address, the difference became clear.

We ask that the *Ramayana* and *Periya Puranam* be burned as a symbol of condemnation—to demonstrate that people should reject and denounce them because they are fabrications, corrupt the public mind, and enable one community to dominate another. We have never said that merely burning them will cause superstition to vanish. We are not so foolish. We call for placing them in the fire to demonstrate public condemnation of works that support the supremacy of one group.

Sir Shanmugham compared this with an invader who once burned a library. I ask him whether we, who were trained by him, would regard our act as such a degrading deed.

Let me make another proposal to him and to connoisseurs of art. When we burn those texts as an act of condemnation, let the connoisseurs say: “Stop! Page 63 contains a beautiful description of a river and displays magnificent artistry.” We shall tear out and preserve that page. Let them say: “Do not burn page 93. Its comparison between human deceit and a mirage is beautifully expressed; it is rare art.” We shall leave that passage unburned. We shall burn the other portions that are obscene, contrary to reason, and calculated to cultivate racial arrogance. I ask whether the connoisseurs will agree to that.

Sir Shanmugham said every mythology contains passages that foster superstition as well as passages of artistic beauty. Great scholars like him can distinguish between them; ordinary people cannot. Here, more than ninety out of every hundred connoisseurs are themselves illiterate. Let scholars extract and publish collections of the artistically beautiful portions of these mythologies and epics. We shall welcome them. This is the proper moment to undertake that work.

## In *Kanda Leela*

Even I find a few enjoyable moments in the mythological plays staged by our Shanmugham company. Their *Kanda Leela* presents a scene on the hill at Tiruttani. The temple priest says wearily: “Could this wretched god not have remained below? He has climbed up and seated himself here, taking the life out of me!” They did not insert this as Self-Respect propaganda. It may have been included merely for comedy, or to show that the public believes temple priests possess such a disposition. Yet this is the portion of the mythological *Kanda Leela* that gives me pleasure.

Similarly, whenever Comrade Bhagavathi performs as Ravana, Hiranya, Surapadman, or Kamsa, he appears to my eyes like a Dravidian warrior. His speech, gait, acting, bearing, and majesty all create that impression for me.

## The Attitude of the People

Just as Sir Shanmugham finds tasteful passages in mythology, I too find moments to enjoy in mythological plays. But are Sir Shanmugham and I their only spectators? The public watches them. Can ordinary viewers make these distinctions? Can they avoid absorbing the obscene or degrading ideas?

Our Muslim proprietor may watch a *Ramayana* play, but he will not begin worshipping with cries of “Rama! Rama!” Allah is his deity and the Quran his scripture. Watching the *Ramayana* therefore will not induce him to regard Rama as God. Nor will our Christian friend David watch *Kanda Leela* and proclaim Kandan to be God; his deity and religion are different. Mythological plays thus do less damage to a Muslim or Christian.

The position of our own people is different. They watch believing that all the gods represented in mythology are their gods. Is it not their minds that are consequently damaged? This is why we say mythological drama harms social progress.

Sir Shanmugham said that to appreciate the flavour of the *Silappathikaram*, one must view it with the eyes and mind of people born two thousand years ago, when the work appeared. I am incapable of acquiring such eyes and such a mind. I would gladly cultivate the vision and thought needed to imagine how the world will be two thousand years hence, but I have no desire to recover the outlook of two thousand years ago.

If someone followed the conventions of the *Silappathikaram* today and left Kannagi to visit Madhavi’s house, Kannagi might leave Kovalan alone, but she certainly would not leave Madhavi alone! I say this only to demonstrate that many ideas in ancient stories are unacceptable today.

## Art Must Not Become a Net

I wish to tell Sir Shanmugham that thousands of young people in Tamil Nadu have now reached this outlook. I also acknowledge that my views on reform were shaped by hearing the speeches he delivered in earlier years.

Our general position on art is this. If art is an instrument that brings joy to the human mind, we have no objection to it. But art has become a net used by one community to subjugate another. Therefore, however powerful the force behind that net may be, we have resolved either to tear it to pieces or to remain unconcerned even if we ourselves are torn apart in the attempt. Thousands of young people in Tamil Nadu are ready to dedicate themselves to this cause. I place that fact before our president.

## Plays for the Growth of Knowledge

We have sung and danced long enough about miraculous tales in which a woman became a skeleton; the only result has been that our own women have been reduced to skeletons. Hereafter, stage plays portraying the pitiful life that reduces women to such a condition.

We have performed the story of Kannappar plucking out his eye and placing it upon the deity. That is enough. Now stage a play showing how the man who builds a temple and performs its consecration plunders the town. Portray life itself. Stage rational and educational dramas that reveal the poor person’s tears, the widow’s sorrow, and the arrogance of religious brokers.

Because I have spoken against some ideas expressed by Sir Shanmugham this morning, I ask none of you to imagine that I am attacking him. He will not think so, and therefore I need not make that request of him.

## The Responsibility of the Audience

I know that theatre artists face many difficulties because of the differing opinions found among the public. In some companies the actor playing the hero may support the Congress, the harmonium player may belong to the Justice Party, and the heroine may possess Self-Respect convictions. Quarrels arise naturally in such a troupe.

In some towns audiences demand a Congress song; elsewhere they demand Self-Respect songs. Large companies do not face this trouble because scene follows scene in rapid and dazzling succession, leaving the public no time to create it. Most theatre artists, however, do experience these pressures.

Actors can conduct their profession without such difficulties only if they understand how public attitudes are changing. We must make theatre artists recognise that the country now wishes to see reformist drama. I believe they will begin doing so. I trust that enthusiasm for reformist plays will soon spread everywhere and that dramatic art itself will thereby develop.

## Kalaivanar’s Skill

Driven by their enthusiasm for reform, some artists introduce reformist ideas even into mythological plays. Our friend N. S. Krishnan has worked skilfully in this field. In one film—I think it was *Manikkavasagar*—he plays an accountant. Pointing to the pen in his hand, he tells someone: “Hey! Do you know what I have in my hand? It is a pen, not sacred *darbha* grass!” Consider how profound that idea is.

I express my happiness at receiving an opportunity to address this assembly of artists, offer my respects to the conference organisers who gave me the opportunity, and conclude my brief speech.

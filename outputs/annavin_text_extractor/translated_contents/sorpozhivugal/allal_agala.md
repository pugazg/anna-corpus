## English Translation

Summary of a speech delivered by Arignar Anna in Madras's 94th Ward on 17 May 1960.

**Comrades!**

All the people living in huts here today have come from rural areas. Very few would have been born and brought up in the city. Agriculture failed in the countryside; there was no industry; the cultivation carried on for generation after generation ceased to yield. They therefore left the villages where their families had long lived and settled here, hoping that they might at least find work in the city.

If you go to the beach in the evening, call over those who sell groundnuts and peas and ask, “Which place are you from?” You will understand the truth of what I say. Most will be from South Arcot district, some from Thanjavur district, and others from North Arcot district.

They laboured to grow crops but received no return. Unable to withstand and manage that famine, and with no other means of livelihood in their villages, they came here. They live by carrying loads, pulling carts and selling vegetables. Congress ministers do not know this truth.

Women do not attend meetings addressed by Congress ministers in large numbers. Even if five or six come, they will not sit below but on the stage. They may be women in appearance, but inwardly they possess an arrogance fiercer than that of men. The mothers who attend our meetings are not like that. They have abundant industry and integrity. One can see no ornament upon them other than a smile. They live by hard work, not on interest from money or on tax revenue.

There is no means of livelihood in the village; even after coming to the city, they find no employment. Why? There is no industry here. Madras ministers have no authority to establish a new factory. Even if a cottage industry is begun here, the decision ends in Delhi. These ministers possess no authority of any kind.

A tiny bird lives in a hollow in a tree. While it goes out in search of food, another bird with a sharp beak enters the hollow. When the first bird returns after finding and eating its food, the intruder remains concealed inside and strikes it with that sharp beak. Relying on the strength of its beak, it drives the first bird away, saying, “This is mine now; henceforth your place is outside.”

The bird deprived of its home does not go elsewhere. It circles the same hollow again and again: “My ancestors pierced this tree with the strength of their beaks and created this place. I was born here. My mother lived here. You occupied it while I went in search of food. Give me my place! Give it to me!”

But the bird inside will not emerge, for fear of losing the hollow. It merely thrusts out its beak and threatens the other bird. Whenever you look at a hollow in a tree, you may see this scene among sparrows.

While “Tamil Nadu” had gone out in search of food, a large bird entered its home. With its powerful beak, that bird now strikes the Tamil bird returning from its search and refuses to let it enter. So the Tamil bird circles the tree again and again. Though the tree has a thousand branches, it does not perch upon them; it circles only its hollow.

The bird that entered while Tamil Nadu was away is “All-India”—“One India.” This new imperialism must depart and make room for our people to live. It is when we demand this that they begin to strike us with their large beak. Our speech is the screech produced by those blows.

Congressmen say these people repeat only the same thing. It does not mean we speak of this because we know nothing else. We know that Khrushchev and Macmillan are meeting in Paris, and we can speak about it. We can also discuss the meeting between Eisenhower and de Gaulle. But our first problem is how the small bird can escape from the large one. Other matters are unnecessary for now. That is why we repeatedly say, “Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians.”

Look at the sparrow: it circles only the hollow. Until it regains its place, it will not leave. Once it has a place, however, if it is a parrot it will speak for joy, and if it is a skylark it will sing. Likewise, first let Tamils—Dravidians—have a separate place of their own. Then ask us who Khrushchev is and what his present position is.

[A passage is missing between Images 3 and 4, where the speech moves to the proposed aluminium factory.]

It had earlier been repeatedly promised that the factory would be established by the Government. Later, for some reason, that position was forgotten and altered, and permission was reportedly given to a private concern called Seshasayee to start the aluminium factory. Our friends asked, “Rather than leaving that factory to private interests, could the Government not take it over and run it? It requires only an investment of five crore rupees.”

The Minister replied: “We waited and waited to see whether anyone would come forward with the money. Nobody did. That is why we handed it to the Seshasayee company.” There are no wealthy people in our country capable of investing in even a factory begun with capital of five crore rupees.

If we ask why the Government itself cannot invest five crore and start it, they ask how they can attend to other matters after spending five crore on this alone. This administration has no means even to spend five crore; we lack the bodily strength to lift and cast the pestle. We do not have the financial resources to start major industries.

But these financial resources exist in the North. They are abundant in the northern part of the Indian subcontinent. Banks with capital running into crores and profits running into lakhs are operated there by private capitalists. Whenever credit is sought to begin an industry, the banks there lend liberally. Numerous new industries are therefore established there. Since they are the masters of Delhi, they readily receive permission to buy machinery. American capitalists, German machinery and British experts are readily available to them. Consequently, their people's standard of living has risen.

What, however, is the condition of our country? Industry has not developed here, and therefore the people's standard of living has not risen. If industries are to grow in our country, our administration needs greater authority—Dravida Nadu must become a separate country.

Under the present Constitution, the State Government has no authority to carry out something even when it wants to do so. Its members may possess sympathy, but they lack the power to act. That is their condition. You prod them, so they become angry and come looking for a quarrel.

Mothers go to a temple-car festival. A child on its mother's hip sees the toys, pesters her, and asks for a rattle or a nodding doll. If she has money, the mother unties the knot in the end of the sari tucked at her waist and buys it. Even if the child breaks the doll the very next minute, she kisses the child and says, “Broken though it is, see how beautifully it has broken!”

But what does the mother do if she has no money? She turns the face of the child asking for the toy in another direction and says, “Look there, the temple car is going!” If the child is somewhat lively, it replies, “I can see the car later; first I want the rattle.” The mother again tries to divert the child's attention rather than admit that she has no money.

An elder standing nearby then enters the dispute: “The child is asking so sweetly; buy it for him.” The mother immediately gives the child two slaps on the back and scolds, “It was a mistake to bring you to the festival!” If her husband is nearby, he says, “Bringing him was not the mistake; your giving birth to him was!” She responds at once, “Giving birth to him was not my mistake. My mistake was choosing you of all people as my husband!”

The absence of money takes a matter that began with a toy and carries it as far as turmoil within the family. The State Government is in the same condition today.

We see a toy and stubbornly demand that it be bought for us. Kamaraj, in the mother's position, has no money in his hand. He therefore seizes us by the neck, turns our heads and says, “Look there at Bhakra-Nangal! Look at Sindri! Look at Hirakud!” We ask again and again. He becomes angry and scolds us: “Have you no other work?” It is because of the cruelty of deprivation and his inability to act that he scolds us.

Am I asking him to take the radiant moon from the sky and hang it as a lamp in my house? Am I asking him to remove the blemish on the moon and make it a looking glass for my home? I ask him to extract the iron sleeping beneath the soil of Salem. I ask only that we regain our own government. But unable to do what we ask, Kamaraj grows weary and laments.

That is why we say governance must be ours. Everyone must therefore work in the coming general election to increase the strength of our party.

3-7-1954-ந் தேதி வேலை மாநகர் ஆட்சிமன்றத்தில் காந்தி
சிலை திறப்பு விழாவில் அறிஞர் அண்ணா அவர்களின்
சொற்பொழிவு:-

“வேலை மாநகர் ஆட்சிமன்றத்தார், மணிமண்டபத்தில்
விளங்கும் உத்தமரின் சிலையைத் திறக்கும் பணியினை
எனக்களித்தார்கள். இதுபற்றிக் குறிப்பிட்ட மன்றத்தலைவர்
வேலூர் வரலாற்றிலேயே, இன்று ஒரு பொன்னாள் எனக்
குறிப்பிட்டார். என்னைப் பொறுத்தவரையிலேயே என்னுடைய
வாழ்க்கையில் - ஏன் - தமிழ் நாட்டின் அரசியலில் ஒரு முக்கிய
மான கட்டம் என்று கூறுவேன். காந்தியாரின் உருவச்சிலையை
நான் திறக்கிறேன் - இந்தச் செய்தியால், அரசியல் வட்டாரத்தில்
ஏற்பட்டுள்ள அதிர்ச்சியை நாடு அறியும்.

எனக்குக் கிடைத்துள்ள இந்த வாய்ப்பு - என்னைப்
பொறுத்தவரையில் - முதல் முறையல்ல; இரண்டாவது தடவை.
இதற்கு முன்னரே, சேலம் மாவட்டத்தைச் சேர்ந்த
இடைப்பாடியிலே, ராஜாஜி பூங்காவிலேயுள்ள காந்தியாரின்
சிலையை நான் திறந்து வைக்கும் வாய்ப்பைபப்
பெற்றிருக்கிறேன்; அனால் அங்குள்ள காங்கிரஸ் நண்பர்கள்,
இங்கு போல் கிலேசம் அடையவில்லை; பீதியடையவில்லை;
“இவனாவது திறப்பதாவது!” என்று கூறவில்லை.

காந்தியாரின் உருவச் சிலையை நான் திறக்கிறேன் - நான்
திறக்கவேண்டும் என்று நகராட்சிமன்ற நண்பர்கள் பெரிதும்
விரும்பியிருக்கிறார்கள். காரணம் என்ன? காந்தியாரின்
சிலையைத் திறக்க நான் மட்டுமே தகுதியுள்ளவன் என்பதாலா?

அதனாலேயே என்னைக் காந்தியாரின் முகாமிலே இல்லாதவனும்
அவரது இட்டங்களில் சிலவற்றை ஏற்றுச்சிலவற்றைக்
கண்டித்தவனும் ஆகிய என்னை, இந்தப் பணியை நடத்தச்
சொல்லியிருக்கிறார்கள், இதைக் கண்டு, எனது அருமைக்
காங்கிரஸ் நண்பர்கள் கலக்கமா அடைவது?

மணத்தை ரசிக்கிறானா அல்லது ரசிக்க மறுக்கிறானா என்று
பார்த்திருக்கலாம். என்னைப் பொறுத்தவரை மல்லிகை
மாற்றாரிடமிருப்பதால், அதற்கு மணமிருக்காது என்று
உரைப்பவனல்ல. அதனால்தான், நண்பார்கள் வந்து என்னை
அழைத்ததும் ஒப்புக் கொண்டேன்.

அவர்கள் அழைத்த நேரத்தில், நகராட்சி மன்றத்தினர்
மெஜாரிடி. முடிவோடு, என்னை அழைக்கத் தீர்மானித்திருப்ப
தாகவும் - ஒரு முறைக்கு இரண்டு முறை - தீர்மானம் நிறைவேற்றி
இருப்பதாகவும் குறிப்பிட்டனர். அகவே நான் இசைந்தேன்.

இந்த சிலைத் திறப்பு விழாவுக்கு, உள்ளூர் காங்கிரஸ்
கமிட்டி யினரும் கடைசி நேரத்தில் கூடி, இந்த விழாவில்
ஒத்துழைக்க வேண்டுமெனத் தீர்மானம் செய்தார்களாம். இந்தப்
பெருந்தன்மைக்கு என்னுடைய நன்றியைக் கூறிக்கொள்ளுகிறேன்.

ஏன், இவர்களுக்கெல்லாம் நன்றி கூறுகிறேன் என்றால். -

என்ற அசைகொண்ட வன் நான் அதற்கு உதாரணம் போல் நாம்
எல்லாம் நடந்து கொள்ள வேண்டும்.

## English Translation

Speech by Arignar Anna at the unveiling of Gandhi’s statue at the Vellore Municipal Council on 3 July 1954.

The Vellore Municipal Council has entrusted me with unveiling the statue of the noble man who shines within this memorial hall. Referring to this, the council chairman said that today is a golden day in Vellore’s history. As far as I am concerned, I would call it an important stage in my own life and, indeed, in the politics of Tamil Nadu. I am unveiling Gandhi’s statue, and the country knows the shock this news has created in political circles.

This is not my first such opportunity but my second. Earlier, at Edappadi in Salem district, I had the opportunity to unveil Gandhi’s statue in Rajaji Park. Congress friends there did not suffer the distress or fear seen here, nor did they say, “He, of all people, is going to unveil it!”

The municipal friends greatly desired that I should unveil Gandhi’s statue. Why? Is it because I alone am qualified? No. Many are more qualified than I. Perhaps they thought: others conduct countless unveilings, and this too would become merely one more among many; let us therefore grant this fellow fitness for such an act as well. I regard that as their generous intention.

The Chief Minister might have unveiled the statue instead of me. But then the event would not have acquired such importance; it would have become one among the many statues he frequently unveils. That is precisely why they asked me—one who does not belong to Gandhi’s camp, who accepts some of his programmes and criticises others—to perform this task. Why should my dear Congress friends be disturbed by it?

They could have watched to see whether I would appreciate or refuse the fragrance of jasmine merely because it grew in another person’s garden. I am not one who says that jasmine loses its fragrance because it belongs to an opponent. That is why I agreed when my friends invited me.

They told me the Municipal Council had decided by majority to invite me and had adopted the resolution not once but twice. I therefore consented. At the last moment, the local Congress Committee also reportedly met and resolved to cooperate with the ceremony. I offer my thanks for that generosity.

I thank them because I desire such generosity to prevail in political life. All of us should conduct ourselves as examples of it.

Some ask whether I respect the noble Gandhi. Respect may be shown in two ways. One may praise a person lavishly to his face and speak differently as soon as he disappears. Or, despite never having associated with him or belonged to his camp, one may reflect in private upon his service and rejoice. I belong to the second kind.

While this noble man of the world was alive, even when we criticised what we disliked and praised ideas we accepted, we never failed to recognise his distinction. Gandhi became a light of the world because his service caused even opponents to think about him. Congressmen alone do not praise him; everyone does, and the people of the world honour him. Such praise from others is the special respect a leader can receive.

Had Congress friends understood this, they should have welcomed me sincerely and taken pride that a member of an opposing party was unveiling the statue. They should have led me here saying: “Look at those eyes overflowing with grace! Look at that beautiful form and luminous face! Behold the noble man!” In doing so, they would have earned honour for themselves and for Gandhi. To say instead, “No one may even touch our Gandhi,” has neither fitness, meaning, honour, nor distinction.

I heard by radio that the noble Gandhi had been shot dead by a fanatic, and I was shaken. The radio station then invited me to speak. His killer, Godse, was a Maharashtrian Brahmin. Fearing that public rage might turn upon that entire community, they asked me to address the people.

Had I been base or consumed by party hatred, I could have exploited that moment and watched many disasters occur throughout the country. I am not so senseless as to applaud such calamity. I explained that one man’s fanaticism had killed the noble leader and that blame must not be placed upon the Brahmin people. I spoke of Gandhi’s services. Why should an action that shocked no Congressman at that time produce agitation now?

Some days later I happened to meet the Congress national poet Namakkal Ramalingam Pillai. He praised me, saying: “I have heard many speak about Gandhi’s greatness, but when I heard what you said on the radio, my heart was cooled. No one else spoke in that manner.”

It is not my habit to recount personal matters, but I mention this for the nationalist friends present. Who can say it is inappropriate for someone like me—one who rejoiced in Gandhi’s fundamental aspirations and the rare labour through which he sought to realise them—to unveil this statue?

Am I someone who abused Congress all day, entered it at election time, and hunted for advantage? Did I cover myself with a Congress cloak and hunt for office? Is this a hand that shook the white ruler’s hand, promoted communalism, or laboured to provoke fanatic violence? After leaving school, this hand did not seek a government job; it worked for the town and for public life. What hand other than this would be more fitting to unveil the statue?

Gandhi protected and developed Congress and obtained freedom for the country. Yet he was not even a four-anna member of Congress. Once the freedom he desired had arrived, he said Congress was no longer necessary and could be dissolved. He grieved that it had become a camp of people hunting profit.

I have not profited by invoking Gandhi’s name; indeed, his followers have kept me suppressed. What is the present condition of the Congress he nurtured? Gandhi said wicked and selfish people lived within Congress as bats live in an old fort and snakes in an anthill. Where is he, and where are those who remain today? Where is that light and where is this darkness?

I understand that Communist comrades here also desired and cooperated in having me perform this unveiling. I thank them as well. Such harmony with opposing parties is political civility. My leader Periyar and Chief Minister Achariyar met together at the celebration of the Prophet’s birthday. That was political civility.

Today I unveil Gandhi’s statue, and I call this a sacred day. Every political party should have cooperated without partisan distinction or hatred. That is political civility. If instead we create a political commotion, we shall not add honour to the father of this subcontinent.

Congress and I possess strong differences of opinion, but we also share agreements. The same is true between me and other parties. Careful thought reveals that our differences are few and our agreements many. To cultivate that common ground, every party must avoid political commotion, develop a spirit of cooperation, and advance political civility.

We see photographs of the American ambassador placing jasmine wreaths at Raj Ghat, the memorial built after Gandhi’s death; of Pakistan’s Prime Minister paying respect; and of many other foreigners offering affectionate homage. What does this mean? Why do foreigners come to honour him? We photograph them and proudly publish the images, saying, “Look at their tribute!” Who are they? Are they Congressmen? They have never belonged to Congress; some opposed it. They are not even of this country but foreigners.

Can anyone say that my affection for Gandhi’s memorial is less than theirs, or that my respect is inferior? When foreigners honour him, you rejoice inwardly. Should political fury and anger arise only when I honour him? A foreigner scatters flowers. In what way are the flowers I offer worse? Our people need this common sense and political understanding.

Yet I do not think it will arrive so easily. Once every year we still quarrel over whether Manmatha was burned or not, though no one alive has seen Manmatha. The dispute never ends. How long can we remain like this?

After independence, Gandhi himself said that we had crossed only one stage and that freedom must transform the lives of poor and ordinary people. I express that same view. He said he felt not joy but anxiety. Is that not still our condition?

If freedom is to become a fragrant flower rather than a paper flower, opposition parties must be given an opportunity to share and experience the freedom that has arrived.

Whoever we see and whatever we hear, they say, “The child of freedom! The child of freedom!” It is not enough merely to feed milk to that child. It needs lullabies, clothes, and play. A mother cannot provide everything herself. Uncles must bring toys, the elder sister sing lullabies, and relatives lift and fondle the child. That is how it must grow.

If the mother says, “I alone gave birth to it; no one else may approach,” nobody will cherish the child or understand its worth. In the same way, every party must have an opportunity to nurture the newborn child of freedom. A child in a wealthy family that is constantly overfed and kept from everyone else will finally become sickly and have to be taken to a doctor.

That is what I wish to point out. Give the DMK an opportunity to lift and cherish the child of freedom. Leave it with us and see; no harm will come.

If you nevertheless imagine that we will cause harm, let me invoke the Krishna story you favour. If we are like Putana, who went to nurse Krishna with poisoned milk, Krishna will take care of himself. Can you not possess at least that confidence? Why fear us?

When you see me unveil Gandhi’s statue, should your faces fall? You should beat your chests proudly and say, “Look! Even that opposition man unveils our Gandhi’s statue. Is this not the greatness of our Gandhiji?” Gandhi became a world light not merely because members of his own household praised him, but because outsiders saw him and offered homage.

You should rejoice at my unveiling it. It would have been an even greater honour had my friend and Congress MLA Comrade Masilamani been present on this platform.

Who am I and who are you? The relationship between us must not be one that breaks. I stress this to my dear Congress friends. We may differ on several matters. I remain in a camp that rejects some of Gandhi’s doctrines, but I have not denied the nation’s freedom. Only a fool would conceal the freedom that noble man obtained.

He desired not merely self-rule for this country, and the self-rule present today is not the self-rule he desired. He said there should be no division between poor and rich; people should not hate one another in religion’s name; conflict must not be created; and caste arrogance must not exist. He wished to see such a land of freedom.

When once asked who a Vaishnavite was, he explained that it was not a person with a sectarian mark upon the forehead and deceit in the heart, a tulsi garland upon the neck and scheming thoughts within. It was a person possessing a truthful heart.

When Gandhi visited the South on an earlier occasion, my movement and I were engaged in an agitation to burn the *Ramayana*. Referring to it, he said: “My Rama is different from the Rama described by Valmiki and Kamban. My Rama is not Sita’s husband, Dasaratha’s son, or Ravana’s killer. He is the embodiment of love and the form of truth.” I then wrote in *Dravida Nadu*: “He has set it afire—what have you done?”

By analysing such fundamental principles, we discovered agreements between Gandhi’s ideas and the basic aspirations of my movement. That is why, when Gandhi was shot dead, my movement’s leader Periyar issued a statement: “Call this country Gandhi Nadu instead of India, and change the name Hindu religion to Gandhi religion. If this is done, we are ready to accept it.” Who came forward? I ask the present Congress leaders: who accepted it?

Gandhi repeatedly said, “Truth is my God.” Who accepted that? You have been unable to digest many of the paths he left behind, while we in the opposition have accepted them and continue working upon them.

At this moment, when a memorial hall has been built and the statue unveiled in the name of that good work, I ask you in Gandhi’s name: strive to cultivate political civility. Fight to abolish caste distinctions. Cooperate in removing the cruelty of poverty. Come forward to eliminate the harms arising from religious belief.

## Translator's Notes

The OCR repeatedly reads “வேலை மாநகர்,” but the heading and historical context identify Vellore Municipality (வேலூர் மாநகர்/நகராட்சி). The source extraction is preserved verbatim.

“மாற்றான் தோட்டத்து மல்லிகை” is Anna’s well-known formulation that jasmine retains its fragrance even in an opponent’s garden; it frames the entire argument for honouring merit across party lines.

The source uses period communal and caste identifiers while recounting Gandhi’s assassination and Anna’s appeal against collective retaliation. These are translated as historical context, not endorsed labels.

“நாலணா மெம்பர்” refers to the ordinary Congress membership subscription category; Gandhi’s formal relationship to Congress is translated according to the source’s political claim without independent historical correction.

The source ends with three imperative appeals and does not include a separate ceremonial closing.

## English Translation

Speech 19, 10 September 1958

**C. N. Annadurai:** Mr Speaker, as many Honourable members have already shown, I cannot be greatly satisfied with the Minister's statement on how much progress in the food situation this year should be accepted.

We should certainly praise those who transformed a deficit State enough for statistics to show a modest surplus. Our condition has not deteriorated like West Bengal, Bihar or Uttar Pradesh, where people struggle for food and the consequences and disturbances echo inside and outside the legislatures. This State has improved enough to escape such severity.

The disagreement is over whom to praise. Government members think the Ministry or Government deserves it. I say the farming people who overcame every imposed hardship and still increased production deserve the true praise.

Farmers have not received their rights properly. Even ruling-party members acknowledge sorrowfully that adequate implements were not supplied. Members of several parties say responsibility and authority to protect what farmers produce were not entrusted to them. Despite all this, they increased output and saved us from misery. Everyone connected with this House owes them thanks.

When Government explains the food position, it should not merely list what it did. A welfare Government should state what could have been done and measure how much of it succeeded. Merely preventing starvation earns no special distinction.

Even during the world's terrible war, foreign rulers imported food and prevented widespread famine here, keeping people at least somewhat calm. If in ordinary times we had acted more vigorously than they did in wartime, we could have moved not merely from deficit to balance but to abundance: three meals for everyone, grain left over for generous export, and revenue for the State's development.

One member, while criticising parties that speak of separation, described Japan cultivating even palm-sized land and creating soil beds over bamboo structures on water. That made me consider how other countries cultivate.

Some countries are threatened by the sea, earthquakes or volcanoes; some see rain scarcely one month a year. Nature has not constrained us so severely. Our soil is not poorer than theirs. What is absent is not natural wealth but strength of mind, especially among the ruling party: the will to see backward conditions clearly and launch intensive plans.

With that determination we could raise production enough not only for all India but for export abroad. Food production is not an isolated issue; many others are bound to it.

Members from every party identified immediate failures, particularly Government's failure to develop and use irrigation adequately. With your permission, I return to my familiar refrain: the division between North and South. This is not language born of hatred.

If one person takes the first large handful of sandal paste from a bowl, everyone else can be served only from what remains. Likewise, after a large part of the subcontinent's revenue is spent on great northern projects, Tamil Nadu receives very little for small irrigation works.

Members have spoken of wasted rainwater, small reservoirs that should be built, neglected dams, fallow land that should enter cultivation, fertiliser not delivered in time, extreme land concentration, improper distribution and cultivators denied rights.

Tamil Nadu's rulers have a favourable political climate for acting: no serious confusion within their party, and even outsiders often help them. Yet despite such calm they have not properly developed agriculture, a fact that must trouble even them.

Food policy must be divided into three parts: production; distribution, meaning how much is sent to each area; and consumption, meaning how it is used. Because the three are closely connected, our speeches sometimes wander. Mr Speaker, you stop us to preserve order, and I thank you.

I regret having to say that work has not proceeded properly. The Minister's previous statistics and current statement even conflict. The English table heading in Image 6 is destroyed by Tamil-only OCR. One source appears to give 42 and 94 for 1956–57, while the present report gives 60 where another value may have been 89. I do not know whether this is a printing error or something else.

Rather than finding satisfaction in uncertain statistics, my party wants everyone to receive three meals a day; rice to sell at three measures per rupee; farmers to receive affordable implements; and fertiliser to go where land exists.

Today fertiliser goes to cool the hearts of profit hunters, not to nourish fields. Members say it is sold on the black market. Government may ask our alternative. Fertiliser such as ammonium sulphate could be distributed directly to the farmer who requests it through post offices, as postal stationery and quinine are sold. If this seems childish, use administrative experience to devise something better. But the present system does not supply timely help.

Members of several parties spoke about the tax on diesel used by agricultural machinery. Even Congress members, though bound to support the motion, argued strongly that the tax should be reduced or abolished. Their words entered one of the Minister's ears and left through the other, just as ours did.

Farmers feared that diesel tax would increase production costs, and that has occurred; consumers lament higher food prices. More serious is the absence of certainty over cultivators' rights.

The Ministry and Congress speak inconsistently: in one place, “land to the tiller”; elsewhere, “our programme is increased production”; elsewhere, “prices fall only when production rises”; elsewhere, land ceilings. Without a definite policy, landowners fear what will happen and leave land uncultivated.

In many places known to me, large owners fearing transfer of land plant casuarina instead of food crops. In Chengalpattu district, which both the Food Minister and I call ours, some reject proposed ratios such as sixty-forty and plant casuarina. Fearing even that may cause difficulty, some grow thorn scrub to cut as firewood.

Government seeks equal status between cultivators and owners. I welcome and praise that aim. But wealthy people skilled in finding legal loopholes work just as intensely to escape it. Painfully and shamefully, I must admit that Government repeatedly loses these contests while selfish interests win. Defeating them requires courage.

Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru has urged his party to create an all-party State-level executive committee on food, examine every related matter and follow its recommendations. At present Government makes a proposal, opposition rejects it, Government attacks our character, and we reply. The country remains trapped in this game.

I welcome members' call to forget party divisions over a life-and-death problem beyond parties. When we demand action, some complain: “You speak of North and South. The North has great rivers and can build dams. What great river does the South have? Perhaps only Annadurai's mouth can be dammed!”

Those responsible for the subcontinent should first have concentrated on water-scarce regions. Areas watered by the Ganges, Indus and Yamuna could manage for five years without immediate giant dams. Had crores instead been allotted to store the small rivers, streams, forest torrents and canals of the dry South through many modest schemes, our region would have developed far better.

Consider the dry areas of Chengalpattu, South Arcot and North Arcot. The Palar project could have prevented drought in Chengalpattu and surrounding districts. Instead, it decays; the Vegavathi project lies unattended; no one thinks of the Cheyyar project.

The Minister and I live in Madras and can rely on Corporation tap water. Farmers along the Palar must wait for river water to irrigate their fields. Government must care about their irrigation and the necessary schemes.

Where rivers lack water, Irrigation Department experts propose scientifically lifting groundwater. Lift irrigation may be the only practical route for parts of Chengalpattu and North Arcot. The State should spend seriously on it. Whether authority for such work belongs to the State or Centre was debated vigorously in Parliament only last week.

Some think only we speak of North and South, but even the Home Minister's report cannot escape the distinction. The Food Minister himself says that although we produced more grain, prices here rose because prices rose in the northern States.

By saying this, he demonstrates that no part of one body escapes the effects of disease, dirt, heat rash or scabies elsewhere. He did not say “neighbouring countries” or “other parts of India”; he said “northern States.” He may carefully avoid the phrase next time! (Laughter.) He clearly establishes that northern conditions affect the South, though this may not mean he has attained political enlightenment.

Food is now the subcontinent's greatest problem. The Union says responsibility belongs to States, while States such as Uttar Pradesh say the Centre cannot escape it. Nehru's English words in Image 12 are mangled by Tamil-only OCR, but the surrounding Tamil quotes him as saying the Centre has no legal responsibility yet has a moral responsibility.

We are distressed that whenever opportunities arose to fulfil that moral responsibility, the Centre deceived the States. Government members may find this difficult to accept.

The Minister's own report says Madras State repeatedly asked to become a separate food zone and the Centre refused. The State should now speak plainly: if the Centre claims no food responsibility, let us secure our own grain; do not interfere or obstruct us. The Ministry must acquire the courage to say this.

Instead of directing anger at those of us who repeatedly speak of North and South, they should display that anger in Delhi, in the proper place and manner, and obtain continuing rights for this State.

Why was scientific artificial rain not attempted in drought areas? Why not use groundwater beneath dry riverbeds? Uttar Pradesh spent lakhs and crores drilling tube wells without result and still asks the Centre for more. Tamil Nadu has sound plans, capable execution and supporting statistics. Why be shy, hesitant or ashamed to demand the necessary funds?

I respectfully ask the Ministry to increase food production, distribute it properly, and, as Kalyanasundaram clearly said, prevent procurement from becoming a hunting ground for hoarders' profit so food reaches buyers fairly.

The Minister's report claims a modest improvement from deficit because he himself is distressed by the present food position. I share his distress. He is ashamed that it has not been corrected; I share that shame. He is pained that conditions have not improved further; I share that pain. With that, I conclude.

## Translator's Notes

The first-page scan confirms the title “உணவு நிலைமை பற்றிய விவாதம்,” Speech 19, dated 10 September 1958.

The Tamil OCR source is preserved verbatim. The statistical table heading and several values in Image 6, and Prime Minister Nehru's English phrase in Image 12, are damaged by Tamil-only OCR. Context is translated but exact English is not reconstructed.

The source OCR says “ப்ரொடெக்ஷன்” where the policy triad and context clearly require production. The Tamil is not silently altered; English translates the intended concept and the concern is separately logged.

Food output, prices, land ratios, irrigation costs and inter-State comparisons are historical source claims requiring verification against the ministerial statement and Assembly record.

“Three measures of rice for one rupee” is a period policy demand. “படி” is a traditional volume measure and is translated as measure rather than assigned a modern metric equivalent.

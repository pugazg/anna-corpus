திராவிடர் கழகத்

23, 24-10-1948 தேதிகளில் ஈரோடில் நடைபெற்ற திராவிடர்
கழகத் தனி மாகாண மாநாட்டுக்குத் தலைமை வகித்த

தோழர் அண்ணாதுரை ஆற்றிய சொற்பொழிவு:-

தோழர்களே!

பாட்டாளி மக்களின் விடுதலைப் போருக்கு முரசு
கொட்டிய நாளான, மே தின விழா சமயத்திலே, தூத்துக்குடியில்,
நமது மாநாடு நடைபெற்றது - ஈரோடில் மீண்டும்
கூடியிருக்கிறோம், அக்டோபர் மாதத்தில், அவனி கண்டு
அஞ்சும்படியான பாட்டாளிகளின் விடுதலைக்கான மாபெரும்
புரட்சி நடைபெற்ற திங்களில் கூடி. இருக்கிறோம்.

அந்த அற்புதமான அக்டோபர் மாதம், ஜாரின்
கொடுமையைத் தகர்த்தெறிந்து, ரஸ்புடீனின் மாய வலையைப்
பிய்த்தெறிந்து, ராணுவத்தின் ஈட்டி. முனைகளை ஓடித்தெறிந்து
இரத்தம் சொட்டச் சொட்ட, ஆனால் இலட்சியம் ஒளிவிட,
சோவியத் நாட்டுச் சோர்விலா மக்கள், தமது தலைவர் முன்
நின்றனர் - நாழும் இன்று, நமது தலைவர் முன்பு அணிவகுத்து
நிற்கிறோம்; அறப்போரில் ஈடுபட்.டவர்கள், அடக்குமுறைக்கு
ஆளானவர்கள், சிறை சென்று திரும்பியவர்கள், தடியடியைத்
தாங்கியவர்கள், கல்லடி. கண்டவர்கள், சொல்லடியைப்
பெற்றவர்கள், சோர்வைப் பெறாதவர்கள், மீண்டும் போரில்
ஈடுபடத் துடிப்பவர்கள், சிறையை நோக்கி நடப்பவர்கள், சீறும்
சர்க்காரைக் கண்டு சிரிப்பவர்கள், என்ற முறையிலே
கூடியிருக்கிறோம். அக்டோபர், அவனியிலே அற்புதமான
புரட்சியைக் குறிக்கும் சொல்லாகி விட்டது - அங்கு,

சோவியத்தில். திராவிடத்திலும், இந்த அக்டோபரில் நாம்
செய்யும் தீர்மானம், துவக்கும் போர், வரலாற்றிலே இடம்பெறப்
போகும் நாளாக வேண்டும் - நாளாகித் தீரும் - ஏன் ஆகாது -
இப்படை தோற்கின் எப்படை ஜெயிக்கும்?

அறப்போரை நாம் அன்று துவக்கினோம் -
ஆளவந்தார்களின் ஏளனம் பிறந்தது - அறப்போரை நாம்
நடாத்தினோம், ஆளவந்தார்கள் எண்ணத் தொடங்கினர்,
அறப்போரைத் தொடர்ந்து நடத்தி வரலானோரம்,
அளவந்தார்கள், சட்டப் புத்தகங்களைத் துருவிப் பார்க்கலாயினர்
- திறித்தனர். முதலில் - சிந்தித்தனர். பிறகு - சீறினர். அதற்கும்
பிறகு - சிறை என்றனர், அறிவோம் என்றோம், தள்ளு உள்ளே,
என்று உத்தரவிட்டனர் தமது அதிகாரிகளுக்கு, எண்ணிக்கொள்,
என்று கூறினார் நமது தலைவர் - சிறைக்கூடங்களில் நமது
தோழர்கள் புகுந்தனர் - சென்னையில் திருச்சியில்,
தூத்துக்குடியில், வேலூரில், கடலூரில் பலப்பல இடங்களில்.
அறப்போரை இடையே, ஐதராபாத் சம்பவத்துக்காக, நிறுத்தி
வைத்தோம். நமது அறப்போர் உச்ச நிலையில், மக மிக
உற்சாகத்துடன் நடைபெற்றுக் கொண்டிருந்த நேரத்தில், பத்துத்
தொண்டர்களை மட்டும் மறியல் செய்யச் சொன்னால் போதாது,
ஏன் இன்னும் பலரை அனுப்பி வைக்கக் கூடாது, தொண்டர்கள்
தான் கூட்டம் கூட்டமாக முன் வருகிறார்களே என்று ஆர்வ
மிகுதியுடன் சில நண்பர்கள் நம்மைக் குறை கூறிக் கொண்டிருந்த
நேரத்தில், தொண்டை மண்டலம் பள்ளியிலே மட்டுந்தானா, ஏன்
எங்கள் பள்ளியில் மறியல் நடத்தக் கூடாது, வாருங்கள்,
வாருங்கள் என்று பள்ளி மாணவர்கள் தத்தமது
பள்ளிக்கூடங்களுக்கு வருமாறு அழைப்பு அனுப்பிய வண்ணம்
இருந்த நேரத்தில், தாய்மார்களின் உற்சாகம், ஆடவரை அஞ்சச்
செய்த நேரத்தில், காட்டிலே விட்டால் என்ன, பட்டினி
போட்டால் என்ன, பதட்டமாக அடித்தால்தான் என்ன, தக்க
விலை கொடுக்காமலா, உரிமை கிடைக்கும், என்று கூறி நமது
தோழர்கள் மார்தட்டி நின்ற நேரத்தில், ஹைதராபாத் தொல்லை
குறுக்கிட்டு, நாம் அறப்போரை நிறுத்தி வைக்கும் நிலையை
ஏற்படுத்தி விட்டது.

என்ன இருந்தாலும் இதற்காக இந்தி எதிர்ப்பை இப்படி
ஒரே அடியாக நிறுத்தி விடுவதா என்று குறை சொல்லும்
நண்பர்கள் இருக்கிறார்கள். அறப்போர் நடைபெற வேண்டும்,
நாம் அதிலே கலந்து பணியாற்ற வேண்டும் என்ற ஆர்வத்தால்,

இதுபோலக் கூறுபவர்களுக்கு என் பாராட்டுதல். ஆனால், அந்த
நேரத்தில் நாம் அறப்போரை நிறுத்தி வைத்தது, நமது
மாண்பையும் மதிப்பையும் நிச்சயமாக உயர்த்தியிருக்கிறது
என்பதை மட்டும் நண்பர்கள் உணர வேண்டும். அளவந்தார்களே
கூட நாம், அடியோடு அறப்போரைக் கைவிட்டு விட்.டவர்களல்ல
என்பதை அறிவார்கள் - மீண்டும் அறப்போர் தொடுத்தே
தீருவோம் - தொடுக்கப் போகிறோம் - அதற்கே கூடி
இருக்கிறோம் - நாள் குறித்துத் தருவார் நம் தலைவர் - விட்ட
இடத்திலிருந்து தொடங்குவோம். அதிகரித்த ஆர்வத்துடன் -
விறுவிறுப்புடன், அதற்கான முன்னேற்பாடே, இம்மாநாடு.

அறப்போர் நிறுத்தப்பட்டி ருந்த இடைக்காலத்தை
மந்திரிமார்கள் தந்திரமாகத் தமது பிரசாரத்துக்குப் பயன்படுத்திக்
கொண்டனர். ஒவ்வோர் நாளும் இந்தியை ஆதரித்துப் பேசுவதும்,
எதிர்ப்பவர்களை ஏளனம் செய்வதுமாக இருந்தனர். கல்வி
அமைச்சர் குறள் கற்றவர் என்கிறார்கள் - வாய்மை தெரிந்திருக்க
வேண்டும் - ஆனால் அவரோ, போகுமிடமெங்கும், இந்தி
கட்டாயமில்லை என்று பேசியே வருகிறார். அறப்போர்
துவங்குமுன்பே ஆயிரம் தடவை, கூறினோம், அமைச்சரின்
திட்டத்தால், இந்தி கட்டாயமாக்கப்படும் விதத்தை, அவருடைய
துணிவும், பொதுமக்களின் மறதியிலே அவருக்குள்ள
நம்பிக்கையும், அவ்வளவு தூரம் அவருக்குப் பொய் பேசும்
சவுகரியத்தை அளித்திருக்கிறது. அனால், கெட்டிக்காரன்
புளுகுக்கும் எட்டே. நாளல்லவா தவணை வைத்திருக்கிறார்கள் -
அமைச்சரின் சொல்லின் போக்கும் வெளுத்துவிட்டது,
சின்னாட்களுக்கு முன்பு, அவர் பேச்சின் மூலமே.

கட்டாயப்படுத்தவில்லை இந்தியை - என்று அவரே
கூறுகிறார் - திருநெல்வேலியில் ஒரு ஆசிரியர், இந்தி இஷ்ட.
பாடமானாலும் எனக்கு வேண்டாம், நான் படிக்க மாட்டேன்,
என்று ஒரு மாணவன் சொன்னால் என்ன செய்வது என்று
கேட்டாராம். ஆசிரியர் அவர்! அவர் கேட்கிறார் இந்த
அர்த்தமற்ற கேள்வியை! நாலும் நாலும் எட்டு என்று
சொன்னால், நான் அவன் தலையில் குட்டவா என்று கேட்கிறார்,
அறிவு பரப்ப வந்தவர் - அசிரியத் தொழிலை அவர் அறிவு
பரப்பும் தொழிலாகக் கருதவில்லை போலும் - அங்கத்தை
வளர்க்கும் தொழிலாகக் கருதுகிறார் போலும்! மாணவன்
சொல்கிறான், எனக்கு இந்தி இஷ்டமில்லை, படிக்கமாட்டேன்
என்று. ஆசிரியரே கூறுகிறார், இந்தி இஷ்டபாடமென்று -

மாணவனுக்கும் தெரிவிக்கிறார். இஷ்ட பாடம் என்பது
உண்மையானால், மாணவன், தனக்கு இந்தி படிக்க
விருப்பமில்லை என்ற உடனே, ஆசிரியர் செய்ய வேண்டிய
தென்ன - சரி, நீ படிக்கத் தேவையில்லை - விருப்பமில்லாத உன்
மீது இந்தியைத் திணிக்கமாட்டேன். என்றல்லவா கூற வேண்டும்.
அனால் ஆசிரியர் என்ன செய்கிறார்? அமைச்சரைக் கேட்கிறார்,
அவருக்குத்தான் இப்படிப் பாதி மதி! அமைச்சருக்கு இருக்கக்
கூடாதோ முழுமதி - அவர் கூறுகிறார், அப்படிச் சொல்லும்
மாணவனை வெளியே துரத்து - என்று. எவ்வளவு தெளிவு -

### Images 1-3

Speech delivered by Comrade Annadurai while presiding over the Dravidar Kazhagam Special Provincial Conference held at Erode on 23 and 24 October 1948.

Comrades!

Our conference was held at Tuticorin during the May Day celebrations, on the day when the drum sounded for the workers' war of liberation. We have assembled again at Erode in October, the month in which the workers carried out a mighty revolution for freedom that made the world tremble.

In that wondrous October, the tireless people of the Soviet land stood before their leader, blood dripping from them but their ideal shining, after smashing the Tsar's cruelty, tearing apart Rasputin's web of illusion, and driving away the army's spearheads. Today we too stand arrayed before our leader: those who joined the moral struggle, suffered repression, returned from prison, endured police batons and stones, received verbal abuse without losing heart, long to re-enter the struggle, march toward prison, and laugh at a raging Government. October has become a word signifying the marvellous revolution in that country. The resolution we adopt and the struggle we begin in this Dravidian October must likewise become a day recorded in history. It certainly will. Why should it not? If this army is defeated, what army can win?

We began the moral struggle, and the rulers mocked us. We conducted it, and they began to think. As we continued it, they started searching their law books. First they laughed, then reflected, and finally raged. After that they said, "Prison." "We know," we replied. They ordered their officers, "Push them inside," while our leader said, "Count them." Our comrades entered prisons in Madras, Trichy, Tuticorin, Vellore, Cuddalore, and many other places.

We suspended the struggle midway because of the Hyderabad affair. It was then at its height and proceeding with immense enthusiasm. Friends criticised us out of eagerness: "Why send only ten volunteers to picket when volunteers are coming forward in crowds?" School pupils wrote, "Why conduct picketing only at Thondaimandalam School? Come to ours too!" Mothers displayed an enthusiasm that put men to shame. Our comrades stood proudly saying, "What if they abandon us in the forest, starve us, or beat us savagely? Can rights be won without paying the proper price?" At precisely that moment the Hyderabad trouble intervened and forced us to suspend the struggle.

Some friends ask why we should have stopped the anti-Hindi struggle so completely. I commend those who say this from a desire to participate. But they must understand that suspending it then certainly raised our honour and standing. Even the rulers know that we have not abandoned the struggle forever. We shall certainly renew it; that is why we are assembled. Our leader will name the day. We shall resume where we stopped, with increased enthusiasm and vigour. This conference is the preparation.

During the interval, ministers cleverly used the suspension for their propaganda. Every day they supported Hindi and mocked its opponents. The Education Minister is said to have studied the *Kural* and ought therefore to know truthfulness, yet wherever he goes he declares that Hindi is not compulsory. Before the struggle began, we explained a thousand times how his plan would make it compulsory. His audacity, and his faith in public forgetfulness, gave him such freedom to lie. But even the cleverest lie is said to have only eight days' life, and the course of the Minister's argument was recently exposed by his own words.

### Images 4-6

The Minister himself says Hindi has not been made compulsory. At Tirunelveli, a teacher reportedly asked what should be done if a student said, "Even if Hindi is optional, I do not want it and shall not study it." A teacher asks this meaningless question! A man sent to spread knowledge asks whether he should strike a pupil on the head for saying four and four make eight. He evidently regards teaching not as developing intelligence but as developing limbs.

The pupil says he does not like Hindi and will not study it. The teacher himself says it is optional. If that is true, the teacher should answer, "Very well, you need not study it; I shall not impose Hindi upon you against your will." Instead he asks the Minister, a man of only half a mind. Should not the Minister possess a full one? His answer is, "Drive such a student out." What clarity! Such is the wisdom of the minister who came to govern. Having ordered the expulsion of a pupil who refuses supposedly optional Hindi, he simultaneously insists that Hindi is not compulsory.

Why did the Minister alter his first order concerning Tamil Nadu? He first said Hindi would not be compulsory there, but within a month changed the order and declared it compulsory. He has offered no intelligent explanation. Power has filled him with arrogance. He does not know what became of the authority and status of men still more arrogant; Acharya knows it from personal experience.

We offered the rulers explanations, but they refused to listen. We appealed, and they rejected us. We warned against needless hostility, and they ignored us. We collected the statements of language experts, and they dismissed them as worthless. Only then did we begin the struggle.

At this conference we shall no longer keep presenting reasons. Sound advice is useless to one who has lost judgment, a sight to one who has lost vision, and a message to one whose hearing has dulled. To this Ministry, arrogant in the belief that no one can question it, wisdom tastes bitter. The intoxication of power has made it incapable of distinguishing friend from foe. We shall say only: let them do whatever they can.

Rulers and ministers! You wield repression without shame. Continue freely; consult Dyer's diary as you do so. Order your men to beat until the blood of warriors who dedicate themselves to a righteous struggle spills upon the ground. You possess the authority and have acquired the habit. You practised by beating workers. You used batons and tear gas. They asked for life; you gave them death. You beat cultivators, students, officials, and strikers until their ribs broke, and called it victory.

You can forget compassion, crush justice, and batter the warriors of moral struggle. But know this while time remains: just as sandalwood releases fragrance the more it is ground, the strength of the Dravidian movement will grow as your repression increases. We have not forgotten that this struggle begun against Hindi is the first stage of our country's war of liberation. With every baton blow you will hear the cry, "Thalamuthu! Natarajan!" Even if one falls dead, the Tamil country will say, "Here is another Velayutham."

Repression is a strange force. After its first blow, those against whom it is wielded grow three times stronger. Dravidam is ready to show that our language struggle contains Bhagat Singhs and Kumarans.

The present rulers once suffered the repression introduced by the British. I believed that memory would teach them the noble power of the sense of rights. Instead they have developed a desire to delight in seeing others undergo what they endured. Let that desire too be satisfied. Warriors of the moral struggle, let these ministers earn a name: even if they cannot govern the Tamils, at least they can kill them. What a noble, nationally celebrated name to engrave in history: the rulers who killed the people!

### Images 6-8

Other parties invite people with tempting promises: "Our influence has reached its height; we shall win the next election and gain seats in the Assembly." We invite people to our movement without such inducements. We call warriors, not wastrels; resolute people able to withstand repression, not political gamblers; people who see no difference between home and prison, not dolls to decorate the Assembly. To protect our country's cherished language, we need one child from every household. We need noble people with the strength to withstand, for language and country, the furious assault of frenzied tyranny.

No lofty principle in the world was established by long speeches alone or by delicate political manoeuvring. It was established by the blood of noble people prepared to give their lives for it. That is the source of our certainty. Our policy is just and honourable; it is not, as they allege, the product of party-political jealousy.

Language is the path of life, and a people's character is formed through that life. We possess a language whose wealth remains undiminished, which crowned kings nurtured, and in which imperishable works of literature exist. Why then should we accept Hindi, an alien language lacking civilisation, grammar, and literature, once introduced and driven out, and which drank the precious lives of two Tamil youths? We ask why we need it. Have we no right even to ask? In what way does agitation on this issue damage their government?

Even ten days ago Acharya said in North India, "Do not compel anyone in anything." He knows the suffering compulsion caused him. The man who worshipped his chosen deity, boasted that he would strike with whatever came to hand, imprisoned more than a thousand people, and failed; the man who, as Governor-General, saw black flags raised against him, now speaks from experience. Why do these ministers fail to understand the subtlety of his words? They trust only repression. They have batons; we have bodies to endure them. They have prisons; we have the courage, and many of us the experience, to go there and carry prison plates. They have guns; we have chests to receive their bullets.

This special Dravidar Kazhagam conference wishes to tell the Government precisely this: we shall not remain mute while our rights are destroyed. Your conduct has removed our concern for life. Come again to the field; the Hyderabad quarrel that intervened is over. We shall give our lives for our own language. You may live with the noble reputation of killing your own brethren for a stranger's language. Conveying this to the rulers is the conference's chief purpose.

To ministers who think they can suppress or destroy the movement by obstructing our propaganda, stopping newspapers, confiscating books, banning plays, and arresting leaders, I say: you cannot destroy this movement filled with vital force. These methods did not arise only in your extraordinary minds. They are arrows despots have long fired at every liberation movement. Such arrows cannot pierce and destroy the sense of rights; their points become blunt and they fall. That is history, and that is what you will witness here.

If you possess even a little capacity for calm reflection, consider the nature of this great movement. It has no wealth and consists of ordinary working people. It seeks no Assembly work or office and has dismissed elections as a nuisance. Its leader is seventy years old and has gone to prison eight times. It has been conducted against the country's many powers and their cunning leaders. Repeatedly attacked, it never lost strength. It came forward with principles whose justice ought to have been accepted at once. Yet our principles have triumphed one by one, and not one has been abandoned. Consider how this great victory was won.

The movement possesses the firmness Socrates retained even in death. It has made its principles victorious by rejecting immediate rewards, driving away pleasure-seekers, retaining people pure of heart, and possessing clarity of thought and practical ability.

### Images 8-10

Before unleashing the police to suppress us, or at least after the news of our beating has delighted your hearts if you lack that much patience, pause and think. Has the country ever finally rejected and hated anything we advocated? Or has it ultimately desired and fully supported each one? Pay some attention to the answer.

When we asked why a Brahmin priest was needed at a wedding, people were startled, reviled us, cursed us, and attacked us with stones and words. "Who are these people to break an ancient arrangement?" they demanded. What happened in the end? Are not thousands of weddings now conducted without Brahmin priests?

If such was the fate of the hereditary authority of the men with sacred grass, what importance have ministers whose status lasts only five years, won by pleading and dancing before voters with "Come, little brother; come, elder brother; come, my dear; come, my jewel"?

Had the leaders of Brahmin society possessed shame and self-respect, would they not have understood the course of time when their Vedas, Puranas, epics, and shastras fled and hid, unable to withstand the propaganda of ordinary people like us? How wisely the British understood that their imperial merchandise would no longer sell, and how intelligently they closed shop. Why do these people lack that wisdom? The courage supplied by our ministers is the reason. The ministers themselves are emboldened by the belief that, however much the Dravidar Kazhagam opposes them, they can still speak sweetly to the people: "Did we not liberate the country? Can you forget us?" They expect electoral tactics to win and allow them to hold court.

The grip of British imperialism has ended not only in India but in neighbouring Burma, Ceylon to the south, and many other countries. British troops have returned home from Egypt, and Britain has relinquished the administration of Palestine.

In all these countries it is natural for parties that fought imperialism to receive an opportunity to govern and the people's affection. Our ministers imagine that the influence Congress gained in this way will endure as long as the earth and the five elements, and that they may therefore do anything. General knowledge, and the democratic spirit that can arise only from it, have scarcely budded in the country. The rulers can therefore behave as though every blow they strike is profit.

They believe they can flourish to the end by saying: "Congress won freedom for the country; the Dravidar Kazhagam opposes Congress and its ministers; moreover, these people hate Brahmins and insult religion."

Congress was formed to secure national independence. That independence has now been obtained. Why, the public is beginning to ask quietly, since the time for a loud voice has not yet come, should Congress continue to exist? Those words sting the rulers' ears like red scorpions.

People are also beginning to ask ministers who boast, "Only we can provide good government": "That may be true in speeches, but why do we not see it in action?"

The country, observing Congress's fascist course, democratically desires and searches for another party. Many thoughtful people insist upon this. Congress members fear, friends hope, and office-seekers pray that the Dravidar Kazhagam will become that opposition party.

If the Dravidar Kazhagam became a party whose sole political object was to win office, it could certainly gather the oppositional forces now seething throughout the country because of the Congress Ministry's conduct, become their refuge, defeat Congress at the next election, and ascend the seat of power.

Congress friends hold court after wrongly calculating that the Dravidar Kazhagam can only shout in the wilderness and has not grown enough to govern. The country's actual condition is different. The consequences of Congress rule, and the suffering those consequences have caused, are considerable.

### Images 11-12

If the Dravidar Kazhagam believed its sole task was replacing Congress rule with another government, it could certainly use the many forces opposed to Congress and win. It does not desire such a victory; its object is not so ordinary.

Its object is not a political contest for the profit of capturing and enjoying power. It possesses a great ideal that will create a new condition for the country. That is why it refuses to spend thought on trifles.

Congress members ask why people who say, "We want neither elections nor power," continually condemn Congress ministers and policy and speak against Brahminism. Friends eager to gain office by any means ask the same. Both speak without understanding the Dravidar Kazhagam's programme.

They call us enemies of the Congress Ministry, Congress, and Brahmins. We certainly condemn the ministers' conduct, but are not their personal enemies. We condemn Congress policy and programme, but are not enemies of Congress members. We strongly condemn Brahminism, but are not enemies of Brahmins. Because we make these three criticisms, Congress ministers, Congress strategists, and Brahmins oppose us and work skilfully to ruin our lives. We do not conceal our condemnation of the conduct of those three groups, but reject the charge that we are their enemies. We oppose not those persons as such, but anyone who exercises domination, whatever their form or name.

If we were merely political enemies of the Congress Ministry, would a conference like this be necessary? Even if one met, would not Madurai Vaidyanatha Iyer, Madisanji Varadachariar, Kasa Subba Rao, and Andhra Prakasam occupy the platform? We would conduct secret consultations and plot to topple the Ministry, not convene a public conference. If we merely hated Congress ministers, Erode would not be our camp; Mylapore and Triplicane would. If our aim were to overthrow them, we would join the ranks organised by those preparing to succeed them. Have we said or thought, "We do not want these ministers; it would be better if these other people became ministers"? Those disappointed after expecting profit from the present ministers may think so. We do not.

We condemn the ministers' conduct not from personal hostility or any concealed motive, but because they are being used to damage the principles we deeply believe and to ruin our ideal. We do not seek to expel them so that we can take their seats, or install someone else and grin at them with a servile mind.

If a green creeper wraps around a traveller's foot, is he its enemy when he tears it away and continues? His work is not to cut down green creepers. He removes it because it lies across the path of his greater undertaking and catches his foot. Does that mean he hates one vine and loves the bottle-gourd vine? In the same way, we must condemn and oppose this Ministry only because it crosses and blocks the path of the liberation struggle toward the ideal we sincerely believe. It is not hatred, deceit, or lust for office. People possessing these and still worse qualities are already there, singing *Vande Mataram*, wearing khadi, and belonging to the ministers' own party.

## Part 2

The stored Part 2 scans are not in reading order. The translation below follows the continuous printed argument: Images 11, 2, 3, 5, 9, 10, 6, 4, 1, 7, and 8.

### Images 11, 2 And 3

One gentleman says, "What is the Dravidar Kazhagam's programme? Do you not know? It is to abuse Brahmins."

A khadi-clad man says, "What work does the Dravidar Kazhagam have except finding fault with Congress?"

A reader of the Vedas says, "The Dravidar Kazhagam? Is that not the band that blasphemes God?"

There is a story of several blind men examining an elephant by touch. To the man who felt its leg, the elephant was a mortar. To the one who touched its ear, it was a winnowing fan. To the man who felt its tail, it resembled a broom. Likewise, people who do not know the complete form and meaning of our programme treat whatever portion falls within their view as the whole, then speak and revile us accordingly.

The complete programme, as I have said, is the noble ideal of creating a new condition for the country. Explain it fully and no listener can continue to object; they must support the Dravidar Kazhagam. The rulers, emboldened by authority, have made it their method of war to prevent the country hearing that explanation. Section 144 everywhere! A ban upon everything! A ban on *Ravana Kaviyam*! Police troops at the Kamban festival! A ban on *Porvaal*! Matters proceed with martial flavour dripping from them.

Last week a writer in *The Hindu* demanded an immediate ban on my *Kambarasam*. Seeing the book, he said, would destroy the pride and delight created by celebrating Kamban; it was corrupting students, who were reading it eagerly in colleges.

What were all the great scholars, connoisseurs, painters of sensuous scenes, and poets about those scenes doing during these three years after I published *Kambarasam*? Were they submerged in devotion to Rama? Why did they not write a rebuttal? Because they could not. Even now, let that writer and my other friends rebut the book if they possess any self-respect. Can they say that the verses I printed in *Kambarasam* are absent from the *Kamba Ramayanam*, or argue that the meanings I gave them do not fit?

They may grieve and cry shame, asking, "Alas, why did you extract and publish all these?" They may incite a clamour to ban the book. But no devotee of Kamban can say that its verses are false or inadmissible material. Nor did we print all such verses; many more remain, and they too will appear. Ban it, ban it! How many works, and for how long? Has any banned work in any age failed to circulate? What madness to imagine that prohibition can prevent new knowledge from spreading!

The Congress ministers' effort to prevent our programme taking root in the people's minds cannot succeed forever. They banned *Iraniyan*. What did they find in Vellore prison? Iraniyan, Prahaladan, everyone, including Narasimha himself! Did others stay quiet, saying, "Those actors have been caught, why invite needless trouble?" No. *Iraniyan* appeared in Cuddalore. Then came the order, "Push them into prison." Officials agonised over which legal section to apply to the comrades, found no answer, and finally told them to go home. Who knows in how many more towns *Iraniyan* will be performed?

Congress appears to fear that the Dravidar Kazhagam will endanger it at the next election and therefore plans to use its present authority to attack and destroy us. It certainly will not succeed. The Dravidar Kazhagam plans to conduct a struggle for rights on every front to create a new condition for the country. These activities are not undertaken with elections alone in mind. Congress performs that kind of work; our purpose and programme are different.

Our ministers endlessly say we are enemies of the Ministry and seek to overthrow it. The Ministry will certainly fall, but not because of us. The warrior of Rome was stabbed to death not by someone in the enemy camp. Unshaken by anything else, his heart broke when Brutus, who had moved beside him, stabbed him: "You too, Brutus?" This Ministry too has its Brutuses, dressed not in black but in khadi.

They similarly call us enemies of Congress without reason or meaningful explanation, as though we merely happen to dislike Congress the way some people dislike a particular food. If opposing Congress alone were our work, would our camp look like this, a gathering of people carrying pots of millet gruel? Would it not unite dwellers in golden cities, lords of usury, zamindars, mittadars and mirasidars, fashionable dandies, upper-class people, orthodox Brahmins, heads of monasteries, Jeeyars, and everyone hostile to Congress?

Who is present here now? Lords who live without labour, or rich men fattened by hereditary privilege? No: workers who know starvation not from songs or books but from experience; people whose lives are charred within houses with crumbling walls; people broken by life's storm and tortured by the cruelty of varnashrama. Are they mill kings, plantation owners, merchant princes, or moneylenders? They are strong people with bodies and determination fit for labour, noble people exhausted by work, not cheats, conjurers, or people rolling in wealth and comfort.

If hostility to Congress alone were our ideal, could we not seat rows of zamindars, mittadars, mirasidars, and other plunderers here? Why do we not let them near us?

### Images 5 And 9

Why, if opposing Congress alone were our work, did people of that kind form a single rank against us at the Salem conference as though fire had touched a wound? We would discover everyone hostile to Congress, gather them together, and create one camp. Have we done so? Are we doing so? Will we ever? Never. We condemn Congress with reason. We have not acquired the contemptible idea of combining with everyone who opposes it for other reasons.

Some zamindars may privately believe Congress threatens their way of life and must therefore be destroyed; people without understanding may think so. If our only work were opposing Congress, we would seek out zamindars and sing their praises. Do we? Let anyone point out even one zamindar among us, except an actor dressed as one in a play. Why have we admitted none? Because our purpose is not merely to oppose Congress. It is to remove the obstacles Congress places across the path to our ideal, not to make blind anti-Congress feeling our nature. Were we of that disposition, our camp would be filled with every available opportunist. It is not.

Likewise, we are called haters of Brahmins. If that were true, we would hate Sankaracharya because he is a Brahmin and support a Tamil monastic superior merely because he is Tamil; condemn Sir C. P. because he is a Brahmin and flirt with Sir Shanmugam because he is Tamil; condemn Acharya because he is a Brahmin and support Avinashi because he is Tamil; condemn P. Sri because he is a Brahmin and support T. K. C. because he is Tamil; condemn Kapilar because he is a Brahmin and support Kamban because he is Tamil. We would follow that meaningless course.

If hatred of Brahmins alone were our work, the movement's headquarters would not be in Erode but in Tirunelveli, and instead of assembling a Self-Respect army we would gather a holy congregation of Saivite devotees. The charge is meaningless. We dislike ritual bundles, rolling prayer beads, almanacs, obsession with antiquity, and caste arrogance wherever they occur. These qualities anger us when found among Brahmins, and perhaps anger us even more when found among Tamils. We have no dispute with Brahmins over partitioned property and have suffered no disappointment after expecting benefit from them.

Would a party make personal hostility to them its sole work for so many years, enduring hardship and losing pleasures? We seek to remove Brahminism because it blocks and damages the ideal journey we undertook to reach our objective, just as we would fill a pit across our path.

Condemning Congress ministers, opposing Congress, and criticising Brahminism are all acts that clear a path toward our objective. To imagine that these are our nature and our entire work is an error born of failing to understand our ideal.

The imprisonment of the Razakars, arrest of Razvi, and alteration of the Nizam's rule were intervening events on the great road toward establishing popular government in Hyderabad. Likewise, criticism of the Ministry, opposition to Congress, and criticism of Brahminism are difficulties we encounter on the road toward our ideal of creating a new condition for the country. We are not fools who mistake these for the whole of our work.

### Images 10, 6 And 4

I said our objective is a new condition for the country. I did not say a new Ministry, or even rule by a new party. A new condition is required. Government alone must not change; the people's outlook must change. Changing the rulers is insufficient. The minds of the governed must change; a revolution must occur in thought.

Because this is our objective, our task is not only difficult but incapable of quick completion. Immediate rewards cannot be seen. Political gamblers seeking profit will not favour such a movement; only warriors of principled struggle will.

Those who desire immediate political profit leap here and there like grasshoppers. When a tree bears fruit, they become bats. If a storm approaches, they seek holes and alleys. When hardship and loss threaten, they shoulder a *kavadi*. They continually calculate their accounts and rejoice.

Our ideal is not like that. A scientist invents an instrument to see distant objects but is not satisfied with the instrument. Its aid lets him observe planets, which delights him, but that delight is not enough. He wishes to learn everything about those planets, and after learning it, thinks further about how to use that knowledge. One victory opens the way to another task. His work continually expands and never stops midway.

The work we have undertaken is the same. Changes in the Ministry or official establishment do not by themselves satisfy us. We ask only what means those changes supply for achieving our ideal of a new condition for the country.

Twenty years ago, who was foremost among lawyers? Alladi. Among doctors? Rangachari. Among speakers? The Right Honourable Sastri. Among singers? Ariyakudi. In drama? Kittappa. In comedy? Samanna. Seeing one small class alone enjoy opportunities across every field, people asked in anguish, "Are members of every other class mere blocks of wood?" Agitation strengthened, an awakening arose, and victory came.

Today we have Barrister Ethiraj, Judge Somasundaram, Dr Gurusami, Sir Ramasami, Chittoor, Desikar, Bhagavathar, and our own king of comedy. These men of ability proclaim that we possess talent and can succeed in any field if the barriers are removed. All of us feel inward joy and pride. But are we completely satisfied that our task has ended? No. They have become victors, and we now desire to see the country receive the benefits their abilities should produce. We must even criticise many of them and insist: "You possess talent; use it for the country."

Desikar's sweet music alone does not satisfy us. What benefit comes from singing only of the Lord at Chidambaram? He should also sing of Kuppan labouring in the field and Suppan who drains his labour, and thereby teach the people understanding. Is it enough for sweet sound to benefit only the ear?

If our policy were merely hatred of Brahmins, we would rejoice at seeing Tamils capture and govern every position Brahmins had occupied, declare our task complete, and happily sing the benediction. But we have not rested with the joy of destroying the cruel monopoly one particular class held in every field.

Through their success, those victors removed one injustice. They must now use their talent to serve the country and help achieve our objective.

Can we admire the lyric, "He came in my dream and mingled with me," merely because Desikar rather than Ariyakudi sings it? Does it benefit the country? Because we do not possess the petty hatred that finds trivial pleasure merely in replacing Ariyakudi with Desikar, we tell Desikar, sometimes rather sharply, to employ his musical gifts for the people's new life.

We seek two changes in every field. First, the cruel monopoly of one class, including in the arts, must be destroyed. Second, every field must labour to create a new condition for the country.

The first object has succeeded to some extent, and victory continues. Omandurar becoming Chief Minister while Dr Rajan remained available, and Kamaraj defeating Acharya to become leader, are great examples. The consequences of those two victories remind us very clearly that the second part of our object remains unfinished.

Omandurar governs: victory. But his mode of government injures our ideal of a new condition for the country: pain. Victory and pain! Pain even after victory! Only another victory can remove it. Our work therefore did not end with the first victory; a task remains.

Can we create a new condition for the country merely by capturing power somehow, with anyone's help and the strength of any alliance? Certainly not. Before the full strength and proper support required to create that new condition are secured, the Dravidar Kazhagam believes it must not make office its aim or become useful to political gamblers gazing at velvet cushions.

It continues to insist upon its broad programme for creating a new condition and seeks to make that the people's purpose too.

### Images 1, 7 And 8

Some offer an extraordinary suggestion: "Why keep talking about all that? Point out Congress's mistakes, unite everyone dissatisfied with Congress rule, win the election, sit on the seat of power, and then try your hand at governing." The Dravidar Kazhagam refuses to value such unstable, meaningless, and fruitless political play.

Future elections will no longer be a goddess granting boons only to Congress. Talk of the auspicious yellow ballot box is disappearing; people increasingly call the yellow box a famine box. We know it, and the rulers must learn it. A recent by-election should have taught them.

The Congress candidate, Gopalasami, was a prominent Brahmin with wealth and campaign machinery. Newspapers advocated for him. Ministers rushed to seek support. Dr Subbarayan toured like a whirlwind; Kamaraj roared; the Chief Minister issued a statement; motorcars of many kinds appeared. Against all this stood Karur Rathinam, not on behalf of any party but as an independent. No newspapers sang his praise, no campaign workers served him, no car carried him in procession, and he had no money. Because of the Salem resolution, he could contest only after leaving the Dravidar Kazhagam, which took no part in the election. During the Karur disturbance case, the Government had charged him under numerous sections, from arson to incitement to plunder, but the case was dismissed.

Such a man stood against the representative of the mighty, powerful, and disciplined Congress organisation. He received more than seven thousand votes; Gopalasami received ten thousand. Victory gave Gopalasami an Assembly seat, but the election also showed how far the people's outlook had changed. Thoughtful observers will understand. Despite every supporting force, how many votes did Gopalasami receive? How many went to an individual without party, propaganda, or money? Does this not show that the glory of the yellow box is fading?

In such a situation, imagine the fate of the Gopalasamis if the Dravidar Kazhagam weakened its programme for creating a new condition, sought only to defeat the ruling Congress by any means, and fielded its Rathinams. A skilful and discerning musician detects the slightest deviation in pitch. An inferior performer asks what went wrong even after the audience claps and laughs. If Congress possesses political judgment, it can understand the lesson of this by-election. Will it?

Coming to power is not so difficult. Nor is speaking words that enchant people, or finding allies. Winning an election through these means and becoming the ruling party is not impossible. But that is not our purpose. We want a new condition for the country. The introduction of Hindi damages that objective; that is why we oppose it. We oppose the various other forces we condemn because they too damage our objective, not merely to gain office somehow.

Some ignorantly divide our programme into fragments like the blind men describing the elephant; many others do so maliciously. From the charge that we seek to overthrow the Congress Ministry to the slander that we are villains who blaspheme God, they use these fragments to make the very people whose welfare we labour to protect oppose us.

Our objective is to create a new condition in this country, not to dominate a party or pursue the servile ambition of office, but to attain the great aim of a new life. As we continue this great undertaking, we must oppose everything that crosses our path and damages the work. But no particular opposition at a particular moment should be mistaken for our final aim or our only thought.

Not only our opponents but our comrades must impress this subtle point upon their minds.

When we oppose the Ministry's conduct, our friends should not think, "Very well, overthrowing these ministers is the task; whom can we seek out and join for that purpose?" When we condemn Congress, we must not cultivate everyone made hostile to Congress for various reasons as an ally and friend. When we condemn Brahminism, we must not recruit lovers of antiquity merely because they are Tamil.

We are an army with a distinct objective. The road is exceedingly hard. More people will entice than threaten us upon it. Pits will be covered with green leaves. There will be not only stinging beetles but singing cuckoos. Driving away the beetle is insufficient; we must not become enchanted by the cuckoo's voice and forget our duty. Eagles will appear in the colour of parrots; form and colour alone cannot reveal character.

As warriors in a liberation struggle travelling such a road, we seek an ideal land. We oppose whatever can destroy that ideal. Opposition alone is not our whole work, nor does our work end with opposition. We oppose because it is necessary to our greater undertaking. We must continue along the road and reach the objective.

03-06-1968 அன்று பாலர் அரங்கில் (தற்போதைய
கலைவாணர் அரங்கம்) நடைபெற்ற, தலைவர் கலைஞர்
பிறந்தநாள் விழாவில் பேரறிஞர் அண்ணா அவர்கள்
பேசியதாவது :

தம்பி கருணாநிதியினுடைய ஆற்றல் பற்றியும் ஆற்றல்
காரணமாகப் பெற்ற வெற்றிகள் பற்றியும் - பெற்ற வெற்றிகள்
எல்லாம் நாட்டு முன்னேற்றத்துக்குப் பயன்பட்டன என்பது
பற்றியும் - அந்த ஆற்றலும் வெற்றியும் நாட்டுக்கு இனியும் நல்ல
முறையில் பயன்படும் என்பது பற்றியும் - நண்பர்கள் பலர்
எடுத்துச் சொல்லக் கேட்பதில் எனக்கு மிகுந்த பெருமிதம்
உண்டாகும் என்பதில் யாருக்கும் ஐயப்பாடில்லை.

தி. மு. கழகத்தில் இத்தகையதொரு பாராட்டு என்னுடைய
தம்பிமார்கள் ஒவ்வொருவருக்கும் உரித்தானது என்பதிலேயும்
ஐயமில்லை.

தி.மு. கழகத்தில் பணியாற்றும் ஒவ்வொருவரும் பல்வேறு
வகையான அறிவாற்றலைப் பெற்றிருக்கிறார்கள் என்பது
மட்டுமல்லாமல்; அவர்கள் பெற்றிருக்கும் ஒவ்வொரு
அறிவாற்றலையும் கழகத்தின் மூலமாக நாட்டின்
முன்னேற்றத்திற்காகவும் தக்க முறையில் பயன்படுத்துகிறார்கள்
என்பதை நினைவூட்டத்தக்க விதத்தில் இந்தப் பிறந்தநாள்
விழாக்கள் நடத்தப்படுகின்றன.

இந்தப் பாராட்டு விழாவில் பாசத்துடன் பாராட்டினார்கள்
என்பதில்தான் தனிச் சிறப்பும், தனிப் பெருமையும் இருக்கிறது.

கருணாநிதியினுடைய இலக்கியச் சிறப்பை, எழுத்து
நடையைப் பாராட்டிய ம. பொ. சிவஞானமும், பன்மொழிப்
புலவர் அப்பாத்துரையும் வியந்து பாராட்டினார்கள் என்று நான்

நம்முடைய நாட்டைப்பற்றி பாராட்டி எழுதியவர்

அவர் எழுதியிருப்பது நூற்றுக்கு நூறு உண்மைதான் என

ஆனால் அவர் நம்மவராகவும் இருந்து - நாம் விரும்பும்

அதை உலகத்துக்கு அளிக்கிறார் என்றால்தான் பாசத்தோடு

## English Translation

The following is the speech delivered by Arignar Anna at the birthday celebration of leader Kalaignar held on 3 June 1968 at Balar Arangam, now Kalaivanar Arangam:

No one can doubt that I feel immense pride when I hear so many friends speak about younger brother Karunanidhi's abilities, the victories those abilities have won, the way all those victories have served the country's progress, and the good use to which that ability and success will continue to be put for the country.

Nor is there any doubt that every one of my younger brothers in the DMK deserves such praise.

These birthday celebrations remind us not only that everyone working in the DMK possesses many kinds of intellectual ability, but also that each of them properly uses every ability he possesses, through the organisation, for the country's advancement.

The distinctive excellence and particular honour of this celebration lie in the affection with which the praise has been offered.

I do not think that M. P. Sivagnanam and the multilingual scholar Appadurai praised Karunanidhi's literary distinction and writing style merely out of astonishment. They are themselves eminent writers; that is why I say they praised him with affection.

They have praised him knowingly and affectionately because of the special happiness they feel that a young man belonging to our movement has attained such mastery in literature, that literature is thereby enriched, and that the Tamil language as a whole is elevated.

Praise offered with affection certainly has a value of its own, beyond every other kind of praise.

If we praise an American writer here in Madras, we praise him with wonder. If an Englishman has written appreciatively about our country, we praise him with pleasure. If we recognise that what he has written is entirely true, we praise him with understanding. But only when he is one of our own, writes in the way we desire, commends the ideal we cherish, and gives it to the world can we praise him with affection.

Every tribute offered by our friends today was filled with that affection.

They spoke of Karunanidhi's power as a writer and speaker, his ability to write plays, and his talent for acting in them.

If people in other parties fail to understand that all this is affectionate appreciation, they may begin to think that to make a name one must write a play, to become famous one must act in at least one play, to be praised one must write two articles, and to have a celebration held one must write two books. We see across Tamil Nadu the resulting waste of money and time and the breaking of spirits.

Only when we understand how Karunanidhi acquired these abilities can we grasp the inner meaning of this celebration.

Before entering any of these fields, a person must first feel that there is something in his heart that he must tell others. Once that urge arises, he may try to express it as an article, offer it as poetry, write a play for others to perform, or act in it himself. All these are forms for conveying an idea one wishes to communicate. If the form is to be colourful, the thought must first be colourful. Karunanidhi acquired such vivid thought at a very young age.

While he was studying at high school, an article he sent me appeared in the *Dravida Nadu* journal I was conducting. Later, when I went to Tiruvarur, I asked that the writer of the article be brought to me. They placed before me a boy so thin and simple in appearance that one might wonder whether even his body could grow to its present size.

“Did you write that article? What are you doing?” I asked. When he told me that he was studying at Tiruvarur High School, I patted him on the back and advised him to stop writing articles for a while, finish school, and then return to writing.

Our friend M. P. Sivagnanam said that Karunanidhi did not have the opportunity to earn a university degree. Had he possessed even then his present habit of following my words to the letter, he would certainly have earned one. Not only would he have obtained the degree, his abilities would now be many times greater. My other younger brothers should learn from this that no one has come to harm by listening to me!

I do not think his worth diminished because he did not receive a university degree. In many countries, people without degrees have attained great fame. But it would also be wrong to conclude that a university education is unnecessary. It is good to have one. When we say that one may succeed without it, that truth is mingled with regret, not joy.

From his youth he cultivated ideas out of an eagerness to tell the people something. He first conveyed them through handwritten publications, then through speeches from the stage, articles, poetry, drama, and cinema. Today, as a Minister, he is giving those ideas all the practical form that ministerial office permits. We all praise that with delight.

Some doubted whether a person devoted to the arts could fulfil public responsibilities. I told them that a good and accomplished artist would display in every responsibility the same ability that he displays in art.

Art is that which has been ordered and shaped. Artists who possess that disciplined quality will bring order to any field they enter, lend it artistic finish, and make its benefit available to others. Others came to share the confidence I had. With understanding, happiness, and affection, they have praised Karunanidhi's work during a year in the Cabinet and his valuable service during the preceding ten years as a legislator. I want every one of my younger brothers to possess these qualities in full.

As M. P. Sivagnanam said, not only Karunanidhi but everyone in the DMK, including all those in its front rank, was born into an ordinary family. The country began enquiring into their families only after learning about them; there are very few among us who were first recognised because their families were already known. I do not say this with any sense that it is wrong or inferior.

No one should imagine that a movement run by people from ordinary families for ordinary people must itself remain ordinary.

It must attain excellence. That excellence must not be merely the personal refinement attained by each participant, nor merely the excellence the organisation attains through their collective effort, but the excellence the country gains through it. History must record the achievement that those born in ordinary families, through a movement conducted for ordinary people, raised people who had been ordinary to a state of distinction.

To achieve that, we must not merely acquire abilities like Karunanidhi's; we must examine how he acquired them.

If someone observed, without his knowledge, how many hours Karunanidhi sleeps each day, they would understand that labour is the foundation of his ability.

This ability did not arrive without labour. Ability never does. It may perhaps happen to a few holy men and ascetics, though even people in those circles say it does not endure beyond a few years. Ordinary people like us can gain ability only through work.

If Karunanidhi has adapted the *Silappathikaram* to suit the present age, he must have studied it methodically at least a hundred times.

He is deeply engaged with Sangam literature and keen to train himself in Tamil grammar. He does not cast study aside and say, “I already have fame; I no longer need Tamil grammar.” Though little time is available to him, he uses it well to enrich the knowledge he already possesses.

I spoke of arduous labour. He does not have even a trace of the physique suited to it. Valour drips from his speech; the moment he sits down, sweat pours from him; as soon as he returns home, medicinal drops go in. Two hours later, family members sit with hands on their cheeks, some stroke their chins, and the elders slap their heads, worrying, “Why must he roam like this, come back crying out like this, work himself so hard there, and suffer like this here?” Doctors around him debate whether it might be this illness or that one. If you saw all this, you would know that he does not possess the build suited to such punishing work.

As though by some strange arrangement, the leaders in Tamil Nadu who do possess strong physiques employ them for many other purposes, not for work of this kind. It is people without any physique at all, such as M. P. Sivagnanam and Karunanidhi, who labour relentlessly and then lie in hospital clutching their stomachs, heads, or necks.

The reason for such hard work is the humility to feel that what we have acquired is still insufficient to give the country. The moment a man thinks, “I know everything,” one may write his history, publish a cheap edition, and announce to the world that there will be no second edition.

Only those who feel they still have much to learn can give the world a great deal. That desire makes them labour hard. There is a difference, however, between Karunanidhi's labour and that of others. After reaching a certain position, some say, “I shall attend to these tasks; you attend to those.” Some engage in particular work while overseeing other matters. That is natural to them.

Karunanidhi is different. Whatever work the organisation assigns him, or whatever work he himself thinks must be done for it, he never distinguishes between tasks by asking, “Must I do this? Could not so-and-so do it? Might I do something else?” From his days as a student in Tiruvarur, he grew into an organisation worker, entered the ranks of its leaders, became one worthy of praise among them, and then became a Minister. At no stage did he divide work into what he should and should not do. He possesses the will to complete any task, and everyone must acquire it.

Only through that spirit can we gain a strength and brilliance no other movement possesses. Our Minister Muthusamy was said to have recited a poem at the morning poetry gathering, demonstrating that every one of my younger brothers is developing such abilities. All these gifts exist among us. Instead of pointing and saying that this person or that one will take care of something, each of us must use every capacity available to express our ideas. That is one good message we can receive on this birthday.

M. P. Sivagnanam and the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly said that some had tried to attack Karunanidhi. I find nothing surprising in that. I would truly have been surprised if no one had tried. A wise man once said, “If no one criticises you, worry for a while. It means you have failed to express the matter strongly enough to trouble other minds.”

In the same way, if the DMK, its Ministers, leaders, or workers are attacked, it is because those who first appeared as question marks have today become exclamation marks. Once they became exclamation marks, many found them repugnant. Our organisation is administering the Government amid an atmosphere of intense resentment.

Tamil Nadu was full of those who believed we were unfit even to be in politics, that we understood nothing, and who persuaded themselves that we were fit for nothing. Even when we were the Opposition, they said we were unworthy of that position. If we have now become the ruling party and conduct government in a peaceful manner admired by people across India, the ordinary human heart, not a cultivated one, finds it very difficult to tolerate.

There can be neither a bull that does not fear a red cloth nor a former ruling party that remains unafraid when the Opposition begins to govern, in our country or perhaps anywhere in the world.

If deeds done in that anger end in attacks, I will feel even more pride than I felt when Karunanidhi became a Minister. If opponents attack him in his capacity as Minister, I shall walk upright and say, “My younger brother is one who can bear anything.”

We are not people who can claim that not even a fly or ant has fallen upon us. There are countless filthy pits into which we have been rolled, many mountain slopes we have climbed and descended, many plots we saw and escaped, and innumerable forms of opposition we confronted. If we attained our present position after all that, I respectfully ask those once again subjecting us to the same cruelty: to what still greater height do you intend to carry us through this cruelty?

You reviled us, and the people began to praise us. You beat us, and the people embraced us. You dismissed us, and the people seated us upon the throne of government. You continue the same conduct. I know of nothing higher than this, and I am bewildered, my opponents, not knowing what still greater benefit you intend to bestow upon us by persisting in it.

Suppose one of our Ministers were attacked and killed. When readers reached the full stop in his life story, they would finish it in tears. They would not end by saying, “Oh, he died? Never mind.” That must be considered.

I mention this because our concern, as M. P. Sivagnanam said, is that such violence damages democracy. Attacks are nothing new to Karunanidhi. Twenty-two years ago, when ours was still the Dravidar Kazhagam, he not only wrote plays for many dramatic companies but acted in one. During a disturbance in Puducherry, others beat him, assumed he was dead, and left him by the roadside. No one can mount an attack greater than that. I say so because it occurred in Puducherry; the “Puducherry” attack itself went only that far. An attack by those who bring “Puducherry” to other places cannot be more terrible. On his birthday we rejoice and take pride that Karunanidhi survived all that and reached this position.

Had you closely observed the relationship between me and the younger brothers who work with me in the organisation, you could have recognised it even at today's function.

Today I am said to preside. The presiding person should announce who will speak. I occupied the chair, but Anbil Dharmalingam performed the task I should have done. Did I stop him and do it myself because he was doing my work? No. Everyone associated with me has gradually learned to share and perform the work that would otherwise fall to me.

That is why our organisation contains so many people who together possess abilities not found even in the Congress when it was the ruling party. Other parties keep one person for each task. It is not so in ours. Even where a task would ordinarily have to be done by me, many people like Karunanidhi have acquired the intelligence to undertake it in my place. That is why the organisation today stands in Tamil Nadu as a fine form of collective endeavour.

During my tour of America, some political leaders asked whether confusion might arise in my organisation or Government while I was away. “It will not,” I said. They asked why. “Because people who think as I do and act as I would are there,” I replied. “You seem to ask this with the fascist ruler Mussolini in mind.” They asked me what story I meant, and I told them what I knew.

Mussolini had a son-in-law who stood next to him, a very able and powerful man. Seeing his ability, Mussolini took him along whenever he travelled abroad. For a long time the son-in-law was pleased. “My father-in-law goes nowhere without me; he takes me everywhere,” he reportedly said. Mussolini's explanation was different: “I do not take him because of that. I take him because, if I leave him behind, I fear he may do something here.”

I said in America that no such condition existed in our organisation or Government. The organisation is filled with people who understand me completely, and Karunanidhi occupies a distinguished place among those who may be said to understand me most fully.

If I am worried, Karunanidhi is the first to detect it. If I am angry, he is the first to detect that too. Our Navalar, unaware that I am angry, may say something to me and, after I answer with four angry words, announce, “Anna is angry.”

Because Karunanidhi belongs to the artistic world, he understands as soon as he arrives: “Ah, he seems a little angry today.” First he discovers why I am angry and with whom, then speaks accordingly, reduces my anger, and leaves after persuading me that he too is angry about the same thing.

I do not say that I have been outwitted. Since I too had links with the artistic world before Karunanidhi, I know something of those methods. That is one special quality of art: when an actor portrays grief, an artist's skill lies in making others feel and instantly recognise it. In the same way, Karunanidhi instantly recognises my anger, passions, and worries and assists well, with concern to change them. I can boldly say that Karunanidhi's equal in this is Karunanidhi himself, because, looking around, I see he is not here.

Do not even tell Karunanidhi that I said it. Newspapers should not publish a great headline declaring, “Karunanidhi's Equal Is Karunanidhi.” We must prevent him from thinking, “Good, I have satisfied Anna.”

The country and I expect far more through him. His ability must give the country achievements many times greater than those already completed. Therefore, of the many thoughts I have about him, I push several back and offer only a few, saying, “This is enough for now.” A true elder brother should behave that way toward his younger brother.

An elder brother does not take everything in his box and give it all away to the younger one, leaving an empty box. If the younger brother then thinks, “Was that all the box contained?” the elder will have no standing in the family. The elder earns respect by opening the box, giving a little, saying much remains, and locking it again.

In the same way, every younger brother should earn my praise. Every comrade of the organisation should earn it, and “comrades” includes our women comrades. This birthday celebration should inspire each of them to do so.

Whether told, “Lay your head upon the railway track,” or, “Serve as Minister for Public Works,” Karunanidhi accepts both as commands of the same kind and uses his ability to the fullest in carrying them out. The country knows that I therefore possess immense affection and respect for younger brother Karunanidhi.

Likewise, the distinct ability of every one of my younger brothers serving as Ministers has been of great use at every stage of the organisation's growth.

Had we not received the beauty of Navalar's distinctive pure-Tamil style, it is highly doubtful whether we would have developed so deep an attachment to Tamil. Had the organisation not acquired through our friend Mathiazhagan the ability to repeat a point relentlessly until the listener accepts it, it is doubtful whether we could have made the country accept so many ideas.

Thus every younger brother, Minister, and comrade uses a particular ability well.

Consider our N. V. Natarajan. His labour is unceasing; he spins like a top. His appearance is such that anyone seeing him might think, “This man knows nothing.” Yet behind that appearance is his characteristic command of every detail. Without it, we must ask how ordinary people could have trusted this organisation and joined it.

Our organisation is filled with people of many such abilities.

I do not say this merely for your sake or to seek pride for myself. I offer it as a friend's advice to the Congress, which hopes to take our place: gather such varied people of ability to your side.

Those you now retain seem to have been chosen only by calculating, “This man abuses them this much; that man abuses them even more.” Where has that taken you? In five years it has brought you to your present position. My civility does not permit me even to speculate where it may take you in another five.

A party, movement, or organisation must gather many people who cultivate their abilities. Tamil Nadu has an abundance of gifted people who can be brought together in that way.

Poets sing and present verse while tens of thousands who are not poets sit and listen, relish it, and enjoy its flavour. This shows that the feeling for Tamil that existed then remains today. Younger brother Karunanidhi has proved his ability and success in nourishing that feeling for Tamil in many ways. I am not only delighted by it but duty-bound to thank him.

My nature is to desire that everyone in our organisation and all its friends cultivate some feeling for art. I have a deep interest in it.

As the Speaker of our Legislative Assembly said, Karunanidhi has reached the point where “Kalaignar” means Karunanidhi and Karunanidhi means Kalaignar.

Had he devoted himself only to the arts, he could have earned lakhs of rupees and immersed himself in comfort. Instead, he remained in that artistic world and laboured with the purpose of putting art to use in politics. For the past twenty-five years, he engaged in politics without thinking that participation mattered only if it led to power; he demonstrated his resolve to remain even in opposition. Since coming to office, his ceaseless work has proved that resolve has not weakened. Fortunately, Karunanidhi is only forty-five!

We need not say he has turned sixty, sixty-four, fifty, or fifty-six. Since he is forty-five, we can still add many years, twenty, thirty, or forty. Today his age is still one of youth.

Some mentioned the baldness of his head and said it showed his age. Baldness in the old has wrinkles; baldness in the young has a gleam. Look closely at his bald head next time and you will understand the truth of what I say: there are no wrinkles upon it.

Thus this forty-five-year-old youth still has abundant service to render our organisation and, through it, our country.

If he is told that thousands praise his intellect, that comrades of our organisation and allied parties praise him, this celebration will give him fresh enthusiasm and help him work with still greater resolve. I firmly believe that.

I must mention Karunanidhi's ability in another important field.

As one who leads an organisation, I have made extensive use of Karunanidhi in establishing relations with other parties. Though I use our other Ministers and comrades in many fields, Karunanidhi has gone to others, explained my thoughts properly to them, and conveyed their thoughts properly to me. Many times I have used him to conduct the mature discussions needed to create such friendship.

His success in most of those instances has given me very great satisfaction.

It is a new and rather difficult field. Its difficulty is not the only concern. When we send him somewhere, we send him expecting him to return to our side. He must understand that, speak where he goes, and avoid remaining there.

Those who saw the film *Chintamani* long ago will know that returning from the place one has gone is not so easy!

Wherever he goes, however, his success is not due to his ability alone. The people to whom I send him possess the same good qualities. They have not misused the situation, but have created a feeling of comradeship. If so many parties joined in alliance at the last election, Karunanidhi must be named among those who worked most successfully at its foundation. With ability, persistence, and mature conduct, he won valuable success for the organisation in those matters.

I have not the slightest doubt that today's gathering will help all these abilities grow many times over and continue serving the country well.

I must also say that all the good and eminent people, allied-party leaders, and organisation comrades who praised my younger brother in my presence have offered me a special feast.

There is pleasure in buying a zari-bordered cloth for me and asking me to wear it. But I feel a special joy in buying my younger brother a zari-bordered veshti and seeing him wear it and walk about.

Now it is not only a younger brother: a grandson too walks in a new shirt, and seeing him genuinely brings joy.

The emergence of new leaders as sons and grandsons restores youth to those growing old. A grandfather cannot think, “Oh, were we not once like this? Did they not dress us too in velvet shirts?” without watching a grandson run.

A father cannot feel that way without seeing a son walk so. Likewise, an elder brother cannot feel the family's full honour without hearing his younger brother praised. This special celebration has allowed us to recognise and rejoice in all the goodwill, praise, and honour enjoyed by this family called the organisation, and I express my happiness.

Our friend Ilamurugu worked to arrange this happiness and bring out this commemorative volume, but was involved in a car accident and is now in hospital, unable to participate fully in the celebration. That is a matter of concern. Fortunately, the doctors have said there are no signs in his body that give cause for anxiety. We regret that Ilamurugu, who wished the event to proceed well and hoped to behold it with delight, cannot take part because of the accident. I thank his wife, Tiruchirappalli district secretary Anbil Dharmalingam, the other comrades from Madras, and comrades from several districts for their great efforts to make the event a success.

Although this is a celebration honouring Kalaignar Karunanidhi, everyone leaving will say how much more Karunanidhi himself would have done had he conducted the same celebration for someone else. My dear younger brother has the individual ability to leave his own distinctive stamp upon any task he undertakes. I rejoice that this rare honour paid to him is an honour paid to my family.

As I have long and repeatedly said, nothing prevents me from taking pride in having such a person as my younger brother. That is why I have often said, “He who has a younger brother fears no army.” The number of such younger brothers must grow. They must be found throughout the country, support good people, and labour for the nation's advancement. This is my appeal.

## Translator's Notes

The translation preserves all twenty scan sections, the dated introductory note, quoted dialogue, political and theatrical anecdotes, and the complete closing appeal. “Younger brother” and “elder brother” retain Anna's movement-family vocabulary as well as their literal familial sense.

The title proverb, தம்பியுடையான் படைக்கஞ்சான், is rendered “He Who Has a Younger Brother Fears No Army.” In the final quotation the printed source uses the expanded form தம்பியுடையான் படைக்கு அஞ்சான் with the same meaning.

கலைஞர் is retained as the honorific “Kalaignar” where it identifies M. Karunanidhi and translated as “artist” where Anna discusses artistic ability. The railway-track command alludes to Karunanidhi's role in the Kallakudi agitation; the source itself does not name Kallakudi in this passage.

Scan-backed OCR corrections were applied before translation to seventeen malformed readings on Images 1, 7-10, 15, and 18-20, including M. P. Sivagnanam's initials, ordinary people, the velvet-shirt recollection, and the final “many times.” No historical claim or period usage has been silently modernised.

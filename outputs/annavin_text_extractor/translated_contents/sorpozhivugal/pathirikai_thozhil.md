## English Translation

A public discussion between Anna and M. P. Sivagnanam on journalism, held on 3 September 1948.

**Anna:** Greetings. We are going to debate whether journalism is a public service or a private enterprise.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yes, it is a useful debate. Is it enough for the two of us to discuss it? Should not the people of the country debate it too?

**Anna:** Yes, let us set that debate in motion. What is your view? What kind of occupation is journalism: public service or private enterprise?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Not only journalism. Every occupation in the country should have public service as its purpose; it should not remain solely a private undertaking.

**Anna:** Yes, that is a noble ideal. Every action of the country's people must not be directed only toward personal welfare.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Whatever each person does should be conducted in a manner suited to the common good.

**Anna:** Let us consider actual practice.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Let us, my friend. But we must examine whether practice enables that noble ideal to be realised, and if it does not, reform practice so that the ideal can be fulfilled.

**Anna:** Looking at journalism with that good purpose, what do you see?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** You are asking whether journalism is public service or private enterprise, are you not?

**Anna:** They are asking us both.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yes. It is not good for any occupation to exist only for private profit, is it?

**Anna:** It is not. When industries pursue private profit alone, economic inequality and social unrest run rampant throughout the world.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Newspapers should undertake the higher task of cultivating refinement in such a world.

**Anna:** Yes. If they adhere unwaveringly to that purpose, no finer public service exists.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** The purpose must be worthy and the action must match it. Is it enough merely to advertise oneself as the guardian of public welfare?

**Anna:** Worth is attained only when purpose and practice coincide. Viewed in that way, journalism should not be a private business, a device that earns profit for its proprietor. It should be organised as an opportunity to perform public service.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yes. Who can deny it? Who would openly say, "I use a newspaper like a grocery shop or iron factory to make a profit, secure my comfort and family life, accumulate property and acquire land"?

**Anna:** They would not say so. But it is true that money can be amassed through journalism just as through other industries.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** True. Money can be accumulated through journalism, and it has been.

**Anna:** Likewise, people invest large capital in newspapers, employ thousands and install modern scientific equipment just as in other industries. We see every method of commerce operating within them.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** In organisation and scale, many newspaper offices today resemble great factories.

**Anna:** Yes. A newspaper may sell several lakh copies and charge twenty rupees per inch for advertising. Of twelve pages, perhaps only six contain reports and opinion, while the remainder carries advertisements. It becomes a great enterprise with lakhs in receipts and expenses and thousands of workers.

Can such a newspaper be called public service? Are not the proprietor's income, standard of living, land, motorcars and other comforts products of its profit? How can we unblushingly call an enterprise that makes him a mansion-dwelling magnate a public service? How will people accept that claim? One merchant becomes rich by selling cloth; this merchant becomes rich by selling news. One capitalist opens a silk shop with ten thousand rupees and earns many times his investment; a man who began a newspaper with eight or ten thousand acquires a ten-thousand-rupee motorcar and a mansion costing forty or fifty thousand. How can his occupation alone be called public service? Why should it be, and how is that description fitting?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** A cloth merchant promotes sales by saying, "Woven on our own looms; the dye is guaranteed." In the same manner, the newspaper merchant says, "We have special correspondents; we are sending our own correspondent to the Paris Conference; only our newspaper gives complete reports." He treats news as merchandise. The expense of gathering it is like the cost of producing goods in any other business, and the reports too are sold for profit. This becomes a private and profit-making enterprise.

**Anna:** Newspapers in western countries sell several million copies apiece and accumulate enormous profits.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Knowing journalism to be profitable, many wealthy men there have become newspaper owners. One magnate runs several newspapers in Britain or America.

**Anna:** Why only there? Capitalists in our country too have recognised journalism as one among many industries. The taste of profit has drawn them into it.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yes. What difference does it make whether profit is earned by selling butter or lime? Profit remains profit.

**Anna:** They do not enter because they regard it as service to the people, the noble work of spreading knowledge or a duty essential to democracy. Specialists plan how to apply unrestricted commercial methods: publish at the right time and for the right purpose, discover where public taste moves, present those portions attractively, persuade great numbers to buy, and make a profit.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** True. The gain is not only monetary. Wealth accumulates and influence grows.

**Anna:** Yes. A mill owner may do little beyond investing capital. The worker's skill and exertion improve the cloth; people desire it, it spreads widely, and profit accumulates for the mill baron. Similarly, a newspaper proprietor employs experts who collect reports, specialists who make them enticing, writers of literary prose, fire-breathing polemicists and storm-raising commentators. He assigns work according to their abilities and gathers the profit. If the mill worker receives six annas, the writer may receive a little more. Is that not the only difference?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** The aims and methods of industrial specialists are as you describe. But do only such people run newspapers? Do all newspapers become devices for accumulating profit in this fashion?

**Anna:** No. People say that bats arrive when a tree bears fruit. When capitalists turn their gaze toward journalism, should we not infer that fruit has begun to ripen there, that it has become a profitable industry?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yet we also see newspapers struggling even to meet operating expenses, growing thin while their proprietors lose heart.

**Anna:** We see it from experience: the tale of a single person who is proprietor, editor, reporter, proofreader, manager and clerk all at once. There is no profit in what you describe, only abundant trouble. Such journals do not live long.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** That is precisely my point. Many newspapers exist in that condition, yet their publishers cheerfully wrestle with hardship and labour constantly, ignoring every difficulty. Are there no other occupations in the country? Do they know no other work, and can they learn none? Of course they can. Yet they continue publishing despite discomfort and lack of profit. Is not the reason their enthusiasm and belief that they perform public service?

**Anna:** It is enthusiasm. Though the task exceeds their strength, it keeps them working without weariness.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Is that enthusiasm not the quality of a public servant?

**Anna:** Yes. Such newspapers are public service, not private enterprise or a route to luxurious living.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Are not such newspapers the majority in our country? Does that not establish journalism as public service?

**Anna:** True, but as I noted, there are also many newspapers that turn their proprietors into magnates.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Looking at those, journalism does appear to be private enterprise.

**Anna:** We can draw a broad distinction. Newspapers that turn bare news into merchandise can, through the proprietor's skill, trade and expertise, be developed into profitable businesses. We cannot say the same of journals that propagate ideas.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Especially journals whose ideas swim against the current and shake public beliefs cannot grow into profitable enterprises.

**Anna:** Profit? Is not mere survival difficult? It takes courage to break social superstition, remove the evils of ancient arrangements, oppose exploitation, and ensure that base people who injure public welfare find no place or opportunity. Even with such courage, a journal conducted on those lines finds support extremely difficult to obtain. For its publisher it is no business at all. It yields suffering rather than profit and blame rather than praise. Those few who fearlessly perform that rare service despite everything face countless obstacles.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** They endure them all with enthusiasm.

**Anna:** Yes. Those who regard a newspaper as a profitable private business rejoice in calculating how much profit has risen from year to year. Those who regard it as an instrument for spreading a principle rejoice in measuring how far it has helped reform the conduct of the people.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** The person who regards journalism as service finds joy in that result.

**Anna:** Yet even those who conduct journalism upon a scale that yields great profit claim that they do so to fight for principle or promote public welfare, do they not?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yes. No one says, "I work merely for profit." Everyone says it is for the public. But examination makes the matter and the truth clear.

**Anna:** Even merchants in fireworks claim that, despite the thousands of available occupations, they manufacture and sell fireworks at festivals only to involve people in devotion and obtain divine grace.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Similar claims are made for every trade. In truth, many are not organised to serve public welfare. Newspapers that live luxuriously while carrying masses of advertisements earn profits from those advertisements, but what principal benefit does the public receive?

**Anna:** Shall we then decide that newspapers carrying many advertisements perform no public service?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** We cannot state that absolutely. But generally we can say this: newspapers that do not publish reports useful for remedying public grievances and promoting welfare, but instead carry masses of profitable advertisements and material serving amusement, excitement, gossip and fascination with the past, are undoubtedly private profit-making enterprises for their proprietors. Of course providing news is itself a form of service to the public.

**Anna:** That depends upon the news provided. If a newspaper lavishly publishes illustrated details of royal splendour, dress, palace events, feasts and revelry, will it bring clarity to people's minds or growth to their knowledge? What good can reading it produce in national life?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** None. It is only diversion and amusement.

**Anna:** Such a paper may sell countless copies, but it cannot be regarded as public service. In these democratic days, when knowledge is spreading, can newspapers that publish sorcery, astrology, fortune-telling, consecration ceremonies and the greatness of holy men be said to serve the public? Is that not selfish profit made by exploiting people's dullness and appetite for diversion?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Then are newspapers that champion rationalism public services and all others private enterprises?

**Anna:** Why should we not say so? We need not even limit it to championing rationalism. We can firmly say that newspapers perform public service when they examine conditions as they arise and publish proposals and plans that advance welfare. They should be unconcerned with profit.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** The fields lie dry and agriculture is ruined. One newspaper may portray the condition in pictures and verse. Another may go further and show the connection between it and the ruling party. A third may write that Inbananda Swami, after twenty years of penance in the Rishikesh mountains, predicted this very year ten years ago, using the dry fields to add lustre to the Swami's image. Another may say that ritual ablution and prayers to Varuna will bring rain and thus perform orthodox religious service. Can one condition not be used to promote many different ideas?

**Anna:** That is exactly what happens. Instead of employing such methods, a newspaper could explain how fields should be irrigated, what should be done when rains fail, and how other countries prevent famine in such circumstances. We could praise that newspaper for performing public service.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** Yes, but would people willingly read articles about such plans?

**Anna:** They would not. Are they not difficult to understand? People will read a lament composed upon ten deaths in a famine, but if told, "This is Denmark's method; this is Norway's plan," they will not read. Newspapers that write in that manner therefore will not sell widely.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** They will not, and they will make no profit, but surely their work will not be without benefit.

**Anna:** Benefit? For whom, and what benefit?

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** The publisher receives no profit. But if conscientious rulers notice a plan printed there and frame a new policy, the country will prosper and the people will live better.

**Anna:** Yes. Seeing that well-being is the reward for all the suffering endured by one who performs public service.

**M. P. Sivagnanam:** We may freely say that every newspaper conducted with that purpose performs public service.

## Translator's Notes

The source describes this as a public debate held on 3 September 1948. “M. P. Si.” is expanded in English as M. P. Sivagnanam, while the preserved Tamil source retains its abbreviated speaker label.

“சிந்து” in the famine example denotes a popular narrative or lament in verse; it is rendered contextually rather than transliterated.

The discussion distinguishes commercial news production from journals committed to reform, but it does not claim that circulation or advertising alone proves the absence of public service.

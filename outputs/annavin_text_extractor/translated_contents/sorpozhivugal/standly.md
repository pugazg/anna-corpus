## English Translation

The address delivered at the New Year celebration held at Stanley Medical College on 25 April 1967:

People here have spoken about the life the Tamil New Year brings. We must first formulate a definition of “life.” Are people truly living as they ought? Are we really living a good and complete life? Can we obtain a new life? We can answer these questions only after defining life.

In our country, it is doubtful whether eighty out of every hundred people truly live. Suppose two friends suddenly meet one day. If one asks the other, “How are you?” will the reply be, “I am flourishing; I am well; I am in a good condition”? Certainly not. The answer will only be, “Somehow, I exist.” Eighty out of every hundred people in our country live at the level of saying, “Somehow, I exist.” Why do eighty out of a hundred exist in this manner? They exist only because they have not died. They persist by begging and seeking alms. We live in this way because of the definition we have given life.

Even before the peoples of other countries had acquired knowledge, our people understood what the definition of life should be. Yet although we possess the rare Kural composed by Valluvar to establish true life, we have not obtained that life. The fact that Tamils do not know how to live in this manner raises doubt about whether we are worthy of Thiruvalluvar.

The good life a Tamil ought to live is not one that enables only himself to live. For a Tamil to live well means enabling others to live. Tamil literature teaches this. Attachment to Tamil itself teaches it.

Some people are suspicious when they see Tamils attached to Tamil; others are astonished. It is like being frightened by one’s own shadow. Although some Tamils may fear in that way, Tamil is advancing through these doubts in Tamil Nadu.

The Tamil was the first to regard the whole world as his home. No one can therefore classify such a Tamil’s attachment to his language as narrow-mindedness. No matter who obstructs or rejects it, attachment to the mother tongue will not diminish; it will well up and overflow.

The notion held by some that attachment to Tamil will injure nationalism and damage unity is meaningless. We are only now creating unity.

A pupil in an English school does not take a daily pledge to the unity of England. But in our country, either every day or once a year, we must pledge ourselves to Indian unity. The very act of saying, “I pledge that I shall conduct myself in obedience to Indian unity,” demonstrates that we are still creating that unity.

We see something similar in films. When actors portray husband and wife, the wife rises in the morning, touches her husband’s feet and brings her hands to her eyes, then bows to his mother before attending to her morning duties. This scene is included because the hero and heroine are not truly husband and wife but are only acting, and the audience must be persuaded to believe that they are married. In the same way, today we are being persuaded by the repeated cry, “Unity, unity.”

We must not forget that we are only now creating unity.

We imagine that if we squeeze by hand all the sugarcane in a field, hold the juice in our palms, and show it in the sunlight, we can produce sugar candy. First, we must see which cane contains the most juice.

Until now, we thought the sugarcane of Uttar Pradesh contained the most juice. That is incorrect. It is said that within India, cane grown in Maharashtra contains more juice than that grown in Uttar Pradesh, and cane grown in Tamil Nadu comes next. More sugarcane is now grown in Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra than in Uttar Pradesh.

We must select the cane that yields the most juice, remove what must be removed, extract the juice, and turn it into sugar candy. If we take only Uttar Pradesh cane, hold its juice in our hands, and expose it to sunlight, it will evaporate rather than become sugar candy.

Indian unity can be formed only if Tamil Nadu’s culture is part of it. If Indian unity is fashioned without our culture, it will be beyond our reach and will not adhere to our thought. The claim that attachment to Tamil will damage unity is inappropriate.

In my political life, after I abandoned the demand for Dravida Nadu, I went to New Delhi. In Parliament, Congress leaders from Madhya Pradesh and Bihar placed their hands upon my shoulders and said, “You have done well. You who had said, ‘We are leaving you,’ have now agreed, ‘We are with you.’ We congratulate you upon your decision.”

I replied, “Our party decided that it no longer wanted the policy of Dravida Nadu, and accordingly we abandoned the demand.” I then asked a Congress leader from Madhya Pradesh, “We have all agreed to remain with you. India’s geographical form is broad above and narrows as it extends southward. Since our land narrows on our side, will you settle ten million of us in Madhya Pradesh?”

After thinking for a while, he said, “That would create a new and dangerous problem.” Indian unity is of such a character that it creates a new and dangerous problem.

If ten million Tamils settled in Madhya Pradesh, people in neighbouring Uttar Pradesh would ask, “Who are these people? Where have they come from? Why have they come? What are they doing?” I do not say this as a joke; I say it with a wounded heart.

This is the present state of “Indian unity.” It will take another century for the unity now being formed to grow to a level accepted by everyone. Losing our distinct characteristics will not create Indian unity. If we seek to establish national unity by losing Tamil culture, it will be, as Bharati said, like selling both eyes to buy a painting.

No one can prevent our attachment to our mother tongue. Nor can we lose our culture.

We are only now creating unity in our country; unity has not yet taken full form. Even in ordinary speech we say “All India.” We call our radio “All India Radio.” Even the administrative services are called the “All India Services.” We repeatedly say “all India, all India” precisely so that no doubt will arise: “Is this India? Is that India?”

If someone told an Englishman that he must pledge himself to England’s unity, he would look suspiciously at the person making the demand. But in our country, we are told to pledge ourselves to Indian unity every day or once a year. We speak repeatedly of unity in order to persuade ourselves.

Tamil culture too must have a place within Indian unity. If the culture of Uttar Pradesh alone is called Indian unity, it will remain beyond our reach and will not adhere to our thought. Attachment to the mother tongue will not be subdued, no matter who obstructs or rejects it. It will well up and overflow. Speaking of nationalism cannot diminish attachment to one’s mother tongue.

## Translator's Notes

The source is a complete five-page address delivered at Stanley Medical College on 25 April 1967. The heading’s OCR contains damage in the parenthetical English spelling of Stanley, but the institutional name is clear. All Tamil OCR pages remain verbatim.

The event is called a “புத்தாண்டு விழா,” and the opening explicitly identifies it as Tamil New Year. English therefore makes that context clear in the title.

The source’s “நூற்றுக்கு எண்பது” is rendered literally as eighty out of every hundred. Its socioeconomic and demographic force is preserved as rhetoric and not independently verified.

The sugarcane discussion combines agricultural claims with an analogy for cultural contributions to unity. The claims about relative juice content and production are translated as stated.

“கண்ணிரண்டையும் விற்றுச் சித்திரம் வாங்குதல்” is a Bharati-derived image: sacrificing the very eyes needed to see in order to buy a picture. The English preserves the compact metaphor.

Political, cultural, agricultural, demographic, geographic, and historical assertions are translated in the speaker’s voice and have not been independently verified.

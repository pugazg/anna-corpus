## English Translation

In 1948, Arignar Anna stressed how slavery must be overthrown:

We have joyfully and proudly announced to the world that our motherland, trapped and ruined under foreign imperialism for a century or two, has gained freedom. But we must ask ourselves whether slavery has been destroyed root and branch, cage and all, or whether some form of slavery still remains within us, knowingly or unknowingly, and directs us. We must discover it and learn its cause.

If we do not, we will not merely fail to obtain a full and new life; the four quarters of the world will laugh at us and say, “Look at that blind man without eyes boasting heroically that he has broken his chains, unaware of the other chains still fastened upon him.”

It is therefore a most important duty for those among us who possess a commitment to honesty and courage in their hearts to think and act upon what we were enslaved to, what we have removed, and what further slavery must still be abolished.

Slavery is a composite affliction. Examining its causes reveals that it is made of many things.

Kandasami has a severe fever. Because of it, his arms and legs cramp, his vision clouds, and his chest hurts. The doctor gives him medicine for the fever.

Kandasami says, “Doctor, the fever has gone, but the cramping in my limbs and the pain in my chest have not. A strange new fatigue has also appeared.”

A good doctor will reply, “Yes, Kandasami. The powder I gave you for four days was only to remove the fever. The medicine I give next will remove the cramping, chest pain, and fatigue, purify the blood, and produce new strength.” A sensible patient will act accordingly.

Fever does not suddenly enter the body like a thorn piercing it. It cannot be removed alone as one plucks out a weed. Fever is a composite affliction. Slavery is the same. Foreign rule was like a severe fever, and it has now gone.

Our enslavement to fate began many centuries before enslavement to a foreigner. It is our hereditary disease, our ancestral property, an ancient illness born in the hermitage and spread equally through palace and hut. It seized and tormented us even in the days of glittering empires, fragrant and graceful arts, epics, painting, heroism, and abundant wealth.

But like washing a wound with rosewater and applying civet perfume so that its odour will not escape, we tried to lessen and conceal the wound’s stench under many coatings called imperial glory and the world of art. We did not try to cure it.

It is wrong to defend what is called fate, karma, deed, or writing upon the head merely as an ancient doctrine. Great people of former times, eminent ascetics, and learned authorities on the Vedas believed in fate. Through philosophy, analogy, story, and epic, they described the power of fate and the supposed truth that man’s small intellect could not change it.

Some people may wish to ask, though not many: “Fool! Are their statements false? Can you destroy the philosophy they discovered in those days? Is it proper to doubt it? Have you the courage to say it must be abolished?” Even those few will not ask directly.

No one can argue that merely because the philosophy of fate has existed since ancient times, it cannot or should not be removed. We see fruit ripen and decay, then worms writhe within it. Can we say, “It is a sweet mango from our own garden; what writhes within cannot be a worm, for fruit contains sweetness, not worms”?

Just as fruit rots, a flower withers and loses its fragrance before developing a foul smell, leaves become dry litter, and buildings decay into rubbish heaps, many philosophies and arrangements naturally deteriorate after lying imprisoned by time and produce many harms. Refusing to recognise this is the conduct of those who do not understand the world.

Fate is a disease long present in human society. Compared with the scale and skill of efforts made in other countries to weaken its grip, we have worked with even greater scale and skill not to reduce but to strengthen the disease. That is why enslavement to fate exists here to such a deeply troubling degree.

Children pass through an age when they cannot identify the causes of events before their eyes. Yet although they lack the ability to discover causes, they do not cease thinking.

A mother mixes affection with food, points to the moon, and feeds her child. The child looks at the moon, thinks various thoughts, babbles, and asks questions.

“Mother, who lit such a beautiful lamp so high above? Why are there so many tiny lamps around that one lamp?”

Another child asks, “Mother, catch that moon and give it to me. I want to play ball with it.” Looking at the moon and then at its mother’s radiant face, it wonders what difference exists between them.

The thoughts surging in a child’s mind are amusing but are not conclusions of inquiry; they are waves of desire. During human society’s childhood, it similarly wandered in search of some cause for each event and natural sight, invented many strange causes and explanations, spoke them, and came to believe them.

Many “truths” obtained during humanity’s infancy are no longer accepted as truths by the world. When Japan occupied Burma, it issued notes that are no longer valid there. Little harm occurs if children collect those invalid notes and play with them. But how much trouble would arise if someone insisted that those notes must still be accepted?

Likewise, consider the harm in insisting that we must continue believing and obeying the thoughts, arrangements, philosophies, and explanations formed during human society’s childhood.

When events occur contrary to expectation, when an undertaking begun in confidence fails despite good effort, when calculations prove false, seed does not sprout, friendship turns into hostility, a pot of paint breaks while a picture is being made, or a veena string snaps and strikes the eye as it is played, the mind becomes confused. When people fear their hearts may break under such bewildering events, they need consolation of some kind.

At that moment, the philosophy called fate enters their minds with a victorious smile. Once it settles there, fate becomes the master, and the person who gave the idea room becomes its slave. Fate commands the slave. Later, a divine coating was applied to the philosophy, which is why many lack the courage to throw it away.

It was said that some great ledger exists in the upper world, in which the life record of every earthly person was written in advance and that everything occurs accordingly. This belief was strengthened by many means.

Whether or not those who did it knew the magnitude of their teaching, they crippled the human mind and blinded thought. If they acted knowingly, they were great traitors; if unknowingly, fools. Whether deceivers or dullards, the stories they constructed have ruined the people of this country generation after generation. They shattered self-confidence, defeated effort, and destroyed progress.

There stands a beautiful young woman of eighteen, crying “Alas!” because the old man who tied the marriage pendant around her neck has died and she grieves that her life is withering.

“Fate, child, fate,” many say, pouring oil into her mind, already a burning cauldron.

An intelligent young woman dares to ask, “Was it just to give me as wife to this old man already walking toward death?”

The reply comes immediately: “That is what was written for you, child. That is your path and your fate.”

A worker cries, “I labour until my bones break, yet I suffer in poverty.” The immediate answer is, “That is all you were destined to receive.”

We set out for the marketplace with money in hand. Before we arrive, the goods there may change, prices may differ, and even the money we carry may become invalid, diminish, or disappear. If we cannot know which event will occur when, and can prevent none of them, what becomes of the market-goer’s thought and action? For the majority of this country’s people, the market of life has been made to appear like this.

When effort bears no result, a plan collapses, or an objective fails, the philosophy of fate does not allow a person to investigate why the effort failed, why the plan did not succeed, why the calculation proved false, or what the cause was; nor does it permit new effort or correction.

It makes him despair: “How hard we worked, yet in the end it failed. Such is our fate.” His ability begins to sleep. Grief and yearning arise. Then stories from puranas and epics are read to him.

A desire arose to discover whether fate might somehow be conquered, known in advance, changed, or corrected. Priests, astrologers, and magicians exploited that desire to earn their living. Later, unwilling to lose the comfort obtained from that livelihood, they constructed more and more fantasies and stories so that people would believe in fate. They swore by God and said anything; the poor man became a dupe.

They said: “O people who boast that fate can be conquered through intellect! Why did the four-faced Brahma lose one head? Because of fate. Though he gave the four Vedas, could even he prevent fate? Could even Siva, who cut off the head, prevent it? He went begging with the skull in his hand.”

Ordinary people became like a man who slips on treacherous ground, breaks his leg, begins walking upon the same ground again, and falls repeatedly.

But a very small number in the world, great thinkers, reformers, and noble people who sought to correct the world, refused to let thought be imprisoned. They began to think, discovered and announced entirely new truths, and the world’s form, nature, ideas, and arrangements began to change.

The flat world became round. The claim that seven worlds existed above and seven below was understood to be mere fiction. It became clear that the sun god, moon god, Indra, Vayu, Varuna, and Agni dwelling in heaven, singing and dancing joyfully while celestial women such as Rambha and Urvasi surrounded them, were delightful imaginings.

Countries such as America, once invisible, were discovered, and instruments once beyond thought were created. The atlas took its victorious place beside the almanac.

In many countries, the old writing was erased and rewritten: “Through the light of knowledge spread by the world’s thinkers, the fog of antiquity began to lift. Heroes arose to transform countries, courageous people to reform society, and people capable of conducting an intellectual revolution.” This was accomplished not merely by writing but by people of ability.

How then could the question fail to arise here: when will slavery to fate end?

The condition of enslavement to fate will certainly disappear sooner than we expect. It has already been shaken and set in motion.

If only this country’s writers, speakers, musicians, and filmmakers refrain from betraying knowledge; cease feeding the pain of repeated enslavement to fate; stop spreading the imaginary stories and ideas that support it; and use their intelligence and skill for ten years to explain the need to abandon belief in fate, the benefit the country would gain by refusing such slavery, how the poor become dupes through belief in fate, and how deceivers exploit them, then we can state with certainty that slavery to fate will be abolished.

Fate possesses no weapons such as submarines, torpedoes, aircraft, poison gas, or bombs. Having freed ourselves from the grip of an empire armed with such weapons, it is not impossible in this age to break the old slavery to fate, which rests only upon ordinary books, time-eaten pages, confusing fantasies found within them, and deceivers who live by those fantasies.

With one powerful assault, a united force of people engaged in intellectual work, a moral struggle, and a selfless effort, we can in our own time overthrow this chain of slavery that mixes poison into people’s lives and allow a new life to flower. Shall we do it?

## Translator's Notes

The source is a complete eight-page published text dated to 1948. The opening year is OCRed as “[948,” but the intended year is clear from the heading format. All Tamil OCR pages remain verbatim.

“விதி” is translated as fate in the sense of predetermined destiny. The associated OCR sequence “விதி, காமம், வினை, தலையெழுத்து” likely includes “கர்மம்” or karma, but English does not silently rewrite the Tamil list.

The fever is called a “கூட்டுச்சரக்கு,” literally a composite bundle or compound thing. “Composite affliction” preserves the analogy that removing foreign rule treats only one part of slavery.

The widowhood passage includes the *tali*, the marriage pendant. The term is explained through its action in the translation rather than replaced with a Western marriage object.

The invalid Burmese banknotes, almanac and atlas, and mythological examples are translated as period illustrations. Scientific, religious, social, medical, political, and historical assertions are translated in source voice and have not been independently verified.

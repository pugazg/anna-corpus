## English Translation

After Navalar was elected General Secretary, Anna invited him to assume the chair and delivered this address at the Second State Conference, held from 17 to 20 May 1956.

“Come, younger brother! Come and assume the leadership! Come; all of us will submit to your command and conduct ourselves accordingly!” said Anna.

When Anna congratulated his younger brother Nedunchezhiyan, cries of “Long live Navalar! Long live the guardian! Long live Anna! Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians!” made the heavens resound.

Food enough to fill the stomach, clothing to wear, a place to live, a means of livelihood, rest and opportunities for recreation are the fundamental needs of a country’s people. To gather and provide those basic necessities, a system of government—good government, an independent government—is indispensable.

Countless countries throughout the world have understood and agreed that their well-being lies in establishing such good government, an independent state of their own.

We have assembled here to devise plans and put them properly into action, so that we may overcome the opposition facing us and continue farther along the path of our ideal.

Must not warriors standing on a battlefield encounter opposition?

With that courage of heart, with unquenchable enthusiasm and enduring attachment, we have assembled here to rescue the country from servitude and establish an independent state that will bring well-being to the people of our homeland.

Our Kazhagam was born amid tears. Periyar had laboured for certain principles. He had insisted upon principles such as the wrongness of adopting an heir. He had travelled throughout the country propagating them, and had written and argued for them in *Kudi Arasu* from 1933 onward. When he acted contrary to those very principles, we pleaded with him to safeguard them; we begged with tears filling our eyes.

At a time when no other degree-holding scholar dared expound those principles—principles that would break the people’s superstitions and blind customs and lead them toward rational thought—we joined Periyar because he propagated them courageously. It was to save, protect and carry forward those principles that we separated from him and took form as the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

Today we have risen and grown to such a height that hundreds of thousands of people surge here like a flood.

In the eyes of all of you gathered here, I see light; I see your inner feeling; brightness upon your faces; and awakening in your bearing, dress and movement.

The person who gave us light, feeling, enthusiasm, radiance and awakening—the original cause of all this great growth—is our Anna. Anna is the taproot of our Kazhagam; all of us are its fibrous and lateral roots. Because we have Anna to guide, advise and counsel us, we need not fear any hostility or opposition. That is why I accepted the responsibility of General Secretary.

The broad Kaveri flows into a great canal, then into smaller channels and finally across the fields. In the same way, after this conference ends, I ask you to carry the ideas you receive here, the speeches from which you benefit and the plans placed before you into cities, towns, villages and every remote hamlet. Explain them to the countless people who could not attend and enable them all to gain understanding and awakening.

We who work with stout hearts and honest ability, bearing the principles of the Kazhagam, need not worry about those who arouse opposition, insult us or hurl accusations.

Cast aside the opposition, abuse and slander directed at us as mere rubbish. Let it become good manure for our growing Kazhagam.

Our organisation has never possessed the facilities ordinarily needed for a political party to grow. We have grown to this extent with very meagre resources.

We have no press strength and no publicity strength. But we have in abundance young people strengthened by principle, youths devoted to the ideal and warriors attached to the homeland. It is through that strength that the Kazhagam continues to grow.

We are fully confident that our ideas, principles and ideals cannot fail to triumph if they enter every mind they have not yet reached, every ear they have not yet entered, every heart they have not yet penetrated, and touch every artery of feeling they have not yet touched. That is why we are engaged in the great task of assembling an army of knowledge and an army of strength.

This State Conference is the turning point for directing those assembled forces along the paths toward the liberation of the country.

We know well how great the responsibility of liberating the homeland is. We also understand that our journey is very long.

All our struggles and agitations both address the people’s daily problems and draw us toward our final ideal. The three-front agitation we conducted and the agitation of 20 February, in which other parties joined us, are struggles capable of drawing us toward that ideal.

Our ideal is that the people of the South, separated administratively by language but united by ethnicity, should establish a Dravidian socialist democratic federal republic. We struggle against and condemn the imperial power and actions of Delhi because the Central Government is so structured as to prevent this ideal from being realised. All our struggles and agitations carry us toward our ideal.

Under the leadership of Kalaignar Karunanidhi, we conducted an agitation demanding that the name Dalmiyapuram be changed to Kallakudi.

Some ask, “Are not names such as Rameswaram, Lalgudi and Srirangam also derived from northern languages? Why have you not demanded that they be changed?”

Why do we demand that the name Dalmiyapuram be changed?

Dalmia, a capitalist from the North, comes to the South and plunders and exploits it. With the favour of the Indian Government, he exercises domination. We demand the removal of the town name bearing his name to demonstrate our opposition to that domination. This name-change agitation serves as an example of our ideal.

Does the demand to change Dalmiyapuram to Kallakudi not demonstrate that we oppose northern linguistic, commercial, economic and political imperialism?

Many laws existed in the country, laws enacted by the British. But Gandhi did not attempt to defy and break every one of them. Did he not choose specifically to break the law against making salt?

Our Dalmiyapuram name-change agitation is of the same kind.

If you ask whether that agitation has succeeded, we say that it will succeed. If not today, then tomorrow; if not tomorrow, then the day after. We are certain of victory.

Does every struggle begun by a political party anywhere in the world succeed within ten days? Can anyone say that every struggle will win immediately?

Gandhi said the tricolour would fly over the Red Fort. How many days passed before that became possible?

How many years passed before Karl Marx’s dream bore fruit? How many years elapsed before Lenin’s efforts yielded victory? In the same manner, if not today then tomorrow, we shall certainly change the name Dalmiyapuram. We shall continue our efforts toward that end.

We instilled in the people the conviction that the northern symbol of domination called Dalmiyapuram must be removed. That feeling still burns with a rising flame. We shall continue working so that it grows and burns still brighter.

We opposed Acharya’s hereditary education scheme and conducted an agitation. We were beaten with lathis, struck by tear-gas shells and made to suffer in prison. At the same time, we won the struggle. Our victory did not merely abolish Acharya’s education scheme; it was great enough to drive Acharya himself from office.

At this moment, we offer our heroic salute to the great warriors who sacrificed their lives in the three-front agitation and to the fighters who endured many hardships.

That three-front agitation gives us sound hope for the future. It is an example of our achievement, strength and courage.

The achievements we have made despite our lack of resources are immense. They fill us with pride and satisfaction.

When handloom workers had lost their livelihoods and suffered, we helped them. Anna and every other Kazhagam speaker carried handloom cloth on their shoulders, went street by street and sold it for them. We supported handloom not as a matter of permanent principle, but because of the existing circumstances.

Our support for handloom did not arise from hatred of machinery. On the contrary, we support the development of machinery.

Machines reduce human labour and increase convenience. Science has discovered that all the goods human beings require can be produced by machinery. We care deeply about that scientific development.

Thirty lakhs of people now depend upon handloom for their livelihood. They are in no position to do any other work. Because they cannot now learn another occupation and survive through it, we temporarily support the handloom upon which they depend.

Handloom weavers have already ceased spinning their own yarn; they now obtain the yarn needed for weaving from the mills. Although the present generation of weavers cannot leave handloom and enter another occupation, succeeding generations must gradually leave it and enter many other forms of work.

At a time when mechanisation is advancing rapidly throughout the world, we cannot expect handloom to remain established forever.

The advancement of human life lies in reducing human labour and increasing leisure. Machinery, not cottage industry, can and does assist this progress.

While the world races toward mechanisation and concentrates upon producing ever newer machines, Acharya introduced a scheme requiring students to learn cottage occupations, supposedly in order to develop them.

We believe that goods must be produced scientifically; that is why we opposed Acharya’s scheme. It is wrong to say, as some imagine, that we opposed him because Acharya was a Brahmin. The same Rajagopalachari introduced the Thanjavur Tenants and Pannaiyal Protection legislation, and we passed a resolution praising him for it. When he introduced a resolution reserving certain varieties of cloth exclusively for handloom production, we praised him then too. But when he introduced hereditary education and tried to blind the educational eye of young children, we opposed him.

When Acharya introduced that scheme, eighty lakhs of people in this State were of school-going age. Of them, forty lakhs entered Classes One through Five, but thirty-five lakhs discontinued their education within those classes. Only five lakhs continued beyond Class Six. Apart from those five lakhs, the remaining seventy-five lakhs were already engaged in occupations such as mat weaving, basket making, pottery, weaving reed and palm-leaf mats, and brickmaking, the very occupations named in Acharya’s scheme. Acharya wanted even the five lakhs studying beyond Class Six to engage in these cottage occupations. Could that suit the country? Could it assist the country’s economic growth?

When even the domestic market for this country’s cottage-industry products is insufficient, where will a foreign market be found? That is why we demand scientific production.

Cottage-industry development cannot take the country to the forefront; it offers no path of progress.

Thus it is clear that we did not support handloom as a matter of permanent principle. We supported it because of existing conditions and in order to protect this generation of weavers.

When conference chairman Navalar announced the resolutions, the applause of the people made the heavens resound.

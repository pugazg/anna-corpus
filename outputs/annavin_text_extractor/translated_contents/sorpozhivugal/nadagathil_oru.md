## English Translation

Speech delivered by Arignar Anna on 16 October 1965 while presiding over the T. K. S. Brothers' staging of *A Father's Wish* at the Rasika Ranjani Sabha auditorium, Madras.

To everyone in Tamil Nadu who appreciates the flavour of drama, seeing the plays presented by my friend, Muthamizh Kala Vidva Rathna T. K. Shanmugam, and his troupe is a rare and valuable opportunity. Although I have now had the chance to see a T. K. S. troupe production again after a long interval, I am delighted to say with pride that I would rank foremost among those who saw a great many of their plays in earlier days.

## Then and Now

Every play staged by the T. K. S. troupe in Tamil Nadu has been designed to bring some innovation and to meet a need shared by the theatrical world and society. They have staged plays depicting the history of Tamil Nadu and plays introducing the great Tamil poets…

[A passage is missing between the first and second scanned images.]

Likewise, they have introduced and demonstrated an innovation in this play. Taking as their basis the question of how children should be brought up in different surroundings so that good ideas may spring from their hearts, they have included children in the production. This is an innovation for both the stage and the arts.

When I say they have involved children in this play, I do not mean that children never acted in plays before. Both T. K. S. brothers began acting in childhood. But in those days they acted only in plays where little children performed whatever adults would do. At an age when they knew nothing of the play of love, boys would enact romance, marriage and everything else. This production breaks from that dramatic style. The children remain children and portray problems arising among children themselves. That is not merely innovative; it also teaches us what kind of environment exists within families.

I saw another innovation in this play. Fifteen years ago, it was usual for my friend Bhagavathi to play the somewhat roguish roles in the T. K. S. troupe, while my friend Shanmugam played the compliant characters. In this play, my friend T. K. Shanmugam appeared in such a rough role that even Bhagavathi might be frightened by him. This was not only an example of his acting ability; it made us wonder how a man so exceptionally gentle by nature could act with such brutality. Yet, when he revealed the love for his child hidden beneath that harshness, all of us shed tears with him. Though the character lived in a manner that could intimidate the whole town, Shanmugam acted superbly when his heart melted because he could not bear the misfortunes that befell his own family.

The police officer played by my friend Bhagavathi was not merely an example of the art of acting; it also reminded us that police officers might be like this. He portrayed a man with fine literary taste, one who speaks in support of a friend and wants the child he raises to be like his own. This did more than serve dramatic art. It pictured a future in which all of us expect members of the police service to conduct themselves in that manner.

The children and youngsters acted in a way that must truly have captivated every parent and everyone living with a family. As the children performed roles filled with many shades of feeling, there can be no doubt that the adults in the audience repeatedly thought of the natures of the children in their own homes and enjoyed the play as though its events were taking place in another family.

## A Delightful Lesson

My friends Shanmugam and Bhagavathi are no longer the sturdy young bulls they once were. They are elders now, managing households with their children. I commend the excellent qualities they have shown through a play involving these children. I am delighted to see that the children too possess wonderful acting ability in good measure.

I am one of those who have worried that dramatic art is declining day by day. Although professional theatre may be declining because it cannot operate permanently as a livelihood, the acting of these boys and the girl makes it perfectly clear that acting talent itself can never perish, that people will not allow it to perish, and that it will continue to grow each day.

This drama is founded on the idea that society will advance if every member of every family properly carries out his or her duty. It is not merely a play; it is a delightful lesson offered to the people of the country.

I knew long ago the purpose with which the T. K. S. brothers stage plays and the good ideas they want drama to spread across the country. I therefore offer my appreciation for their excellent service to theatre.

## A Good Child, a Good Father

Today I also saw on stage some of the principal actors whose work I enjoyed in earlier days. I saw my friend Sivadhanu in a policeman's role fifteen years ago. At that time I thought that only fifteen years ago could he delight audiences in a role of this kind. I am pleased to see that even now, the moment he appears, everyone eagerly recognises, “Sivadhanu is coming; Sivadhanu is acting.” I offer my appreciation to him too, and to every other actor and actress who took part in the play.

I had no time to inquire which child had played which role. Nor would it have been proper to ask beforehand. Had I learned their identities in advance, I might even have been deluded into wondering whether that was why their acting seemed good. Only when the play ended did I ask who had performed these roles. What I then learned showed that the Tamil proverb “A good child comes from a good father” had not gone in vain: the children who took part displayed fine acting ability.

Those familiar with the dramatic world know well that anyone who joins T. K. Shanmugam's troupe naturally acquires good acting ability. I convey my best wishes to those boys and to the girl.

## A Fine Play

Finally, I greatly commend the construction of the story. Tiruchi Bharathan, who wrote the play, has shown fine ability in composing it in an innovative manner. It is easy for anyone to include children as characters in a play. But deciding what they should say is possible only when a writer restrains, to some degree, the intoxication that can seize a writer. Writers are tempted to make someone or other voice everything that arises in their imagination.

My friend Bharathan restrained that temptation and wrote the play in the manner in which children themselves would speak: what they would say, how they would say it, and in what fashion. I offer him my appreciation too.

I am immensely happy to have had, after a long time, the opportunity to see a fine production by my friend Shanmugam's troupe. I thank you all and take my leave. Greetings.

## English Translation

## Tamil Festival Public Meeting, 14 January 1958

Speech delivered by C. N. Annadurai at a DMK Tamil Festival meeting presided over by General Secretary Navalar on 14 January 1958.

I read that Minister Bhaktavatsalam told the Lakshmipuram Youth Association, "We permitted them to show black flags." If that statement is true, I ask through this gathering: when did you grant permission, and to whom did you communicate it?

Without cause, the Government prohibited the meeting planned for the third. Permission was formally requested and refused.

Although no permission was needed merely to display a black flag, we departed from custom and sent the Nagercoil resolution in English to the Chief Minister, Police Minister and Commissioner of Police. Since Meenambakkam, where the demonstration would occur, lay in Chengalpattu district, we sent it to that district's police authority too. Where was the reply?

When was permission granted? Was it sent in writing, conveyed by telephone, or communicated in a meeting with us?

Three people spoke to me, but none wore the Government's seal or spoke officially. All three said the same thing: "Do not show the black flag on the sixth; do it on the seventh. Why must everyone demonstrate? You alone could show one." They spoke as individuals who cared about me, not authorised representatives. I could not accept their advice.

Why did the Minister not answer my written notice responsibly? Had he called me and argued clearly that the protest should be confined to Meenambakkam, I might, with the General Secretary's approval, have agreed. I am not obstinate. This was not the last possible occasion for a black-flag protest against Pandit Nehru.

Those who approached me served only as postmen, empowered to repeat what was said elsewhere but unable to offer proposals of their own or accept mine. Bhaktavatsalam's claim that permission was granted is therefore a filtered falsehood. One wonders how many such people sit in the ministry.

Why prohibit a meeting meant to explain the demonstration? If the Government feared that people would obey Annadurai's instructions and make the black-flag event successful, why not hold its own meeting on the fourth and ask them not to demonstrate? If people obey only my word, ought that not shame you?

Since a case concerns the events of the third, I do not wish to explain them further here. I repeat: from whom did I seek permission, and when did you say it had been granted?

You know me and the organisation to which I belong. You know that we entered the Assembly with twelve lakh votes. The country knows we have the possibility of sitting in the ministry next. If we wished, the DMK could govern Madras State by speaking warmly with the Communists, reaching terms with the Socialists and shaking hands with Congress reformers. Because we treat such calculations as trivial, I wrote respectfully to the Minister for permission.

Did he summon me or reply in writing? No. Some people came privately. Bhaktavatsalam may be angry that Annadurai brings matters from the room into the open. I do not expose things without cause. I speak publicly today only because the Minister's course forces everything into the open.

P. Balasubramaniam, editor of the *Sunday Observer*, telephoned me. He did not speak as an authorised emissary but as an elder brother. He offered the remarkable suggestion that I alone show a black flag.

The Nagercoil General Council resolution required all fifteen DMK MLAs and two MPs to lead the demonstration. How could I act alone? Nehru's insult was directed at Achariyar and other elder scholars, not at me or our movement. No one among us fits that description, and Nehru could not call us so. We had no personal hostility toward him. We resolved upon a dignified procession led by elected representatives so that, if police struck, the first blow would fall upon us.

Balasubramaniam then suggested the seventh. I pitied the advice. The English words "go back" mean "return." If Nehru arrived on the sixth, completed his engagements, and was already departing on the seventh, what meaning would remain in shouting "Nehru, go back"? Except those who reject English altogether, anyone who understands it would not choose the seventh. I therefore insisted upon the sixth.

If a black flag might disturb Nehru's pleasure, spend the remaining forty-eight hours praising him, playing music, presenting dance recitals, and send him away happy and peaceful.

V. V. Ramasami next visited as one concerned for us and repeated Balasubramaniam's advice. Then a senior police officer came and said the same. Why borrow unofficial intermediaries rather than send someone authorised to negotiate?

With public support I earned the right to sit opposite you in the Assembly. I have protected your honour when it might otherwise have scattered in the wind. Would your eyes be dimmed or your office damaged by summoning me for a discussion? I would enter the Assembly in February in any event. A responsible Minister did not call me, though I have a duty to correct him. We were therefore left no course but the black flag.

We have witnessed many acts of repression and will not lose heart. What pains me is the rulers' accusation that buses were burned and stones thrown. Hearing that ten of our people died would not alone break my heart; I call you to accept sacrifice. Tamil warriors shed blood upon the Ganges, in Kadaram and in the history celebrated by the *Kalingattuparani* and Rajendra Chola. We have not forgotten that heritage.

But I call upon you to shed blood, not to strike back. Our members should have condemned and physically handed over anyone who retaliated violently. That is what I would have said had I been permitted to speak on the third. I would have asked every member to control himself and become, in effect, a guardian of order.

On the third, police stopped my car and arrested me and my companions. They arrested Karunanidhi and the others following in another car, even Karunanidhi's driver, Rangan. People say that even the loom in Kamban's home would compose poetry; similarly, the Government apparently regarded even the driver of Karunanidhi's car as a frightening enemy.

Why such fear? Rather than die repeatedly of fright, could they not find a single rope? That night eleven of us were locked in two cells, each roughly ten by eight feet, at the Adyar police station. Six were confined in a room where even two could not lie stretched out comfortably. Before locking us in, they removed our upper garments. With no cloth to spread upon the rough floor or cover themselves, comrades slept leaning against the wall. I did not sleep.

MPs and MLAs answerable to seventeen lakh voters spent the night half-clothed in that cell. The next day we were taken to Saidapet Sub-Jail, where there was no class distinction. An earthen urine pot stood beside our heads, next to the vessel containing drinking water. We drank from a tin cup.

We do not care greatly about the humiliation inflicted upon us. Outside, I saw comrades dripping blood, their bodies and clothes soaked until one could smell it when approaching. Then I knew I had not endured the worst cruelty.

If another agitation occurs, put us in front. Stand us without upper garments and strike us; let blood flow so the country and world can see. Such is the Government's hardening of heart.

I will disclose something private. At midnight on the third, a senior police officer came to our cell. Moved by pity and with tears in his eyes, he held the bars and said, "These hardships are temporary; they will not last. Please endure them."

I was distressed to hear that disorder had occurred that day. I told him: "Some troublemakers apparently rioted. Had our meeting been permitted, I would have had DMK members catch and hand them over. Had I known such disorder might occur, I might have agreed to confine the sixth's demonstration to one place or even pleaded with our comrades, with the General Secretary's approval, not to demonstrate. Please bring Navalar to meet me so I may discuss it."

The officer agreed to try and said he would return only if successful. What could the poor man do? Only the first part came true: before I could meet Navalar freely, the authorities arrested him and brought him to where I was confined.

At every stage we extended a friendly hand; the rulers placed a hand upon our throats and answered only with repression.

The Minister's words will not drive the DMK into violence. In the nine years since its founding, the DMK has not placed even a particle of faith in violence.

*Viduthalai* wrote in effect that nothing can be achieved by the *ottu murai*, the ballot method, and that success requires the *vettu murai*, the method of explosives or violent force. I state emphatically that the ballot method is necessary and violent methods cannot achieve our purpose.

Those who imagine repression can cage us and take away our principles will certainly be disappointed. Police officers treated us without dignity. We know to whom officials show respect and when, and we know how those same officials treated today's rulers in earlier days.

We must insist upon a public judicial inquiry into police repression. I shall say "judicial inquiry," and you must answer "we demand it." The assembled people responded with one voice. I shall then say, "the violence of the sixth, including burning buses and throwing stones," and you must answer "we condemn it." Again the gathering did so.

We must not retreat from duty. Some say Annadurai knows how to build a force but cannot keep it disciplined. I will show that I can. When I say "stand," all of you must stand; when I say "sit," you must sit. The entire gathering obeyed.

Let the Government understand that my pointing finger accomplished what batons, tear gas and guns could not. This gathered strength is not for violence or empty pride; it brings us great responsibility.

Fire in the kitchen stove causes no loss; fire upon the roof destroys house and town. Whatever blame is placed upon it, the DMK will not lose discipline. The difference between a khaki-clad policeman and a soldier upon a battlefield is discipline.

You may wear no khaki, but Tamil people are engaged in a great struggle. Violence has no place in a nonviolent moral campaign. Striking back when struck is uncivilised and contrary to our politics. Everyone must understand that.

Sheikh Abdullah, Nehru's close friend and a world-famous figure, was imprisoned in Kashmir for roughly four and a half years. Upon release, he said his homeland should possess autonomy and determine its own rights. He was swayed neither by Nehru's frown nor smile.

If Sheikh Abdullah spoke so after four and a half years of confinement, the rulers must understand how mistaken it is to think four days in jail will make us abandon our principles.

## Related Assembly Speech on the Governor's Address

What can you show under your rule? Growth through planning? Dams? Factories? Schools?

Whenever the House meets, do you show anything but heaps of bodies? Then Mudukulathur; today Madras.

How many baton charges and shootings have occurred under your rule? How much repression? Your hands bear bloodstains.

You conduct police rule, not democratic government. To remove that stain, I strongly demand a judicial inquiry.

The Minister accused black-flag demonstrations of provoking violence. I ask: in which black-flag protest did violence appear? Can he show one? We have never incited violence and do not believe in it.

## Translator's Notes

The source compiles a 14 January 1958 public speech with a short related Assembly excerpt whose printed date reads “13-2-38.” The excerpt follows references to the January 1958 Nehru black-flag events, but the year is not silently corrected without direct bibliographic confirmation.

“ஒட்டு முறை / வேட்டு முறை” is a Tamil sound-play contrasting the ballot or vote with explosive or violent methods. The rhyme cannot be reproduced exactly in English; both meanings are stated explicitly.

The source forcefully rejects retaliatory violence, condemns bus-burning and stone-throwing, and frames bodily sacrifice within a nonviolent “அறப்போர்,” translated as a nonviolent moral campaign.

The OCR prints Sheikh Abdullah's confinement as “4% ஆண்டு,” interpreted contextually as approximately four and a half years but requiring source verification.

Descriptions of confinement and police violence are translated as historical testimony.

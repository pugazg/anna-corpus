20-53-1956 அன்று 2அவது மாநில மாநாட்டில்
அண்ணா அவர்கள் ஆற்றிய சிறப்புரை:-

சிறப்புரையின் சுருக்கம்

“இந்த மாநில மாட்டில் கூடியிருக்கிற நீங்கள், 4000 அடி.
உயரத்திலே இருக்கிற வால்பாறையிலே இருந்தும், நெடுந்
தொலைவிலே இருக்கிற கதூத்துக்குடி.த் துறைமுகப் பட்.டினத்திலே
இருந்தும், தலைநகரம் என்று கருதப்படும் சென்னை நகரத்திலே
இருந்தும் இவை எல்லாவற்றிலும் சிறப்பாகக் குறிப்பிடப்பட
வேண்டிய சிற்றூர்களிலேயிருந்தும் இலட்சக்கணக்கில் இங்கே
திரண்டிருக்கிறீர்கள். நான், நாட்டைப் பார்த்தும், நாட்டை
ஆளுகிற ஆட்சியாளர்களைப் பார்த்தும் கொஞ்சம்
ஆணவத்தோடு கூடச் சொல்வேன். 'சென்னையில் தாங்கள்
கூட்டிய மாநில மாநாட்டில் ஒரு இலட்சம் மக்கள் கூடினார்கள்,
நாங்கள் இப்போது அடைதந்திருக்கிற வளர்ச்சியை நீங்கள்
காணவேண்டுமானால், திருச்சியில் நடைபெறவிருக்கிற மாநில
மாநாட்டில் மூன்று இலட்சம் மக்களை நாங்கள் கூட்டிக்காட்டு
கிறோம் - என்று நான் கூறினேன். சொல்லிவிட்ட பிறகு, எனக்கே
கூடக் கொஞ்சம் சந்தேகம் வந்தது சற்று அதிகமாகக்
கூறிவிட்டோமோ?' - என்று! இன்று நாம் பார்க்கிற இடங்களில்
எல்லாம் தி. மு. ௧. தோழர்களைத்தான் பார்க்கிறோம். “இந்த
மாநில மாநாட்டுப் பெருந்திடலில் மட்டுமல்ல, திடலில் இருத்து
திருச்சி கோடிவரை ஆறு; ஏழு மைல் தொலைவிலுள்ள
இடங்களில் எல்லாம் - நெடுஞ்சாலைகளில் எல்லாம் நம்முடைய
தோழர்கள் நிரம்பியிருப்பதை நாம் காண்கிறோம்.

உதாரணத்திற்குச் சொல்ல வேண்டுமானால், ஏதாவது ஒரு
பொருளை இங்கிருந்து ஸ்ரீரங்கத்திற்கு அனுப்ப வேண்டுமானால்
அந்தச் சாமானைப் பக்கத்திலே இருக்கிற தோழரிடத்திலும்,
அவர், அவர் பக்கத்திலிருக்கிற தோழரிடத்திலும் கொடுத்தால்,
சாமான் தானாக ஸ்ரீரங்கம் போய்ச் சேர்ந்துவிடும்; அந்த அளவுக்குத்
திருச்சி நெடுகிலும் நம் தோழர்கள் நிறைந்திருக்கிறார்கள்.

மிகப் பெருங்கூட்டத்தை நாம் இந்த மாநில மாநாட்டில்
காணுகிறோமென்ற கழிபேருவகையால் மட்டும் நான்
இங்கேபேசிக்கொண்டிருக்கவில்லை; வந்திருக்கிற தோழர்கள்
அத்தனைபேரும் நல்லதோர் குறிக்கோளுடன் இங்கே வந்திருக்
கிறார்கள்; 'கழகம் என்ன கட்டளை பிறப்பிக்கக் காத்திருக்கிறது
என்பதை அறிந்து செல்வதற்காக வந்திருக்கிறார்கள்; ஆற்றல்
மிக்கவர்கள் வந்திருக்கிறார்கள்; அஞ்சா நெஞ்சர்கள் வந்திருக்
கிறார்கள்; சிறைச்சாலைக்கும் - குடும்பத்திற்கும் வித்தியாசம்
தெரியாதவர்கள் வந்திருக்கிறார்கள். இவர்களை எல்லாம் பார்த்து
நாம் பெருமையடைகின்றோம் என்றபோதிலும், நாட்டை ஆளும்
பொறுப்பை ஏற்றுக் கொண்டிருக்கும் கட்சிக்காரர்கள்,
நம்முடைய கழகத்தைப்பற்றி வைத்திருக்கிற தப்புக் கணக்கைத்
தவறான கணக்கு என்பதை உணர்ந்துகொள்வதற்கு இந்த மாநில
மாநாடாவது வழிகாட்டியாக இருக்கவேண்டும் என்று நான்
உண்மையிலேயே விரும்புகின்றேன்.

உங்கள் அனைவருக்கும் நன்றாகத் தெரியும் - தி. மு. ௧.
விலேயே கடைசி பலவீனன் நான்; அதுமட்டுமல்ல; நான் அதிகமாக
பயத்த சுபாவமுடையவன்; என்றாலும், இந்தப் பாம்புகளைக்
கண்டு நான் பயப்படாமலிருந்ததற்குக் காரணம் உங்களுடைய
மன்றத்தில், 'புலி வந்தாலும் பூனையாகிவிடும்' என்ற நம்பிக்கை
இருந்ததால்தான், இந்த நச்சுப் பூச்சிகள் நிறைந்த பெருவெளியை
நாங்கள் தேர்ந்தெடுத்தோம்.

இந்தப் பெருவெளியில் பாடுபடுவதற்குத் திருச்சியில் உள்ள
நமது கழகத் தோழர்களான அன்பில் தருமலிங்கம், பொன்மலை
பராங்குசம், இருச்சி மணி, நாகசுந்தரம் - இவர்கள் பெயரிட்டுக்
கூறப்பட வேண்டியவர்கள். மற்றும் என்னுடைய கவனத்திற்கு
இந்த நேரத்தில் வராதிருக்கின்ற எண்ணற்ற தோழர்கள், இங்கே
நாங்கள் வந்தபோது “இந்த வெட்ட. வெளியிலாவது - நாம்
கூடுவதாவது?2” என்ற சந்தேகத்தை நாங்கள் கொண்ட நேரத்தில்,
“இந்தச் சந்தேகத்தைத் துடைக்க முடியும்' என்று எனக்கு

வாக்குறுதி அளித்தார்கள். வாக்குறுதி அளித்ததோடுகூட, பட்ட
கஷ்டமும், எடுத்துக்கொண்ட முயற்சிகளும் மகத்தானவை.

உண்மையிலேயே நீங்கள் இப்போது தீர்மானித்தீர்கள் -
நாம் எல்லாம் பொதுத் தேர்தலில் ஈடுபடவேண்டும் - என்று!
நாம் எல்லாம் பொதுத் தேர்தலில் ஈடுபடுவதற்காக நீங்கள்
காட்டுகிற ஆர்வத்தைத் தேர்தல் காலத்தில் நீங்கள்
வளர்ச்சியடையச் செய்து, நாம் வெற்றிபெறுவதற்கான சூழ்நிலை
கிடைக்குமானால், இந்த மாநில மாநாட்டை வெற்றிகரமாக
நடத்திய நம்முடைய கழகத் தோழர்கள் நாட்டை அளவும் தகுதி
படைத்தவர்கள் என்பதை இந்த மாநில மாநாடு நிச்சயமாக
எடுத்துக்காட்டும்.

இங்கே உள்ள பந்தலுக்கான பொருள்கள் அத்தனையையும்
நாமே விலை கொடுத்து வாங்கி இரவு - பகல் என்று பாராமல்
இந்த இடத்திலேயே முகாமிட்டு, நம்முடைய பொதுச்
செயலாளரும், நானும் தோழர் சம்பத்தும், மதியழகனும், மற்றும்
சென்னையிலே இருந்தும், சுற்றுப்புறமிருந்தும் வந்திருந்த
தோழர்களும் உழைத்தோம். நம்முடைய மதுரைத் தோழர் முத்து
அவர்கள், இரவு - பகல் என்று பாராமல் உடனிருந்து, இந்த
மாநில மாநாட்டுப் பந்தலை இத்துணைச் சிறப்புடைய
தாக்கினார்கள்.

மகத்தான வெற்றியை இந்த மாநில மாநாட்டில் நாம்
பெற்றிருக்கின்றோம்; என்றாலும் மாற்றுக் கட்சிக்காரர்கள்
மமதையாலேயோ அல்லது, நாட்டு நிலைகளை அறிந்து
கொள்வதற்கு நேரமில்லாத காரணத்தாலேயோ தி. மு. கழகத்தைப்
பற்றி இன்னும் தப்புக் கணக்குப் போடுகின்றார்கள். அப்படித்
தப்புக்கணக்குப் போடும் கட்சியினர், “தி. மு. ௧. இன்று தோன்றி,
நாளை மறைகிற ஓர் எரி நட்சத்திரம்' என்று கருதினார்கள்;
அவர்கள் அத்தனைபேரும் பார்த்துக் கொண்டேயிருந்தார்கள்;
சதிவேலையில் ஈடுபட்டுக் கொண்டே நம்மை ஓழித்துக்
கட்டுவதற்குக் காத்துக்கொண்டே இருந்தார்கள்; நமக்குள்ளாகப்
பிளவு மூட்டலாமா என்று கனவு கண்டுகொண்டிருந்தார்கள்;
ஆனால் இன்று, இத்தனைக்கும் பிறகும் இந்த அளவுக்கு
வளர்ச்சியடைந்திருக்கின்றோம்.

தந்தை பெரியாரோடு நான் பதினைந்தாண்டுக்காலம்
பணியாற்றியிருக்கிறேன்; அவரோடு இருந்த நேரத்தில், ஒவ்வொரு

தேர்தலில் எங்களை எதிர்ப்பவர் யாருமில்லை என்று சொல்லிக்

## English Translation

Special address delivered by Anna at the Second State Conference on 20 May 1956. The following is the summarized address.

You who have assembled at this State Conference have come in hundreds of thousands: from Valparai at an elevation of four thousand feet, from the distant port city of Thoothukudi, from Chennai, regarded as the capital, and, most importantly, from countless small villages.

Looking at the country and its rulers, I once said with a little pride: “One lakh people attended the State Conference you held in Chennai. If you wish to see how far we have grown, we shall assemble three lakhs at our coming State Conference in Tiruchirappalli.” After saying it, even I wondered whether I had exaggerated. Today, everywhere we look we see DMK comrades. Not only is this vast conference ground full; our comrades fill every highway and every place for six or seven miles from here to the Tiruchirappalli Fort.

To give an example, if an object had to be sent from here to Srirangam, one could hand it to the comrade standing nearby, who could pass it to the next, and it would reach Srirangam by itself. Our comrades fill Tiruchirappalli to that extent.

I am not speaking merely from delight at seeing this enormous gathering. Every comrade has come with a good purpose: to learn what command the Kazhagam is preparing to issue. People of ability have come; fearless hearts have come; people who see no difference between prison and home have come. Though we are proud to see them, I sincerely wish that this conference will at least guide the ruling party to realise that its assessment of our Kazhagam is wrong.

You all know that I am the weakest person in the DMK and, moreover, naturally very timid. Yet I did not fear the snakes in this open field because I trusted that, in your assembly, even a tiger would become a cat. That is why we chose this expanse filled with venomous creatures.

Our Tiruchirappalli comrades Anbil Dharmalingam, Ponmalai Parangusam, Tiruchi Mani and Nagasundaram deserve to be named for their labour here. Countless others whose names do not occur to me at this moment promised to remove our doubt when, on arriving, we wondered whether we could ever assemble in this barren expanse. The hardship they endured and the effort they made after giving that promise were immense.

You have now truly resolved that we should enter the general election. If, during the election, you enlarge the enthusiasm you have shown for participation and create the conditions for victory, this conference will certainly demonstrate that the comrades capable of conducting it successfully are also qualified to govern the country.

We purchased every material for this pavilion and camped here, working day and night. Our General Secretary, Comrade Sampath, Mathiazhagan, I, comrades from Chennai and the surrounding areas, and our Madurai comrade Muthu worked without regard to night or day to make this conference pavilion so distinguished.

We have achieved a magnificent success here. Yet, from arrogance or lack of time to understand the country, members of other parties continue to miscalculate the DMK. They thought it a meteor that would appear today and vanish tomorrow. They watched, engaged in conspiracy, waited to destroy us and dreamed of creating division within us. Despite all that, we have grown to this extent.

I worked with Thanthai Periyar for fifteen years. At every meeting, with me and comrades who had laboured even more than I seated beside him, he would ask, “Who is there to carry out these tasks in accordance with my trust?” Perhaps that reflected our shortcomings; perhaps we lacked the qualities he expected. That is not why I mention it. When all of us were with him, Periyar asked who would continue his work after him. But in our time, I need not even ask who comes after me. I can proudly say that, while I am still watching, my younger brothers will attend to every task.

Our movement has gained a strength no other movement possesses because we have learned to discover and bring forth each person’s dimmed or hidden talent, unrecognised ability and fighting strength buried in the heart like gold sleeping beneath a mine. That is why, in this assembly and on this stage, we see so many heroes of action, warriors of thought and people with the power to fear nothing.

We have attained this enormous growth in seven or eight years, but the price was not small. The tears shed were not few, the blood spilt was not ordinary and the sacrifices offered were not slight. We endured countless lathi blows and acquired countless scars of sacrifice. Those with bodily strength can endure blows, but enduring abuse requires not physical strength alone but firmness of heart.

Countless people abused us, using Tamil words unworthy even of a dictionary. We bore all of it and advanced because of our faith in our goal, our certainty that we would bring it to successful completion and our great confidence that the people would grant us overwhelming support.

As for financial power, we may give outsiders the impression that we possess abundant money, but no one among us has the wealth needed to conduct a great movement.

Do we possess the strength of the press? This morning I saw *Swadesamitran*. A comrade described our great procession and asked, “How can I call it merely a procession? Was it not a flood of people overflowing its banks?” Yet its editor printed only a quarter-column report, perhaps in the fifth or sixth column of the sixth or seventh page. Half a dozen reporters sit here writing without rest. I do not blame them; I express sympathy. What splendid sights they see and how laboriously they write, only for every letter to be neglected by their proprietors. We grieve and sympathise with them.

Sometimes when I see *The Hindu* office building, I sigh and then smile. I sigh because we must oppose such a powerful fortress; I smile because the thought arises that we are, after all, succeeding. Our movement does not depend on press strength. However much newspapers black us out, we do not care.

All of you are walking newspapers. No, it is wrong to call you that: you are enduring epics. Many people unable to attend this conference remain confined in small villages. Meet them and tell them of the beautiful scenes, rich ideas, fine addresses, stirring speeches, sound plans and reasons presented here. You came to become little messengers carrying these things to those who were absent. One cannot do that by treating this as a visit to a festival.

You do not return carrying festival gossip. You have directly seen the formation ready to reclaim the sacred land, listened fully to the speeches, impressed them upon your hearts, reflected sincerely and adopted decisions. Carry all of this throughout the country, to every city, town, village and remote hamlet and to every good person you meet. The resolution you finally adopted, that the DMK should enter elections, now makes it imperative that our ideas spread everywhere.

Before the ballot was held, some comrades wanted two or three of us to speak on whether the DMK should contest the general election. I told them that we need not speak. Otherwise people might later say that skilful argument had misled them. Let the people think for themselves.

Even now I do not know where the ballot boxes were placed or how the people voted. Their verdict has given us a great new responsibility.

Four thousand comrades voted that the DMK should not contest, reminding us that this growth alone is insufficient. I will not dismiss them as not our people, nor say they are not good. They are very good. Out of a sincere wish that no danger should befall us, they said, “Do not enter the election now.”

I tell them that no political dictionary says the word “victory” must always be written beside “election.” Election is a democratic duty. Just as reaching maturity marks a new stage in a person’s life, when a movement reaches a certain stage it enters a new stage of politics. We have a democratic duty to demonstrate that we have reached it.

We hold meetings in every town and explain the injustice inflicted upon us under the Five-Year Plan. The Bhakra-Nangal canal is like a vast sea, while the Manimuthar is like a tiny channel. Of Rs. 2,800 crores spent, we did not receive even Rs. 700 crores. We cry, “Friends, comrades, people of the country, question this injustice!”

But what do ordinary people who are not deeply involved in our organisation say after the meeting? “They point out so many errors and deficiencies in the Government, but what do they ask us to do? What path do they show?” We have had no answer.

At least when Saivites hear “Nama Parvathi Pathe” during a puranic recital, they shout “Hara Hara Mahadeva” and consider their duty done. How do we conclude our meetings? The modest say, “With this I conclude my brief remarks,” while those who forget modesty say, “With this I conclude my address.” We do not end by saying, “Therefore, people, do this.”

I have seen only three methods in the world for telling the people what to do: the *vettu* method, violence; the *vottu* method, democracy through the vote; and the *ottu* method, attaching oneself to someone powerful. These are the three paths we have found by which a country gains freedom.

We have seen countries liberated by armed force. In Indo-China, Ho Chi Minh turned forests into tents and mountains into camps. He found protectors among honourable people, put rifles and bombs into the hands of ploughmen and reapers, taught them to hide when attacked, take cover when bombs fell, strike from concealment and then stand and fight, driving the enemy into flight. Thus he succeeded in liberating Indo-China.

I have many energetic younger brothers who ask, “Anna, do you not like this?” I know it, rejoice and take pride in them. But I regret that such conditions do not exist here. Seeking liberation through bombs is not the democratic method to be used in the twentieth century.

Comrades may ask why bombs were used in Indo-China and the Naga country. Those people obtained them because Japanese invasions had left great stores of weapons in those regions. Ho Chi Minh used those weapons; those national fighters use Japanese arms.

Our generation has not even seen bombs. The only explosions our ears have heard are ceremonial blasts at temples, not artillery. Having merely read of that sound in books, adopting armed struggle may display courage but lack wisdom. It may help to an extent but will not carry the ideal to fulfilment.

Another is the method of attachment, the method Congress used: attach oneself firmly to whoever is powerful and secure “freedom.” Congress remained attached to the British and obtained freedom.

If violence is useless and attachment is useless, what remains is the vote.

Some comrades ask whether we can do it. If you cannot, who can? If you do not do even this, what will you do? If we cannot demonstrate our strength here, where can we show it?

Our purpose is to make Dravida Nadu belong to the Dravidians. While we labour to realise that ideal, a rain of repression drenches not only our heads but our hearts. Should the rulers who unleash it not be removed?

One task is to make the country belong to its rightful people. The other is to deal with a Government that obstructs us while we work for that goal, destroys our culture, dreams of annihilating us through abuse and repression, confiscates our books, demands security deposits from our newspapers, throws comrades into prison, imposes Section 144 on meetings and denounces us to outsiders. How long shall we leave it in place? How can we say, “Let them remain there while we work outside for Dravida Nadu”?

While we search for a pot of honey, a scorpion keeps stinging us. Is there any use in letting it sting and saying, “I am concerned only with finding the honey”? That is why I wanted us to contest the election, and the people have given their verdict.

Some ask whether you possess the money to take part. Who financed this State Conference? Where did it come from? Each supporting pole here may cost six rupees, and the white cloth overhead alone a thousand. Where did we obtain them, comrades? Each contribution was small, but together they created this beautiful pavilion. Election funds will be collected in the same way.

Even a snake charmer playing beside an anthill does not know whether a snake is inside. If his melody is right, the snake rises by itself. Go where you must, explain matters in the proper way and, as Jesus said, knock and it shall be opened; ask and it shall be given. He is a man of grace and may obtain grace that way. We are politicians, yet for us too, ask and it shall be given; knock and it shall be opened. These forts are not secured by double bolts. You know people who entered without even knocking.

Did Ramasamy Padayachi knock before entering? No. Did Manickavelar ask before entering? No. They stood briefly at the threshold. Those inside looked through the window and asked who was there. “Do you not know? I am Manickavelar.” They invited him in, and he entered first. Ramasamy Padayachi stood wringing his hands, looked around, saw Govindasamy keeping close watch nearby, told him to move away a little, and went in too. Ministerial office is obtained that easily. Looking at these two shows that we are not undertaking this work for office.

Even to play a minister on stage, one must wear glittering clothes, apply irritating powder, bear a heavy turban and learn a new gait. Only after learning all this can one get the role. Two ministers governing the country endured not even that much; they suddenly became ministers. In such circumstances, would we enter elections merely for ministerial posts?

No one should think we act for office. This is a democratic duty, and the present political situation insists that we recognise it.

Imagine Kamaraj travelling to Delhi to meet Nehru. Nehru first meets the Chinese ambassador, speaks to Japanese commanders, sees the American ambassador, asks Rajkumari Amrit Kaur whether there is a garden party, and only two days later meets Kamaraj.

Nehru asks, “How is your province?” “Peaceful,” says Kamaraj. “The commotion of Acharya’s time?” “Not a trace.” “Periyar Ramasamy?” “A friend useful to us.” “The Communists?” “They have become saints.” “Who opposes you?” “A few youths in the DMK.” “Will they contest elections?” “They, contest an election? Congress will face no competition next time.”

Nehru then imagines that, like a Narayana temple with entrances on all four sides, Congress will have no door at all in the South and everyone may enter. Our decision today is meant to throw dust upon that expectation, not to satisfy some petty desire that we should all sit as ministers.

Does ministerial office make anyone great? If great people enter office, the office gains greatness. Merely sitting in it cannot make a person great. There was a minister called Avinashilingam Chettiar; where is his address now? There were ministers called Jothi Ammal, Varghese, Gurupatham and Madhava Menon; who remembers them? There was Venkatasamy Naidu; who cares about him today? Perhaps even he does not know.

Omandur Ramasamy Reddiar, who once ruled in splendour, now conducts Saivite propaganda. Kumarasamy Raja, who once wielded the sceptre, has withdrawn into a governorship. The aged Andhra Kesari, once prepared to unleash angry repression, has withdrawn from politics. Acharya, who boasted that all political Chanakyan cunning lay with him, now writes about the greatness of Hari Parandhaman.

After seeing all this, would we desire ministerial posts? If such a desire somehow arose, our theatre company is available. Four days in costume would satisfy it. We enter this work not for office, but to prevent Congress from saying it faces no opponent in the next general election.

You know that I met Kamaraj once or twice. On one occasion, with Kamaraj beside him, a close friend said, “Kamaraj is dear to all of us, a good man, and very fond of you.” I replied, “Perhaps.” He said, “Only promise not to trouble him for two or three years; then there will be peace.”

I turned to Kamaraj and asked whether that was his view. Kamaraj smiled. I asked the friend why he spoke when Kamaraj said nothing. He replied that Brahmins had sworn to destroy Kamaraj and Rajagopalachari was planning against him. If we too opposed him, the two forms of opposition might combine and bring him down; therefore he asked for two years without opposition.

I replied that the request insulted me. It portrayed me as one who troubles every newcomer, opposes everyone in office and abandons opposition when begged. Do not ask again. I will not oppose while they do good, and I will not spare them once they deserve opposition.

When Acharya held office, he introduced the Thanjavur Pannaiyal Act, and we supported him. He abolished rationing, and we supported him. He did several good things, and we supported him. We never doubted his intelligence or brilliance, but regretted that it did not benefit us. Then he introduced his education scheme, breaking children’s hands and piercing their eyes, and we immediately opposed him. Likewise, we shall support Kamaraj while he does good and oppose him if he harms the country. Kamaraj then used his customary device and said, “What of it now? Let it be.”

Yesterday I called our General Secretary: “Come, younger brother! Come and assume the leadership! Come; all of us will obey your command.” That was not mere ornament. Such a condition is growing in our Kazhagam and must grow further. Only then can it possess sound democratic character and succeed in reclaiming the country.

Ours is no ordinary land. We were born in a land that yields gold, yet are struck by poverty. We live where iron exists, yet suffer want. Pearls lie in the roaring sea, yet life is like curd beaten by a churn. The forts built and ruled by our ancestors have fallen into ruin, their moats lie waste, no one reads their literature, and we lack even the skill to decipher their script and ideas.

France possesses some resources and lacks many; so does Germany. England has little beyond coal and limestone. But in our homeland, the land of our fathers and ancestors, we gather eaglewood and sandalwood; if we dive, we gather pearls. Elephants pursue us as if protesting that we are leaving them behind.

Anything grows in our country: long-leaf tobacco, sweet sugarcane and red paddy heaped like hills. No kind of produce is absent. Atomic scientists have said that thorium and uranium suitable for atomic power lie buried along our coasts. Why, in a country endowed with such natural wealth, should poverty exist? Why should we suffer deprivation?

Comrades from Burma, Ceylon and Malaya melted our hearts when they said they had gone abroad because they could not obtain even one mouthful of rice here.

No other people endure this disgrace. Do we see a Chinese person sweeping refuse abroad, an American keeping a petty stall, or a Burmese person repairing our road? Yet the Tamil taps rubber in Burmese forests. In Ceylon’s tea estates he exposes his chest, back and stomach to crabs, poisonous creatures, the kangani’s lash and the cruel laws of rulers such as Kotelawala. In Malayan forests he is treated worse than an animal, squeezed dry in Zanzibar and stranded in Trinidad like a bird with broken wings.

The Kaveri floods through the land of his birth and the Vaigai flows where he lived. There is abundant wealth. His ancestors were named Chola, Chera and Pandya, and we still see the ruins of their forts. Yet the Tamil fled abroad because he could not obtain a mouthful of rice here. No one asks why or investigates this condition. Therefore, when the DMK thinks of recovering its country and reflects upon what a country is ours, our hearts truly fill with pride.

In Chennai I said, “Even a dog recognises fertile soil. Do the leaders who govern not know what a dog knows? They do not; what can be done?” Kamaraj sent word through a friend asking why I spoke of dogs and demons. I would not call Kamaraj a dog; I understand that would diminish political dignity. I have observed that dignity for fifteen or twenty years and will not depart from it, however much they divert me. They should not take a metaphor as personally addressed to them.

We entered this work because our country possesses such wealth. Sir R. K. Shanmukham Chetty might have entered it, but perhaps did not regard it as so great a task. Sir A. Ramasamy might have done so, but perhaps it seemed too small for so great a man. Sir Lakshmanaswami might have done so, but perhaps saw no need amid his other work. Rajagopalachari might have entered it, but perhaps feared it would endanger him later. Because they did not, ordinary people like us entered it.

After eight years of searching for the people and spreading principles, a small story comes to mind. A mouse wished to marry its daughter to the strongest possible bridegroom. It approached the sun, but the sun said a dark cloud could cover him and was stronger. The cloud said wind could scatter it. The wind said a wall could stop it. The wall told the mouse, “Do you not bore holes through me? You are stronger.” Thus the young mouse finally married another mouse.

Likewise, in the days of the Justice Party we searched everywhere for wealthy patrons. We imagined that if only we entered Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar’s house and obtained his support, we could recover the country. But once inside, emerging again with honour became a Herculean task. Others resembled the cloud that said it would dissolve in the wind. After searching in this way, we finally came to you, the people. We have not been disappointed; the party has grown beyond expectation.

A country is ready for liberation when the feeling of freedom rises in the hearts of its poets, artists, painters, epic writers and workers of action. On that day, the country has already gained freedom.

Some ask where election money will come from. What do we lack while our artists are with us? I do not mean they should bring their earnings. As far as I know, artists wait for income that has yet to arrive while mourning what has already gone. I mean that if they turn their artistic service toward this cause for six months, the problem is solved.

We must use our opportunity for national liberation and to create conditions against rulers who harm the country. This State Conference has helped establish that situation. Do not let the feeling, enthusiasm and interest gained here stagnate. Develop them throughout the country and follow the path shown by our General Secretary.

With sincere unity, we elected Comrade Nedunchezhiyan chairman of this State Conference and General Secretary of the Kazhagam. Many friends praised him in many ways. Those who saw my happiness as they spoke know that I accepted every truth they stated.

I ask one thing of the comrades. Nedunchezhiyan is far more restrained than I. I sometimes become suddenly angry, and some may become victims of that anger. They may treat it as ridicule or imagine that Anna has developed affection for someone else and is therefore angry with them. If I become angry, it is because I think it will bring them to a better path. Nedunchezhiyan does not become angry even to that extent. Today the Kazhagam has received as General Secretary gold refined in the furnace, with some of my shortcomings removed.

I do not arrive at meetings at the appointed time. If organisers fail to convene a meeting on time, Nedunchezhiyan will take a whip to them. He is refined gold from which my defects have been removed.

When friends asked why I should leave the General Secretary’s responsibility now, I said the Kazhagam must grow very tall; I am short, and I am entrusting it to a tall man.

He has the strength to fulfil all our hopes. For that strength to grow, comrades must cause him fewer difficulties and offer him greater fellowship.

We ourselves chose and practise democracy; no one imposed it upon us. If it had been imposed, people might watch with oil poured into their eyes to see whether it worked properly; even those with dim sight might claim that right. But we voluntarily introduced and are developing democracy. We therefore understand its value and possess responsibility for protecting it.

Democracy allows anyone freely to debate general principles and determine policy. For example, whether to hold the conference in Tiruchirappalli is a fundamental question; one may oppose it or propose Madurai. But after deciding on Tiruchirappalli and beginning to build the pavilion, if democracy is invoked even to debate how many feet deep each pole should be planted, only “democracy” will remain and the pole will not stand. Do not give the General Secretary such difficulties.

I endured them in two ways. I often appeared to listen to every matter though I did not hear many of them. I considered some, forgot what should be forgotten and acted only upon what required action. Nedunchezhiyan is not like that. From his college days he has been a disciplined student. If comrades burden him at the beginning until he bends beneath their demands and wonders at the trouble involved, we will fail to receive the intellectual power he can give us.

Comrade Nedunchezhiyan is my younger brother, and I am proud to call him that. He too has taken pride in regarding me as his elder brother. But while he holds the office of General Secretary, I pledge at this State Conference that I shall accept every order he gives as the order of a leader and submit to it.

If anyone doubts whether I can do so, ask Periyar Ramasamy whether anyone has obeyed a leader more faithfully than I.

Once I toured North India with him. At Lucknow University, Periyar spoke in Tamil and I translated into English. Afterward, the student chairman learned that I held an M.A. and asked me to speak briefly in English. I looked at Periyar; he said not to speak, so I declined. They pressed me again; he again said no. When they insisted a third time, Periyar told me to explain that I had come only to translate his speech, not to deliver one of my own. I did exactly that. Why? Because I conducted myself with such discipline under Periyar. I was the last and most obedient person he had.

## Translator's Notes

This is a single continuous conference address assembled from two OCR source files and 23 scans; both are retained together in the verbatim source block. The source date appears as “20-53-1956,” evidently an OCR corruption of 20-5-1956, consistent with the stated 17-20 May conference. Anna’s “வேட்டு / ஓட்டு / ஒட்டு” passage is deliberate three-way sound play: violence, voting, and attaching oneself to a stronger power. Historical political claims, terminology and numerical comparisons are translated as stated, without modern endorsement or silent correction. The mouse-marriage fable and pavilion-pole analogy are retained in full because each carries a distinct argument about popular strength and democratic discipline.

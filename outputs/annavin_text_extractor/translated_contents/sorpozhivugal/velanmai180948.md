## English Translation

Speech delivered on 18 September 1958.

**Thiru C. N. Annadurai:** Mr. Speaker, as far as the fundamental tax question now before this House is concerned, it is framed in a manner that members of every party can welcome with some degree of satisfaction, irrespective of party differences.

Experience has made it sufficiently clear that agricultural income tax operates not only in our country but in every country employing progressive methods of taxation, without causing injury to anyone. That experience has, after all this time, given our State Government the courage to act.

Within the Indian subcontinent, several other States have already imposed agricultural income tax and are now at the stage of resolving complications arising from it. Though delayed, the ruling party has been compelled to bring forward a progressive law with good intent. I regard that as a sound example of progressive ideas spreading in the country.

Members discussed not only whether this law would advance social justice and what this form of taxation would produce, but ranged from the lice grazing upon a head to the merciful Lord at a feast, explaining every imaginable connection.

The reason for such wide-ranging discussion is that many members feel dissatisfied that, even if the Bill passes, numerous ideas they hold may not be properly realised. They therefore connected it with every possible consequence.

It would have been far more useful had those introducing the tax clearly explained its reason without requiring all these connections. In their statement of objects and reasons, however, they did not stress an intention to establish social justice or the necessity of ending landlord domination.

They effectively said: “The tax revenue the Central Government was to give us has fallen somewhat short. We cannot impose further taxes here. We need a little money in the meantime, looked for a place from which to collect it, and found this place suitable.”

It is therefore clear that the Bill was not brought from highly progressive conviction. It was prepared when the Government and ruling party needed some money and tax revenue. That is why my respected friend M. Kalyanasundaram said nobody should regard it as a progressive measure.

Yet my greatest satisfaction is that people engaged in every field and members of every party, even if resentment remains within them, accept the basic principle that income must be taxed. The country has reached a healthy position in which income should be taxed whether it comes from industry, commerce or agriculture.

Many members strongly condemned exemptions in certain places and asked why exemptions should be granted, particularly to temple lands and lands of monastery heads. To my delight, satisfaction and pride, many Congress members themselves raised this.

Then another Congress member said: “Charitable institutions are maintained to prevent atheism. Should we lay hands upon their heads?” His phrase, “lay hands upon their heads,” makes it seem that he imagines hands have been laid upon somebody else's head.

In imposing a tax, one should lay hands on nobody's head; one should lay hands only on the money box. Nobody asks that hands be placed upon charitable institutions. If one places a hand on the heads of most monastery leaders, one will find only nits and lice there.

The hon. member argued that only they can prevent atheism from growing. True believers will clearly understand that disorder within theism is what spreads atheism in the country. He requested exemption for them. Though he asked out of a good intention to prevent atheism, he did so under the mistaken belief that they cultivate theism.

I consider the exemption unnecessary. They should voluntarily come forward to pay tax in this manner to the Government that administers the country.

When agricultural income tax is imposed, nobody will object to taxing income. But the tax must not operate so as to diminish the income that can be earned or harm agriculture.

Members said cultivators' existing income is already inadequate and is steadily declining because they face many deficiencies. Prices of materials used in agriculture have risen, leaving them unable to buy what they need.

I am neither a great landlord nor a man surrounded by landlords as friends. I do not know whether every such claim is true. But I can understand one thing: money cannot be accumulated suddenly in agriculture as it can in commerce or through owning large factories.

When this tax is implemented—not merely now, but when it actually comes into force—I humbly request the rulers to watch very carefully whether agricultural income tax harms agriculture.

Economists and political thinkers alike have pointed out that a principal cause of the present economic distress, both in our State and across the Indian subcontinent to which it is joined, is our failure to achieve the expected success in agriculture.

The Government must therefore be ready to remove any feature that creates another crisis for agriculture or damages agricultural growth.

An hon. member observed that even laws enacted with good intentions depend for their success upon the rules framed to implement them and the officials who enforce them. When a Bill is passed, the Legislature often has no opportunity to debate those rules with equal clarity.

All of us who support the law's broad purpose therefore wish to point out that the rules must be framed so the statute is not misused and agricultural development is not harmed.

Another point was raised by a lawyer with a spirit of sacrifice: the rules should not become hunting grounds for lawyers. We greatly enjoyed that observation. Yet members also pointed out that it is not enough merely to frame clear rules; the methods used by enforcing officers may themselves be improper.

On one side, all these expenses are recognised. On the other, the great body of cultivators is required to prepare and submit accounts. Even if these farmers understand the tax in principle, they may not know how to maintain accounts in the form demanded by law.

Thus, even a good statute is today placed in officials' hands in circumstances that permit improper application against cultivators. When these two sides meet, even if expected revenue of seventy lakh yields only sixty or fifty lakh, let that be accepted. The Cabinet must ensure it does not receive the people's curse along with the money collected.

The State Government does not know how to relieve the cultivators' severe distress. Prices are rising, yet it lacks authority to control them. If fertiliser prices continue to rise, it lacks authority to control them. It lacks authority to regulate the movement of food from one place to another.

I say this not merely to complain about the division of powers between the State and Central Governments. In all these matters affecting our people, the State Government cannot move even its little finger.

If a State Government unable to help or satisfy cultivators continues imposing tax upon tax, it will truly incur their curse.

I warmly welcome an hon. member's suggestion that the opposition should explain to the country that this law is just and good, and clarify who will and will not be affected. But only if it is introduced with great care will there be a proper way to implement it correctly.

I also wish to mention another issue raised by several members. When it was pointed out that the law was introduced because the Government needed money, one member asked sorrowfully, “Does it not need money? How can government function without it?”

Nobody says government should operate or implement schemes without raising taxes. Support or opposition depends upon when a tax is imposed, upon whom, in what amount, for what purpose, by what collection method, and for what reason. No party today claims it is ready to govern without taxation.

While offering many reasons for raising money through this tax, one member said it would automatically produce a ceiling on landholdings.

I draw attention to a proverb, trusting the hon. Speaker will not misunderstand me: “Catch the crane by placing butter on its head.” Asked how to catch a crane, the foolish method says: put butter upon its head; when the butter melts into its eyes, the crane will close them, and then one may seize its neck.

Likewise, they imagine that if agricultural income tax is imposed upon landlords, the landlords will say, “We do not want either the tax or the land,” surrender their holdings voluntarily, close their eyes like the crane, and allow themselves to be caught.

When asked why they have not imposed a land ceiling, they point not only to this tax but to the achievements of Vinoba, who belongs to no one party but to every party and stands beyond parties. They say a ceiling will arise automatically.

Whether it will arise automatically or not, what difficulty do you face in imposing one? Which party prevents you? What loss will you suffer? What became of your election promise to impose a ceiling? Giving no answer and saying the ceiling will appear by itself is truly strange.

When I was in college, a student smoked excessively. Asked why, he replied with an English phrase now damaged in the OCR and claimed that by smoking he was both burning the white man's wealth and making a sacrifice for freedom.

I mention this because the land-ceiling argument is similarly contrived. The Government has not introduced a ceiling perhaps because it expects landlords to divide their land voluntarily, because it lacks the will to divide it, because it fears some party members will leave if it acts, or because it fears election contributors will stop giving properly. No person with progressive views appears to be preventing it.

The Government must therefore not use this tax as an excuse to prevent or postpone a ceiling on land.

I welcome the law's basic purpose. To ensure that purpose is not defeated, clarify the rules that remain unclear and ensure those administering it do so properly.

Whether the measure raises seventy lakh or seven crore, nobody will stand in the way if the tax paid by the people is organised and spent well, returning to benefit those same people. With that, I conclude. Greetings.

## English Translation

Speech delivered at the State Autonomy Conference organised by the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam in Madras on 28 July 1968:

When we ask for state autonomy, they say, “Speaking in this manner will destabilise the Union government and place the country in grave danger.” Before offering my explanation, I ask them with affection, humility, and firmness:

Who gave you, and for what reason and at what time, the exclusive right to speak as though no one but you possesses the right to recognise danger to the country, make the country strong, or show it the proper path?

If a child walks into the street, a car may strike it and place it in danger. Everyone possesses both the right and the duty to recognise that danger and save the child. Why do they speak as though we have lost our way and they alone are guardians possessing the right to protect us?

Such speech from Congress leaders might have been a permitted error fifteen years ago. Twenty years ago, people might have accepted it as correct because they did not understand these matters. But if they speak that way even in this awakened age, the world will regard it only as an example of arrogance. The claim that no one except Congress members cares about the country is meaningless, inappropriate, and fit to be rejected.

What, then, is the work of the Union government? On the stage, a king is said to summon his minister and ask, “Minister, does rain fall three times a month in our great city?” In the same way, once a month a Union minister gathers the state ministers in Delhi and asks, “How is education in the schools? Has cholera been prevented?” Delhi has no responsibility other than conducting such durbar-style conversations.

It is the state government, not the Union government, that is intertwined with the joys and sorrows of the people.

The Union government may care about the people. But what kind of concern is it? When a fire breaks out in a hut settlement, a wealthy gentleman on the third floor may say, “Some black smoke is visible; it seems to be a major fire.” The Union's concern is like that.

But who trembles in alarm when the hut settlement catches fire? The resident of the neighbouring hut. In the same manner, it is the state government that must confront the people's grievances face to face.

What happens when authority is accumulated far beyond necessity? I recently contacted the Delhi Food Ministry by telephone. Since I do not possess much ability in speaking over the telephone, I asked a friend to speak. Food Minister Jagjivan Ram was away, so a Deputy Minister named Shinde spoke. We tried to explain that a failure to receive Delhi's order permitting sugar to be released from the Kallakurichi sugar mill would cause a great loss.

It took fifteen minutes for him to understand the name Kallakurichi. I do not blame him for being unable to understand the name. Those who divided authority so that the sugar mill is situated in Tamil Nadu while the power to control it rests in Delhi are the ones at fault.

If the strength of the Union government exists only to cause fear in Assam, leave Tamil Nadu floundering, and create anxiety in Kerala; if it exists only to destroy our independent thought little by little and leave us without even the ability to think, then it becomes our duty, through our collective strength and the strength of each one among us, gradually to reduce that unjust power.

The central government was strong under the Gupta Empire. It was strong under the Mughal Empire. It was strong under the British Empire. But where are those empires today?

I genuinely regret having to compare the present empire with empires that collapsed. Those who know history understand that whenever those imperial rulers sought greater strength for their empires, collapse followed.

History can show no central administration stronger than the one under Aurangzeb. But we should reflect for a moment on what became of that empire.

Our concern is simply that the Union rulers desire to carry a burden they cannot lift. If the strength of the Union government means the aggregate strength produced by adding together the individual strength of every state, that is defensible in argument, good for politics, and suitable in practice. But if the Union government alone possesses every right and all power while the states possess only enough authority to totter along, that is suitable for nothing.

When the Union government takes all state powers and hoards them, the states do not merely become weak; the Union government does not thereby acquire any new strength of its own.

Let us consider every power other than national defence. Let the states take the powers they need. Then let the Union government take the remaining powers that the states willingly grant it.

The present Constitution should be examined, the distribution of powers between the state and Union governments should be changed, and greater powers should be given to the states. I have proposed to the Administrative Reforms Commission that a committee be appointed to study this question.

What work does a state government have today? It must perform countless tasks: feeding the people, providing employment, establishing industrial justice, preserving public health, and developing the wealth of education.

They say that a strong Union government is needed. We must consider: for what purpose does the Union government require strength, and against whom is that strength to be used?

Power may belong to an individual, an individual organisation, a state administration, or the Union administration. But they keep speaking without understanding or explaining for whom and for what purpose that power is to be used.

If the Union government needs strength to resist Chinese aggression, we are certainly prepared to help provide that strength.

When it retains authority over every small matter, from the Kallakurichi sugar mill to the small factory at Katpadi, the result is that it cannot devote sufficient attention to major matters. That is why we say authority must not be concentrated in Delhi.

It is therefore wrong to say that only a strong Union government can solve problems. If they hesitate to grant state autonomy, our fear is that, burdened with excessive powers, they may grow ineffective in major matters such as defence.

The principle of state autonomy was born precisely to change the condition in which every superior power rests in Delhi.

## Translator's Notes

“மாநில சுயாட்சி” is rendered as “state autonomy,” the established political and constitutional term in this speech. The translation distinguishes it from secession or complete sovereignty, neither of which is proposed in the text.

The theatrical question about whether rain falls “மாதம் மும்மாரி” invokes the conventional image of a king ceremonially asking his minister about ideal rainfall and public welfare. It is translated closely as satire on remote supervision.

“தர்பார் பேச்சு” refers to ceremonial court or durbar-style conversation without direct administrative engagement.

“மத்திய துரைத்தனம்” and related expressions are translated contextually as Union administration or Union domination, depending on the sentence.

The proposal described in the speech leaves national defence with the Union and argues that states should possess necessary powers, with remaining powers voluntarily assigned to the Union. It is translated as stated and not evaluated against later constitutional doctrine.

Political, constitutional, administrative, historical, and biographical assertions are translated in the speaker's voice and have not been independently verified.

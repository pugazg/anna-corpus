## English Translation

The following is a summary of the speech delivered by Arignar Anna in Chennai’s 49th Ward on 27 May 1960:

Friends!

“In certain quarters of Tamil Nadu, there prevails a disheartening atmosphere in which people ask whether even education is necessary. The reason is the unfortunate situation that has arisen here, in which every office can be held without an education. I will not say that education gives one everything, but nothing is spoiled by education. The work of a reading room is, and ought to be, to sharpen natural intelligence, compare it with the knowledge of the world, and engage that intelligence in meeting our country’s needs.

“Our friends may have found themselves obliged to conduct this reading room while contending with political aversion and hostility. However much you set politics aside, without our even knowing it, politics continues to follow us like our shadow.

“When our ancestors ruled, the king’s entire duty was to prevent wild animals from entering the country and, with an army, destroy enemies who looked human but possessed animal natures and sought to enslave certain countries, thereby protecting his land and people. In those days, the people had little need for politics. But today’s government is not of that kind.

“At a wedding held this morning, our friends spoke very movingly, and all their talk concerned how many children a family might have. Those who govern the country are carrying on a great campaign saying that if people have too many children and families grow large, the country’s condition will deteriorate. Politics therefore intervenes even in what we eat and how many children we have. I am not speaking cunningly like Achariyar and saying that this is unnecessary. We must take an interest in the acts of a government.

“Government intervenes in the daily work performed by people in our country, the quality of that work, the wages paid for it, and the way those wages are fixed. Since government intervenes in all these things, should it not be a good government and also our own government?

“It was to insist that this age should not remain like the old one that the national poet Bharati sang, ‘Everyone is a sovereign of this country.’ Some among today’s people have begun to rule like kings, although there was no earlier basis for expecting them to rule.

“Let me give an example. The people had doubts about whether Chief Minister Kamaraj would govern this country. Yet he himself says, ‘They say I did not study in college. What if I did not study? Is the work getting done or not?’ We could not previously have concluded that he would obtain an opportunity to govern and speak in this way.

“That is why we can make anyone govern. Not only can we appoint them, we can watch whether they govern properly. Beyond merely watching, we can supervise them and offer particular suggestions while such a government rules. If this government does not function properly, we can create another. Today the people possess authority and rights to this extent. Only if we understand all this and act will we obtain good government.

“Before we exercise our right once every five years, the work of reading rooms should be to explain daily events to the public and encourage people to take an interest in them. If instead you read only stories and ask whether you will obtain salvation in the other world, I cannot tell you, because I have never gone there and returned.

“Rather than reading such books, we must read books and politics that help answer how we should live in this world, what we must do to live, and how the result of life may be good. With the assistance of reason, we must consider what life is, what government is, what a right is, what a desire for liberation is, and what agitation and struggle mean.

“When I say this, Congress friends at this meeting should not think, ‘He belongs to that party, so naturally he will insist upon this particular idea.’

“Because our friends have established the Purasai Reading Room, I wish to describe the ideas prevailing in the country today and stimulate your thought for a moment.

“One party praises the government by saying, ‘Everything is proceeding well. Some deliberately find fault with the government. The present administration is just and honest; therefore, there is no defect of any kind in it.’ I need not say which party that is.

“If members of other parties make accusations, is that their fault? If I ask Sathyavani Muthu, ‘You find fault; what fault have you actually seen?’ she will immediately reply, ‘Do you not know? Under the British, eight measures of rice sold for a rupee. Now only one measure is available for a rupee. Do you not know that, though you know how to eat?’ On hearing this, I could only feel ashamed; I could not answer.

“There are defects in the administration. When asked how they will remove them, members of the ruling party say, ‘The plans will be fulfilled.’ Sathyavani Muthu immediately asks, ‘How long must we keep watching you say this? We have watched for thirteen years, and you have been unable to do us any good.’ They answer, ‘Wait another ten years.’

“Another group asks, ‘What exactly will you do in those ten years? Please at least tell us that.’ They reply, ‘Do you not know? We shall build dams and factories.’ If asked, ‘Will the dam be here?’ they say, ‘Good heavens, what do north and south matter in this?’ Ideas of this kind are now colliding with one another in the country.

“Another idea is attached to this: ‘This country is India. Whether it is Salem or Kanpur, it is all India. Accept it.’

“In opposition, others present many sources and say, ‘India is not one country; the word Indians does not exist in our literature.’ They ask how it can be true that the British merely gave the collective name India to what had existed as many regions.

“Let me offer an example. There is a proper order determining how the hand should be arranged in our body and where the eye and ear should be. Our hand has five fingers, and each performs a distinct task. When we write, only two fingers work. When we beckon someone, all five work. Consider what would happen if all five fingers were required always to remain stuck together.

“Until you reach a sound decision on these two kinds of question, the problem will continue to grow.

“If you think what Congress says is right, join it and work for its growth. If instead you think what the movement says is right, join the movement and work for its growth. Do not take this to mean that I am asking you to join a political party. I emphatically say that you must necessarily come forward in support of one position.

“To say ‘We want no politics’ without doing this is not good.

“Minister Kakkan says that people of the Harijan communities drink water only from silver tumblers in every home. Others say, ‘No, they drink only from tin tumblers.’ Go to the settlement and learn the truth. If you then meet Kakkan again, ask, ‘Sir, why do you tell such lies at a public meeting?’

“In this age, you have heard as much as you need, and we too have said as much as we need. I know that the young have reached a decision. Friends of this reading room must work to explain these two positions to older people, elders, and mothers, and lead them too along a sound path.

“The unity of a single India spoken of by Congress resembles unity formed on a train. They themselves now suffer greatly because of this unity.

“Two families travel on a train. A child is eating sweets. Another child sees it and asks for one, and the first child gives it. The child’s mother immediately says, ‘See how united these children are! Because of this, your daughter must marry my son.’ The others laugh. But when the train reaches Villupuram, they get off, and there is no longer any connection between the two families. If the boy later grows up and says, ‘I shall marry only the girl who loved me one day on a train,’ can it happen?

“A man travelling by train puts his hand through the window and buys peas from a vendor. But if he opens his hand, the peas will fall; if he does not, he cannot eat them.

“Congress now struggles in the same way. How long can this false unity last? How can we rely upon it? I therefore ask you to understand this and prepare the way to change this government in 1962.

“Kamaraj is now touring Thanjavur district. At one meeting, he asked what Dravida Nadu could mean after the Andhra, Kerala, and Kannada regions had separated, and why the DMK had not stopped them from separating. He need not study advanced politics to learn the answer. It is enough for Kamaraj to listen to one meeting addressed by our Navalar Nedunchezhiyan; the truth will become clear to him.

“What we have said from the beginning is: ‘Separate along linguistic lines and unite along ethnic lines.’

“Kamaraj says that the head of a family who has gone to the office will never return. Our Navalar explains this issue as though explaining it to primary-school children. If Kamaraj still cannot understand it, it means he is unfit even for that class. He does not understand the question of Dravida Nadu.

“While the woman heading a household is cooking, suppose the man says, ‘I know how to cook. You go to your mother’s house.’ Instead of cooking properly, he puts every ingredient into a pot, pours in water, and lights the stove. What will happen? The woman observing him will say, ‘You need not concern yourself with this matter. Please go and sit on the front platform.’

“Likewise, we must ask those who do not know how to sit aside. I ask you to do this in 1962.”

## Translator's Notes

The source identifies itself as a summary of a speech delivered in Chennai’s 49th Ward on 27 May 1960. All seven Tamil OCR pages remain unchanged.

“படிப்பகம்” is a reading room or local study centre, not merely a library collection. “புரசைப் படிப்பகம்” is retained as Purasai Reading Room, reflecting the place-name in the source.

“ஆச்சாரியார்” is retained as Achariyar, the political honorific used by the source. “நாவலர்” is expanded only where the source itself gives “நாவலர் நெடுஞ்செழியன்.”

“அரிசன மக்கள்” is a period expression derived from “Harijan.” It is rendered as “people of the Harijan communities” to preserve the historical wording while signalling that it is not neutral contemporary terminology.

The rice measure “படி” is translated generically as “measure,” since its exact historical volume is not required by the comparison. The train, peas, body, cooking, and family analogies are translated closely as argumentative illustrations.

Political, electoral, economic, regional, linguistic, numerical, and historical assertions are translated in the speaker’s voice and have not been independently verified.

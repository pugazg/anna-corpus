## English Translation

Speech delivered by Arignar Anna in 1948, when untouchability and the temple-entry question were raging:

Everywhere in Tamil Nadu stand beautiful temples that splendidly reveal the imagination of their sculptors. They were built by the crowned Chera, Chola, and Pandya monarchs and by the Pallavas. Distinctive virtues and glories are attributed to each, and they are praised as shrines celebrated in sacred songs. A miracle is told of every temple. Names such as Thayumanavar, Dayaparar, the one who dissolved incurable karma, the one who performs the sacred dance at Thillai, and the Lord of Srirangam are themselves spoken with rich meaning. Many stories of glory are told: the temple whose door opened on hearing a song, the shrine where coins were received upon the steps, and the holy place from which a divine messenger went forth.

In other words, temples are abundant. Their beauty and glory are proclaimed. It is said that worshipping there gives happiness in this world and the next. A proverb says that one should not live in a town without a temple. Devotional songs ask, “What sort of feet are those that do not circle the sacred temple? What sort of eyes are those that do not behold the Lord?” The temple is called the abode of purity.

After saying all this, piling up books about it, erecting houses of God everywhere, and presenting in song, dance, and picture their grandeur, the necessity of visiting them, and the benefits gained there, we inspire desire, attachment, and affection in people. Then we behave like someone who plucks the veena’s strings but blocks the ears of the person who comes to hear the music; like an artist who creates a lovely picture and invites everyone to see it, but blindfolds some who arrive; or like someone who praises the sweetness of fruit juice and drives away a person who asks for a little to drink. Having proclaimed that temples possess glory and the power to confer benefits, we prevent not merely a few but nearly eight crore people from coming or entering.

This did not begin yesterday. It has continued generation after generation, either as though they were being punished for some offence, or simply because those upon whom we inflict cruelty have lacked the power to punish us. Is this proper, just, or righteous? Can it continue indefinitely? These are not merely questions; they are the heartbeats of anyone whose thought is free from deceit.

Parents sometimes show an object to children and then hide it. We proclaim the greatness of sacred places and lock them. Why may people of one particular community not enter? The true reason is simply that we did whatever we could get away with. Other explanations are waves arising in the mind, not truth.

Think of the pain produced by identifying a place as beneficial and glorious, then saying that one particular group alone may not enter it. Think also how hard our hearts must be to say so. Tonight, close your hand and call the little child in your home. Say, “Little brother, there is a wonderful thing in my hand. It shines, and no one else can obtain it.” The child will jump and plead, “Brother, give it to me.” Reply, “No, I cannot. It is not for you; I will not give it.” You will discover the trouble that follows: pinching, wrestling, and even tears. The mother will hurry in, embrace the child, call it a wicked game, pull your hand away, and angrily say, “Do not deceive the child.”

Within a family, even this may contain a kind of amusement. But do we understand how much pain arose in the child’s heart?

If a child’s mind suffers so, consider the cruelty we inflict upon eight crore people. We proclaim the greatness of God’s house while saying, “You alone must not come there.” Think whether that is right.

We have already preached that preventing devotees from going to the place where the Supreme dwells is a sin like separating a child from its mother.

We have already explained that untouchability as practised in today’s Hindu religion differs from the way ancient wise and noble people lived, and that those worthy people did not observe it.

We proudly count these people as members of Hinduism and swell with satisfaction over the statistics. Yet refusing them admission even to the temple regarded as the religion’s source has created a wound in the heart, a wound that cannot heal. Seeking relief, many among them turn to the refuge of the Cross or to Islam. We have already warned that Hindu society is diminishing.

We have tried plucking every emotional string: courage, sacrifice, duty, and devotion.

We have drawn the people’s attention to Nandanar and Thiruppaan Alvar.

We have sung the devotional verse: “Even if they are outcastes who wander eating carrion, behold them as the gods whom we worship.”

We have sung that verse and tried.

The effort made in our country to abolish untouchability and open sacred temples has exceeded the effort taken elsewhere to change practices that were unnecessary and harmful.

On thousands upon thousands of platforms we have sung: “We shall not regard caste or religion. If they have attained noble birth in this land, whether Vedic scholar or of another community, they are one.”

We have discussed the true thought of Vyasa, the broad vision of Parasara, the essence of the Vedas, and the truth of the Upanishads.

How many conferences, reports, satyagrahas, fasts, and agitations arose to wipe away this stain? Yet, even after them all, we must speak with a sigh. One may see Madurai Meenakshi but cannot worship Ranganatha. One may see the Lord of Palani but cannot behold the Lord of Tirumalai. People narrate with every emotion the greatness of the Lord of Thillai, who granted liberation to Nandan, yet one cannot see him. What a tragedy! The sense of difference shown among people has now extended even to God. We have reached a condition in which some gods permit themselves to be seen and others must not be seen. Why? Whom are we to ask?

This is an urgent and necessary question that must be put to reason and courage.

From time to time, news appears that a few temples in a few places have been opened. P. S. Murthi, an Andhra leader and member of the Legislature, announces that he will take Adi-Dravidas to Tirupati hill. Orthodox people intend to oppose him.

Today, however, the old agitation and panic are absent. That does not mean that the attitude of orthodox people who insist upon following ancient doctrines has changed completely.

Shock, terror, the determination to prevent entry at any cost, and anxiety that total ruin might follow have declined and continue to decline. But it is neither closeness, love, nor wisdom to say that temple entry can occur only after a complete change of heart. A law is therefore necessary, and a propaganda programme explaining it is urgently necessary.

The former and present condition of temples, the benefits they confer, and who receives which benefit are separate questions.

Likewise, the paths by which Adi-Dravidas may progress and prosper, and whether temple entry alone will remove their suffering, are separate questions.

I am not now speaking of those separate issues. Temples exist in this country, and Adi-Dravidas exist here too.

We say that we may enter temples but they may not. Yet we call the very people we bar from temples children of the motherland and our brothers. When boasting that we are a nation of forty crore people, we say to them, “Do not come near; stand at a distance.” We forget that they number eight crore, one-fifth of the population. We plunge the country into harm as though one of its five senses had become defective.

Even those who speak of many reforms within temples prevent Adi-Dravidas from entering.

Likewise, even people prepared to speak lovingly about providing Adi-Dravidas with education and health facilities say that they must not be allowed temple entry.

This strange disposition has a cause. The attitude is not meaningless.

They consent to provide charity but feel troubled when asked to grant a right. Giving charity is easy; granting rights is difficult. Charity demands little sacrifice and little courage, while granting rights requires much of both.

The relationship between giver and recipient of charity remains approximately that of master and slave. Giving and receiving a right is different: it establishes equality and equal status. Charity is the result of the feeling “Alas, poor thing.” A right is the result of the cry “Why?” That is why they do not consent. Untouchability is a device for stealing rights.

We must reflect upon what a painful absurdity untouchability is.

Harmful things should not be touched: rubbish, refuse, and foul-smelling substances. We certainly ought to practise non-contact with them. Acid, explosives, poison, a striking cobra, a stinging scorpion, and many other dangerous things should not be touched. That is reasonable. But consider the pain and meaninglessness of saying, “We will not touch you,” to many crores of people, our compatriots who have lived alongside us since our ancestors’ time.

No one who reflects upon it can argue that this cruelty should continue. No one can claim that untouchability has produced, produces, or will produce any benefit. On the contrary, its countless harms can be demonstrated without elaborate explanation. Eight crore people are treated as untouchable, and we wound the hearts of them all. What greater harm is needed?

We rejoice on hearing scholars say that compassion, righteousness, love, and friendship must grow among the country’s people, and that these qualities mark civilised life. We praise Vivekananda’s teachings, celebrate Vallalar Ramalinga’s Samarasa Suddha Sanmarga, and hold festivals for Raja Ram Mohan Roy and Dayananda. In our own day, we even contribute money to the Harijan Fund. But when someone calls, “Come, let us abolish untouchability completely,” qualities such as righteousness, compassion, love, and friendship become tortoises withdrawing into their shells. From somewhere, the cobra called orthodoxy rises and hisses. The weapon of reason is needed to subdue it.

We fold our hands before a forger. A sex worker commissions a special ablution. A destroyer of families conducts a temple consecration. A profiteer donates a silver bull. Are there barriers preventing people of deficient conduct, filthy minds, and crooked ways from entering temples? No.

Yet we bar Adi-Dravidas alone and say that they must not enter. Is that just?

Your answer is, “It plainly is not, but…” is it not?

Friends, we have said “but, but” for too long. Forget the “but,” abandon fear, and deliver the verdict: “Therefore, untouchability must not exist, and temple entry is essential.”

People speak hesitantly with “but” not because they believe those treated as untouchable should remain outside the temple, but because they say, “Let the government pass a law.” What more does the government need? The people’s minds have not yet matured to the point where such a law is unnecessary; but neither are they determined to prevent such a law. As a citizen, I tell the government that this is precisely the right moment to legislate temple entry.

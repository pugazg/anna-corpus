## English Translation

Anna: Mr. Speaker, I respectfully state that even at this final stage before the Bill becomes law, I object to it. I wish chiefly to advance one argument: do not take hasty action and throw the situation into disorder. The Honourable Finance Minister may imagine that he has succeeded in his attempt to render a small service, as far as he can, to me and to the party to which I belong.

Honourable Mr. C. Subramaniam: I have not succeeded. You have not yet finished speaking.

Anna: Nevertheless, because certain questions of principle emerged from it, I am obliged to say a few words about them. Although the Dravidar Kazhagam and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam have separated, the Finance Minister said that today the DMK’s curtain had been torn away and that they had seen our true form. We will not reveal our true form so easily.

Even if the Finance Minister has truly discovered our form, his own statement shows a difference in policy between the Dravidar Kazhagam and the DMK. He said there was nothing in the DMK’s newspaper *Nam Nadu* or in my writings upon which action could be taken.

Honourable Mr. C. Subramaniam: I did not say there was nothing to criticise. I said there was no ground for legal action.

Anna: He says there is no ground for legal action. That strengthens my argument. The Finance Minister therefore considers it DMK policy to express these ideas through lawful means, while it is Dravidar Kazhagam policy to express them unlawfully and without concern for the law. This alone reveals the distinction between the two organisations.

Contradiction, variation, and difference may ordinarily appear in Tamil to be words signifying the same thing, but I need hardly point out that when deep ideas are examined, each has a distinct meaning. I trust the Honourable Finance Minister understands this. Still, I accept the ruling party’s desire to say something about the DMK at this moment as an honour it has conferred upon us.

They also said that when legislative issues confront us, we become frightened and shut our eyes tightly, as children do on seeing terrifying objects. In truth, we are children, and you are displaying an excessive amount of terror. Both points emerge from your argument.

Perhaps they say that we run away and close our eyes, and then laugh, believing those acts to be cowardice. But the Honourable Finance Minister surely knows the distinction between the two English words “prudence” and “cowardice.” Even if it is true that we leave the House, it is not through cowardice. We may have risen and left because remaining would be improper.

I wish to tell the Finance Minister one more thing through you. Closing one’s eyes out of fear is one thing; people also close their eyes when they are disgusted by what they see. At times we may have closed our eyes in disgust at an act. He must understand that closing one’s eyes is not cowardice.

When Mr. Kalyanasundaram spoke, he said one should not take shortcuts in the name of rationalism. Had he understood that rationalism exists to change the practice of taking crooked paths, he would not have said so. He asked: if one does not believe in the puranas and epics, why burn them? Why take a shortcut? Such shortcuts, he said, should not be permitted.

I shall tell him one thing. To see how inappropriate it is to call the course advocated by one of the most influential leaders in our province a shortcut, consider a historical example. Long ago, Martin Luther publicly burned what the Christian world regarded as sacred in Europe, arguing against the Bible remaining in Latin and against the Pope’s resulting dominance. Since our friend Mr. Kalyanasundaram frequently travels to Europe, I ask him to use some leisure there to learn about this as well.

Therefore, burning the Constitution today cannot simply be regarded as a contradictory act or a shortcut. Yet I truly understand the Police Minister’s argument that disorder may arise in society. If other means of preventing it are possible, this law is unnecessary. As I said earlier, enacting a foolish law would only incite them further to perform that act.

The Finance Minister would understand the difference between our methods and those of the Dravidar Kazhagam if he observed how each treats Brahmins. Brahmins are members of the DMK. We believe that Brahminism must be destroyed by Brahmins themselves. Although this may take time, we consider it the sound path.

The DMK works to abolish Brahminism by civilised means, even if that takes time. The Dravidar Kazhagam is eager to accomplish the same thing differently, intensely, and without delay. I do not know what advantage the Finance Minister gains by saying, “These two parties are the same.” Even if he gains the advantage of preventing a few Brahmins from becoming members of the DMK, I am not concerned. But we are obliged to state our party’s policy clearly, and that is why I say this.

The Finance Minister asked whether, in order to abolish slums, all slums could be burned. As he asked it, I wondered how many such questions he must have used to confound the opposition when he practised in Coimbatore as a great lawyer.

When municipal authorities order that no slum should exist at a particular place, and huts are erected there again in violation of the order, they dismantle those huts or burn them down.

If a corpse lies rotting, it must be taken away and buried or else burned. It cannot be kept without cremation; no one would keep it. I therefore tell him there is no use in merely raising the argument, “Can it be burned?”

Finally, I had said that Mr. Kamaraj and Periyar should meet because the two are mutual friends. The Police Minister replied that, as Sangam literature speaks of one-sided love, Periyar might possess great affection for Kamaraj while Kamaraj had none for him.

It was inappropriate for him to speak here like the messenger, charioteer, or bard of Sangam poetry. Perhaps, standing between them, he knows well what kind of affection Kamaraj has for Periyar and Periyar for Kamaraj. I do not know how much he knows about it.

Even if Periyar’s affection is truly one-sided and Kamaraj has none for him, I believe they will admit that Periyar is a leader possessing sufficient influence among the people for Kamaraj to go and meet him. He did not suddenly arrive in our state from Africa yesterday morning. Nor is he some strange being left among us by the British when they departed.

For a long time he has laboured continuously for this country. He served as a member and leader of the Tamil Nadu Congress and left because certain policies could not be achieved through Congress. Nevertheless, during the general election he worked continuously for you because he believed that Congress should remain in office under present conditions.

Just as it is proper for a child to go to his father despite any estrangement, even if Kamaraj has no affection for Periyar, I believe it is proper for him to approach Periyar at least as a former leader and former comrade. I imagine Kamaraj is absent from the House today because he would be hurt if anyone said something here about his former leader and comrade.

I will not say, as the Honourable Finance Minister said of me, that he has run away. In more civilised language, we may say that he has hidden himself. We may suppose that instead of grieving here, he is obtaining peace of mind elsewhere.

While speaking, however, the Police Minister remarked, “Why do you not go and meet him instead of Kamaraj? Even if father and son have separated, it would be most appropriate for them to meet for this reason.” He said that by meeting again and reuniting, we might recover the advantage lost through separation.

With his good intention as my support, I shall try to meet Periyar. But until then, I ask you to hold this law back. If the law is enacted first and I then meet him, I will not be carrying a message; I will merely be giving him notice that committing this offence brings so many years in prison. I need not go and tell him that. He can see it himself through the newspapers.

If my meeting him for this reason would truly produce a benefit, and if we could reunite with him, I wish to tell the Finance Minister that a son forgetting what his father did and returning to join him is not one of his faults but one of his virtues.

Even if he disregards me, I shall go and speak with him. Until then, hold this law back. I know what he will say if the law is enacted before I go: “I am eighty years old. What does it matter if I spend three years in prison? No one need grieve for me.”

Therefore, even at this moment, I wish to say one final time: what is needed now is a meeting. A meeting is necessary; a law is not. I conclude by stressing this once more.

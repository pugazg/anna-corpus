## English Translation

In 1948, Arignar Anna delivered a speech on the subject “Youth Are Needed in Independent India”:

Young people are the spearheads of the army that fights for rights. A storm of revolution lives in their hearts. Theirs is the season before the poison called anxiety has been mixed into the pool of life. In those pure young hearts, the sun called reason has risen and destroyed selfish, pleasure-seeking thoughts resembling venomous insects.

Youth is like the breeze that crosses a sandalwood forest, absorbs its fragrance and coolness, and comes to delight the people.

Illusion and mental confusion tremble to approach the young. Young people abound in the resolve to brush aside empty talk and walk forward, the courage that insists upon suppressing the arrogance of the worthless, and the nature that boils over and strikes at injustice without regarding danger. Questions such as “Can it be done? Is the time right? Do we have sufficient strength? Should we not wait until tomorrow?” do not sound sweet to them. What reaches a young person’s ear is this: “Have you heard of this cruelty? Are we to remain idle? Are we people without feeling? Set out, fight, and continue the struggle until injustice perishes or we do.” Young people are steel rods in motion. Their hearts contain enthusiasm like a waterfall, their faces bear the glow of courage, and their actions reveal a throbbing vitality. Standing with bowed heads, heaving sighs, and dragging out the question “Is this something we can do?” are ways they dislike. They are heroes of action.

Young people are not satisfied merely with their fighting ability; they possess ideals. Having read that welfare and civilisation flourish in various countries of the world, that education and industry abound there, and that their people live happily, will they not grow angry when they see slavery and ignorance writhing in their own motherland? When education and industry are advancing everywhere in the world, will they not naturally feel outraged to see more than eighty out of every hundred people here remain illiterate? Having studied the history of noble people who fought for rights, martyrs who sacrificed everything to destroy injustice, and great heroes who laboured so that the people might live, how could their minds adopt the devotional refrain, “What can we possibly do, O Supreme Being?” That is why young people are full of vigour.

The young hero beats his chest and declares, “My country is a golden land; nowhere is there its equal.” The country must rise; its resources must increase; wealth must grow; and industry must prosper. The habits of one person subduing and exploiting another, of imposing distinctions of high and low, of gazing sorrowfully at the sky, of preaching Vedanta that life is an illusion and the grave alone is certain, and of singing weary verses asking for the boon of never being born again must change. As Bharathi said, “Everyone is a sovereign of this country”; all must live with rights and joy. The delightful dream of youth is that the country should become a flower garden and attain a condition praised by all the world.

The young person does not understand independence or liberation to mean merely a change in legal terminology. He regards it as a new condition, new beauty, a new form and new happiness: the country assuming a new splendour. That is indeed the truth.

“Why is there so much poverty?” comes the question.

“What can be done? We live in an enslaved country,” comes the answer.

“Why does illiteracy run rampant?”

“Slavery and foreign rule.”

“Why has industry not developed?” comes the question.

“Can a caged bird fly?” comes the reply.

Thus a single cause was shown for the country’s many different defects. Again and again, people were told that there was one medicine capable of making them all disappear: independence. Young people were captivated. They laboured while imagining that delight, and told everyone that in independent India happiness would be born, knowledge would grow, the enslavement of one person by another would end, and wealth would abound.

Knowing that such is the picture called independent India, young people must prepare themselves to create it.

A country that has gained independence resembles a stage curtain painted long ago with all manner of colours, faded by time until its figures have disappeared and its colours have run and scattered, while even the occasional repairs have worn away. Such curtains may be seen in fourth- and fifth-rate theatrical companies in their early days.

Any country freed after a long period in the grip of slavery will resemble that curtain. There is no benefit in being satisfied that it is enough. Nor is there any benefit in saying that we will repair a little here and a little there. The fate of a fourth-rate theatrical company will follow: no fame and no income. It is useless to speak deferentially: “But the person who painted this curtain was divinely blessed with the magic of colour. Am I worthy to erase and repaint what such a person created?” It must indeed be erased and repainted. The ability of the young must produce a new picture.

Yet does that not require several things? Many kinds of colour, bowls in which to pour and mix them, brushes, and, more importantly than all these, the means of acquiring the skill to paint are all necessary. The work youth must perform in independent India has comparable requirements. The time of battle, when sinews were taut and pledges were given, has ended. In independent India, bends must be straightened, pits filled, roads repaired, cunning plotters restrained, people without minds of their own educated, exploiters checked, the weary invigorated, famine prevented, learning spread, industry developed, and youth made fruitful. Think of it, and task after task appears rapidly before the mind’s eye.

These things must be done, and the determination required for them must be secured. Whatever is done must be done well. Slogans, processions, and shop closures are not enough for this. The method, skill, means, and approach are different, certainly different. Young people must become teachers and show the world to villagers. They must become physicians and provide remedies for the illnesses of both body and mind. They must work to build bridges and reservoirs, change agricultural methods, reform industrial practice, and transform village administration. They must tactfully free the people from the grip of antiquity, reveal the splendour of modernity, and create a new world. Young people must become the sculptors of that new world.

This requires ability and courage. It requires knowledge and inquiry. It requires contact with the outside world. Above all, it requires character and training. Young people must not say, “Let the elders do this work. These tasks cause great weariness; we shall confine ourselves to fighting, counterattacking, and bathing in fire.” A new picture cannot be painted by those who cherish the old. Becoming angry after they have painted it is futile.

When young people undertake this work, antiquity will threaten them; orthodoxy will intimidate them; caste will resist them; and convention will rage. Blind doctrines, stubborn prejudices, and meaningless attachments and affections will all rise like bats circling in a dark hall. Opposition will emerge from relatives, elder and younger brothers, parents, elders, and every unexpected quarter. Young people need the character to continue their work without fear or fatigue in the face of all this. They need clarity. They must learn where and how these things have been accomplished elsewhere and then act; for that, contact with the outside world is necessary. Many difficulties may arise while doing this work. A strong heart tempered with the patience to correct them is required. Young people need a disposition suited to all these demands.

The enemy is an illusionist. He cannot be seen by the eye, and even when he begins to appear, he comes not in one form but in many. He appears in the form of a teacher who angrily asks, “Can this injustice be tolerated?”; in the form of a mother standing with tearful eyes and asking, “My son, why do you torment us like this?”; in the form of a father who says in fury, “Sinner, do not show me your face”; in the form of an elder who speaks with disgust, “Bah, this fellow has become a wastrel”; and in the form of a friend who mocks, “What an enormously great genius! He has discovered something entirely new.” The illusionist called antiquity appears in all these ways. Young people need the skill to resist and fight it and, when it claims kinship and speaks words of affection, not to be bewitched by them.

What do we see around us today? There goes a gleaming motorcar, a symbol of civilisation. True, inside sits a gentleman of leisure: wealth and pleasure, pleasure through wealth, wealth for pleasure, a wealthy man enjoying himself. Very well, let the motorcar run. But what do we see coming from the opposite direction? A human being puts his chest to a burden cart and pulls it in place of a bullock. Poverty and labour: the labourer is poor, while the gentleman of leisure travels by motorcar. Is he going to the house of a courtesan? No, to the shrine of Sambamurthi!

Must this spectacle continue to exist in independent India?

There is a gathering. An ornament of oratory, a speaker who is bliss itself, pours forth a continuous discourse on the relationship between the individual soul and the supreme soul. Let him speak. He sings, “All that is seen is illusion; know that all that is worn is illusion.” It is an alapana in Kalyani. But look at his finger: a Blue Jager diamond. Beside him is a silver vessel containing fresh milk boiled with powdered sugar candy. Is his mind attentive to the subject on which he speaks? The doctrine of illusion!

Will this exist in independent India?

The gentleman enjoys the discourse on illusion and returns home. The servant who accompanied him has learnt a line from the song and sings softly:

“This body is false indeed.”

It reaches the master’s ear. He calls out loudly and orders:

“Pull the door shut!”

Will this too exist in independent India? Will we still hear, “Hey, move aside a little. Do not touch me; stand at a distance”?

“What is my lineage? What is my gotra? What is my status? Can I give my daughter to that fellow? What is my caste, and what is his?” Will this fury exist in independent India? Young people did not labour so that everything seen today might remain exactly as it is. The full meaning of independence is a condition in which such horrors have been abolished. Young people need strength of heart to labour for it.

The great task before youth is to find a way for every human being to live as a human being. There is the immense work of increasing both the country’s natural resources and the richness of its people’s minds. There is the responsible work of creating a new life. For this, young people need knowledge of many subjects; a readiness to receive any idea; energy joined with intelligence; boundless love for the people; confidence that the people can be enabled to live; and the conviction that the country’s resources and order can be renewed so that all may live without deceiving or exploiting others, without subduing or ruling them, and without being enslaved. They need to understand that it is no error for the old to pass away and the new to enter.

A block of stone is given to a sculptor as the raw material for his work. What he next needs is a small chisel and a great heart. Likewise, in independent India, young people need reason and a sense of self-respect in order to gain a new life, a full life.

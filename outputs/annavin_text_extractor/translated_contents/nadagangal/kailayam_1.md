இந்தச் சுயமரியாதைக்காரா கொஞ்சம் தொந்தரவு தருகிறா

## English Translation

A hall in an upper-storey house. A wealthy man is there, along with Veerasami, who regularly works for him. The rich man's name is Masilamani. The scene consists of their conversation. Their speech is colloquial, but it offers many new ideas worth seeing. Brother, read it and stage it, so that reason may be seen!

**Masilamani:** Hey, Veerasami! Why was the maid inside wiping her eyes?

**Veerasami:** I scolded her a little, sir.

**Masilamani:** Why?

**Veerasami:** Look, master. I drew water for my bath. She washed her hands in it. Her hands were dirty, and by washing them there she polluted my bathing water.

**Masilamani:** Is that why you became angry, you fool? Last year you lied to me that you were going to Thanjavur and went to Kumbakonam instead, didn't you?

**Veerasami:** I did, sir.

**Masilamani:** Why? Did you bathe in rosewater? You rolled in mud like a buffalo!

**Veerasami:** It was the Mahamaham, sir.

**Masilamani:** Yes, the Mahamaham. But what was the water like? Mud, wasn't it?

**Veerasami:** Yes, sir.

**Masilamani:** Was it clean?

Was it more polluted than the water in which she washed her hands?

**Veerasami:** She washed dirty hands in it.

**Masilamani:** You witless man! You say her dirty hands polluted the water. Where was this intelligence that day?

**Veerasami:** I wasn't alone, sir. There were many people.

**Masilamani:** Should I go and question all of them? Why did *you* roll in that mud?

**Veerasami:** I did not know any better.

**Masilamani:** Thousands upon thousands plunged into that tank, which had little water. When so many people entered it, wouldn't it be churned into mud?

**Veerasami:** Yes, sir.

**Masilamani:** What do you call a man who goes to bathe and instead coats himself in mud?

**Veerasami:** We must call him ignorant.

**Masilamani:** Say it plainly, fool! You have enough sense to say that the maid's dirty hands polluted the water. Do you know the story of the Mahamaham?

**Veerasami:** Yes, sir. Once every twelve years people go to wash away their sins.

**Masilamani:** To settle the old account and begin preparing a fresh one? Isn't that so? I didn't ask that. I asked the Mahamaham story.

**Veerasami:** They say bathing there brings merit.

**Masilamani:** We cannot clearly see whether merit comes or not. But cholera comes to town, and that is plain enough. People carrying every imaginable disease have plunged into that tank and risen from it. They go and bathe there, and call it merit. What intelligence!

[A prominent local man enters and sits down, saying, “Sadasivam! Sambho! Mahadeva!” He wears sacred ash in thick bands and carries several books. Masilamani looks at him and then at Veerasami.]

**Masilamani:** Hey, Veerasami! Who is that?

**Visitor:** Namaskaram. I am from this town. My name is “Ornament of Oratory” Brahmananda Murthi Swamigal.

**Masilamani:** What brings you here?

**Visitor:** Where else could I go? Are you not the person who must support and save people like us?

**Masilamani:** Veerasami, ask why he has come and what the occasion is, give him an answer, and send him away.

**Visitor:** What would he know? I intend to give Purana discourses in this town. You must support them.

**Masilamani:** So now you have begun setting out on your own? The days when people sent for you are gone, and now you come uninvited? Very well. The market must have become terribly dull.

**Visitor:** In one sense, that is the situation. But I have discovered some new truths and wonderful matters in the Puranas. These Self-Respecters in town are causing some trouble now, you see. Even they will be unable to find fault with my discourse. It will please everyone without hurting anyone's feelings.

**Masilamani:** Say that it will be slippery like okra. That is what you mean, isn't it? This is for money, surely?

**Visitor:** Not for money. Siva, Siva! What use is money? Who values it? Has it not been said, “Even an eyeless needle will not accompany one on the final road”?

**Masilamani:** Yes, an eyeless needle certainly will not go to the market. Who would buy a needle with a broken eye? Get up and go.

[The Purana preacher rises and leaves. Masilamani then addresses his servant.]

**Masilamani:** These fellows have become a great nuisance. They have no other livelihood, so they go about lying like this and ruining the town.

**Veerasami:** They say that reading or hearing a Purana will take one to Kailasa.

**Masilamani:** Kailasa indeed! Hey, Veerasami, even a donkey has gone to Kailasa. Do you think I speak outrageously? I tell you the truth. There is a Purana like that.

**Veerasami:** A donkey went to Kailasa?

**Masilamani:** Yes. There is a sacred story that a donkey and a bull both went there. An Adi Dravidar woman lived by cutting grass.

**Veerasami:** Yes.

**Masilamani:** What else could an Adi Dravidar woman do to live? Was she the daughter of an Iyer household, able to dance and win medals? Her custom was to cut grass for her livelihood. One day she piled grass beside a Pillaiyar temple. Who did?

**Veerasami:** That woman.

**Masilamani:** Then listen. When a donkey and a bull began eating the grass, the woman beat and chased both animals. Which ones?

**Veerasami:** The donkey and the bull.

**Masilamani:** The donkey and bull began running around and around the temple.

**Veerasami:** To escape the beating.

**Masilamani:** They could not bear the beating, but did not want to abandon the grass. As they ran, the grass fell little by little.

**Veerasami:** Because of the speed at which they ran.

**Masilamani:** Yes. That was all that happened. At once the hosts of Siva came and took them away, saying the donkey and bull had circumambulated the Pillaiyar temple and worshipped with sacred arugam grass.

**Veerasami:** They came, and then?

**Masilamani:** What else? Devotees build temples, perform consecrations and chariot festivals, conduct divine worship, and where do they go? To Kailasa. They took this donkey along to that same Kailasa.

**Veerasami:** Master, who wrote a story like this? It is terribly foolish.

**Masilamani:** Ask anyone who reads the Puranas whether such a story exists. And that is not all. One man, seized by uncontrollable lust, did something to his own mother...

**Veerasami:** Did he kill her?

**Masilamani:** Even that would have been less terrible. He raped his own mother.

**Veerasami:** Who was that sinful fellow? Where is he?

**Masilamani:** He is now in Kailasa. Listen. After violating his mother, he thought his father would become an obstacle and killed him too.

**Veerasami:** He took his own mother as a woman and killed his own father! Shouldn't he have been cut to pieces and rolled in a barrel of thorns?

**Masilamani:** You or I would give such a criminal the punishment he deserved. But listen to what happened. The Lord forgave him. That great sinner became a man of merit and reached Kailasa.

**Veerasami:** Is that really true? I cannot believe it.

**Masilamani:** The *Tiruvilaiyadal Puranam* has a chapter called “The Absolution of the Great Sin.” Ask about it.

**Veerasami:** How could the deity approve and admit such a great sinner to Kailasa?

**Masilamani:** Do you know what caste he belonged to? He was an Iyer, an Iyer! Do you imagine Kailasa has nobody like him? There are tongue-cutters, nose-cutters, head-cutters and plenty of such killers there. If questioned, they will say it was done out of devotion. Do not think I tell you something nonexistent.

A man went to the temple at Tiruvarur with his wife. A flower lay inside the temple. Do women not love flowers?

**Veerasami:** Yes.

**Masilamani:** The lady saw the flower, picked it up and smelled it. Her husband was elsewhere, engaged in devotion.

**Veerasami:** Very well. What happened next?

**Masilamani:** Another devotee in the temple saw the woman pick up and smell the flower, and came running.

**Veerasami:** And then?

**Masilamani:** You tell me. What would an honourable man do?

**Veerasami:** He would say, “Lady, do not take that flower; it belongs to the deity.”

**Masilamani:** If he were rather rough, he might abuse her. Do you know what this man did? He ran inside and brought a sickle.

**Veerasami:** He brought the sickle and...

**Masilamani:** Cut off the woman's nose.

**Veerasami:** You sinner! For picking up and smelling a flower, he cut off another man's wife's nose? What injustice! What did her husband do? Even a man without sensitivity would grow furious.

**Masilamani:** Listen, Veerasami, to that performance too. Her husband ran over and asked what had happened. The nose-cutter told him. At once the husband seized the sickle.

**Veerasami:** And struck the fellow upon the head?

**Masilamani:** No. He struck a hand.

**Veerasami:** That was proper. It was with that hand that the sinner cut her nose. The hand deserved to be severed.

**Masilamani:** Fool! Whose hand do you imagine he cut? He cut off his own wife's hand, saying that the hand which took the flower must be severed.

**Veerasami:** Shameless, unjust man!

**Masilamani:** Do not scold them. Both are Nayanmars. Do you understand now? The man who cut the nose dwells in Kailasa, and the man who cut the hand is there too. Devotion, my boy, devotion.

**Veerasami:** Is this devotion? Is cutting off a nose and one's wife's hand devotion?

**Masilamani:** The nose-cutter's name is Seruthunai; the hand-cutter is Kalarsinga. I am not conducting a religious recital. Ask those people if you wish whether this is in the *Periyapuranam*. People of this kind have all gone to Kailasa. Why should we go there?

**Veerasami:** Master, I had imagined something else about Kailasa. Only now do I understand. Is it a place where people like these live? We do not want such a place, master.

Younger brother, this is only a very ordinary part of the effort to spread rationalism in the country. Many learned people continue to draw pictures of quality that bring clarity to thought.

Not only does reason spread; as the grip of the Puranas diminishes, interest in Tamil literature grows. People who refused to know the *Tirukkural* ten years ago today discover new meaning in it.

1959.

## Translator's Notes

Images 1-3 and 5 were recovered from transparent-background scans after the original OCR pass treated them as blank. The Mahamaham is the twelve-yearly temple-tank festival at Kumbakonam. The preacher's `காதற்ற ஊசியும் வாராது காண் கடை வழிக்கே` invokes Pattinathar's familiar warning that worldly possessions do not follow one in death; Masilamani deliberately twists `கடை வழி` into an ordinary marketplace and rejects a needle with a broken eye as unsaleable. The “great sin” episode is identified in the source as `மாபாதகம் தீர்த்த படலம்` of the *Tiruvilaiyadal Puranam*. Seruthunai and Kalarsinga are named in the *Periyapuranam*. Derogatory caste and gendered language is preserved as part of the historical satire and does not express translator endorsement.

## English Translation

**Place:** A magistrate's courtroom.

**Present:** The Magistrate; counsel for the complainant; Sivanesan, the accused; Manickam Chettiar, the complainant; Muthusami, a temple priest and witness; Irulandi, another priest and witness; a court attendant; several lawyers; and townspeople.

**Situation:** Counsel for the complainant, Manickam Chettiar, has offered many arguments to prove Sivanesan guilty and is making his final summation.

**Counsel:** This man was a clerk in Manickavasagam Chettiar's wholesale shop. Chettiar placed extraordinary trust in him. Yet this man, who enjoyed that trust, stole ten viss of camphor from Chettiar's shop and carried it away. That is his offence.

**Magistrate:** (Looking at the accused.) What is your name?

**Accused:** Sivanesan.

**Magistrate:** Do you admit committing the offence?

**Accused:** But I have committed no offence.

**Magistrate:** You heard counsel explain the case, did you not?

**Accused:** I was listening.

(Counsel rises and indicates that he will question the accused.)

**Counsel:** This is a court, a hall of justice.

**Accused:** I stand here trusting that I shall receive justice.

**Counsel:** No impertinence. You were a clerk in wholesaler Manickam Chettiar's shop, were you not?

**Accused:** I was.

**Counsel:** What happened last Friday?

**Accused:** I heard there was a music concert at your house.

**Counsel:** Do not be cheeky. No one asked about affairs at my house. What happened in Chettiar's shop?

**Accused:** Is that what you are asking? Had you asked that clearly at the outset, why would I speak about your house?

**Counsel:** Very well. What happened at Chettiar's shop last Friday?

**Accused:** Business.

**Counsel:** Nonsense! This is a court; there is no place here for clowns.

**Accused:** Your Honour, I merely answered the learned counsel's question. He gets terribly angry and...

**Magistrate:** (To counsel.) Come to the point.

**Counsel:** Yes, Your Honour. Sivanesan, did you steal ten viss of camphor from Chettiar's shop last Friday?

**Accused:** I did not steal it. Chettiar gave me those ten viss and told me to take them to wholesaler Madhava Mudaliar and bring back horse gram in exchange.

**Counsel:** At least your account is candid. Did you deliver it as Chettiar instructed?

(Counsel sits.)

**Magistrate:** Do you have a lawyer?

**Accused:** No.

**Accused:** What can I do if great men such as yourself misunderstand it?

**Magistrate:** Do not put on an act. Is it true that you carried the camphor out of the shop?

**Accused:** Yes.

**Magistrate:** And that you did not deliver it where required? Is that also true?

**Accused:** Yes.

**Magistrate:** What more is needed to punish you? Very well, what defence do you offer?

**Accused:** I have one or two witnesses whom I ask to be examined.

**Magistrate:** Who? Give the name.

**Accused:** My first witness is Muthusami, priest of the corner temple.

(The court attendant calls Muthusami.)

**Magistrate:** Name?

**Muthu:** Priest Muthu.

**Accused:** Muthu, did I come to the temple for the evening worship last Friday?

**Muthu:** You worshipped Pillaiyar.

**Accused:** What else of note happened?

**Muthu:** All at once, ten viss of camphor were burned.

**Accused:** That is all, Muthu.

(Muthu starts to leave. Counsel stops him.)

**Counsel:** Did this man burn the ten viss of camphor?

**Muthu:** No, sir. He gave it to me; I burned it.

**Counsel:** Did you know it was stolen property?

**Muthu:** How could I know? How can I tell whether offerings brought to a temple are honestly acquired or stolen?

**Counsel:** Did you not stop him?

**Muthu:** It would be a sin to stop worship!

**Counsel:** Enough! No talk here of sin and merit.

**Muthu:** Are sin and merit forbidden here too? I did not know, sir.

(The priest leaves.)

**Accused:** My second witness is Irulandi.

(The attendant calls Irulandi, who enters.)

**Accused:** Irulandi, were you present last Friday when the holy man beneath the banyan tree preached?

**Irulandi:** Yes, sir. You too were there.

**Accused:** What did he say to me?

**Irulandi:** He said, “Ignorant fellow! Why have you not yet devoted yourself to service? Worship the lord of the corner temple. Offer him everything.”

**Accused:** What did I do?

**Irulandi:** You appeared entranced and repeatedly fell down in worship.

**Accused:** Then?

**Irulandi:** The holy man gave you sacred ash and you applied it.

**Accused:** After that?

**Irulandi:** You picked up the basket and ran.

**Accused:** Ran where?

**Irulandi:** I ran behind you. You went straight to the corner temple and burned the camphor in the basket. All the devotees fell down in worship.

**Accused:** That is all.

(Irulandi leaves.)

**Accused:** Chettiar is also a witness.

(Chettiar enters.)

**Accused:** Chettiar, was a religious discourse held at your house on Thursday?

**Chettiar:** Yes.

**Accused:** Which story was told?

**Chettiar:** That of the devotee Ramadas.

**Accused:** When it ended, what did you tell me?

**Chettiar:** I said that Ramadas's devotion was devotion in its truest form.

**Accused:** You also said we should all be devotees like him.

**Chettiar:** Yes, I did.

**Accused:** What did the Bhagavathar say then?

**Chettiar:** He said something or other.

**Accused:** Please say it. He remarked that you bore the name of an incomparable Nayanar and that Manickavasagar too was a great devotee, did he not?

**Chettiar:** He did, hoping I would invite him another day to tell that story.

**Accused:** He even gave us the story in brief.

**Chettiar:** Yes, he showed us a sample.

**Accused:** That is all.

(Chettiar leaves.)

**Magistrate:** How does any of this evidence help you?

**Accused:** Whether it helps me or not, what these witnesses said is true.

**Magistrate:** Yes. And therefore?

**Accused:** It should establish that I am not a thief but a devotee. Can devotion itself be punished?

**Magistrate:** What devotion? Is taking property from your workplace an act of devotion?

**Accused:** Ramadas and Manickavasagar stole something like ten viss of gold, did they not?

**Magistrate:** They were devotees.

**Accused:** So am I.

**Magistrate:** They were divine incarnations.

**Accused:** And I alone am not?

**Magistrate:** You are speaking insolently. Be careful, or contempt of court will be added to this offence.

**Accused:** When you yourself insult devotion...

**Magistrate:** Stop!

**Accused:** Sir, I do not know what will happen in this case. I am no thief. When I carried the camphor from Chettiar's shop, I intended to do as he had said. On the way, however, I was captivated by the holy man's words. I first thought it was another person's property and therefore unjust to use it otherwise than its owner directed. Then I remembered the previous evening: the Bhagavathar had said that the devotee Ramadas spending the Padshah's tax revenue was righteous, just and proof of the greatness of devotion; my employer had approved; and the thousands assembled there had cried, “Rama! Rama!” I immediately became convinced that devotion stood above the question of whether the camphor belonged to Chettiar. I burned it for Vinayaka. If Ramadas and Manickavasagar were thieves, I too am a thief.

**Magistrate:** However high a sparrow flies, can it become a hawk? Can you become Ramadas?

**Accused:** No. But a sparrow too is a bird. Just as both sparrow and hawk are birds, Ramadas and I belong to the class of devotees.

**Magistrate:** Enough of this pretence. You cannot escape by invoking old stories.

**Accused:** Are the stories of those devotees unsuitable for a court of justice?

**Magistrate:** This is a court. Cases are tried here according to law.

**Accused:** Is there no relationship between justice and law?

**Magistrate:** Enough chatter. Must you do something merely because Ramadas did it? What devotion Ramadas possessed! You...?

**Accused:** My devotion is not one whit less. Does your disbelief diminish it?

**Magistrate:** I do not care whether you are a devotee. You stand before this court as a thief.

**Accused:** True, just as Ramadas and Manickavasagar once stood accused.

**Magistrate:** Do not return to that old story. Those were different times. The justice of that age...

**Accused:** Is it right to say that? Does society today not still believe what people of that age called justice? Mothers then believed that circling a sacred fig tree would bring pregnancy; they still circle it today. I myself once saw your wife doing so.

(Laughter in court.)

Do not ridicule devotion. Our magistrate is learned and intelligent. If circling the tree were laughable, would he not have stopped his wife? I have seen the wives of many eminent officials at that tree.

**Magistrate:** Enough of your mythological sermon.

**Accused:** Sir, had those who govern ordered an end to the mythological sermons preached throughout the country, just as you order me to stop, I would not stand accused as a thief before you today.

**Magistrate:** Who told you to steal?

**Accused:** The feeling called devotion.

**Magistrate:** Does it tell you even to steal?

**Accused:** I, a poor devotee, stole camphor. Thirumangai Alvar committed highway robbery for devotion.

**Counsel:** Anyone doing that today would be punished under Section 395.

**Accused:** If the justice of that age does not apply today, those who govern must prohibit the teaching of old myths that preach it and declare such teaching unlawful. Mythological propaganda freely continues through song and theatre. I therefore believed that the government which permits it still honours the justice of former times.

**Magistrate:** That is your madness.

**Accused:** You call me mad for this alone. Yet the neem tree of that age still has more authority than you do, and Garuda and the monkey remain gods as before. How then can you say that the justice of that age differs from today's? If its justice is unacceptable, the mythological preaching that makes people believe it must cease.

(Turning to the court.)

You think I am babbling. Last month Padma, who lived next door to me, received seven years' rigorous imprisonment. She threw her child into a well and was caught when she tried to jump in and die too. Padma was poor, too poor for her child and herself to live. Many times she had heard the story of Nallathangal and seen it performed. When poverty became unbearable, she decided that Nallathangal too, praised as a chaste and noble woman, had thrown seven children into a well and died, and that she should do likewise. They sentenced Padma to seven years. Padma killed her own child, sir, but mythology incited that killing and society conspired in and abetted it. Our lords of righteousness never thought to punish them.

**Magistrate:** Do not speak in circles. Be brief and finish.

**Accused:** I am not a thief but a devotee. The mythological preacher, the holy man, and Chettiar who arranged the discourse made me believe that those driven by devotion are forgiven whatever they do, even theft, and attain greatness both here and in the next world. They are guilty of inciting my offence. Punish them together with me. If justice requires that they are innocent, then I too am innocent.

(The court adjourns.)

(The Magistrate's chamber. The Magistrate and counsel.)

**Magistrate:** Sir, there is justice in what he said. Countless atrocities have truly occurred in the name of devotion, yet for some reason their perpetrators are celebrated as devotees. As he said, how was it righteous or just for Manickavasagar and Ramadas to take another's property and spend it to display their devotion? Yet we search for a penal section for the man who stole this camphor.

**Counsel:** All manner of things occurred in the name of devotion in olden times. What can we do?

**Magistrate:** The curses of those old times still possess our people. When he said that the sacred fig tree was circled at my house, I could not bear the shame.

**Counsel:** That is trouble caused by women. What can we do?

**Magistrate:** We cannot dismiss it that way. You know my uncle, Sir Ramarajan, whom everyone praises as London-returned, barrister and minister. Luckily that fellow appears not to know what he does, or he would have ruined our honour.

**Counsel:** What did Sir Ramaraj do?

**Magistrate:** Why ask about that disgrace? He goes to the Palani Murugan temple and carries a silver torch. A silver torch! They call that devotion.

**Counsel:** Sir Ramaraj?

**Magistrate:** The very man. If so great an intellectual can act like that in the name of devotion, what is so extraordinary or wrong about this fellow burning camphor?

**Counsel:** But there is the law.

**Magistrate:** Yes, piled like a mountain upon the table. Yet when I think of what occurs in this country in the name of religion, his act hardly seems an offence. A holy man dear to Sir Ramaraj recites the Puranas. He tells of a Nayanar whose wife smelled a flower reserved for God. Enraged that she had smelled a flower meant for the Lord, the man cut off her nose.

**Counsel:** He sounds a complete fool.

**Magistrate:** Leave aside his foolishness. Consider the brutality. If that case came before our court...

**Counsel:** Punishment under Section 326 would be certain.

**Magistrate:** Yet Sir Ramaraj celebrates the story of such a madman. Why did he need an Oxford University education?

**Counsel:** Still, olden times held admirable ideas about father and son, elder and younger brother, and teacher and pupil. Take Ekalavya. He did not learn archery directly from Dronacharya, but made an image of him, treated it as his teacher and learned. When Dronacharya demanded his teacher's offering, Ekalavya cut off his own thumb out of reverence. See how nobly the teacher-pupil relationship was formed!

**Magistrate:** (Laughing.) Let that rest. You cite one story to show its wonder, but another shows its distortion. Brihaspati is the teacher and Chandra the pupil; Tara, the teacher's wife, makes love to the pupil who came to study, and he consents. Is that not obscene?

**Counsel:** True. There are stories of both kinds. Bharata, who refused to rule the kingdom belonging to his elder brother and said Rama's sandals alone should rule, exemplifies a younger brother's love.

**Magistrate:** And Sugriva, who had Rama shoot Vali from concealment and then enjoyed his kingdom, also lived in that age.

**Counsel:** Yes, that too is true.

**Magistrate:** Even according to the Puranas, then, it is hardly true that the old age was some glorious holy era inhabited by supremely noble people. Between ourselves, how did the Pandavas take Draupadi as wife to all five and enjoy her? How did that woman agree to such a cruel arrangement? Shameful even to say aloud. As that man said, propaganda for such old rubbish must henceforth be stopped.

**Counsel:** What can we do?

**Magistrate:** Why can we do nothing? The educated are few and the uneducated many. The uneducated think it right to follow people like us. If we too roll in the old mire, the uneducated crowd says, “Even so learned a man rolls in it,” and falls into the pool en masse. To reform these people, we must first reform ourselves.

**Counsel:** Yes.

**Magistrate:** For example, you do not need sacred ash displaying a useless caste-religious mark, and I do not need this namam.

(Both erase their sectarian marks.)

**Magistrate:** That alone is insufficient. We must all speak against these old arrangements.

**Counsel:** We certainly must.

**Magistrate:** One more thing. Tell Chettiar to withdraw that case...

**Counsel:** I shall tell him.

*18 August 1946*

## Translator's Notes

“வழக்கு வாபஸ்” is rendered “Case Withdrawn”; “வாபஸ்,” from Hindustani *wapas*, was common colloquial usage for withdrawal or taking back.

A “vis” or “viss” was a historical South Indian unit of weight. The source's “பத்து வீசை” is therefore retained as “ten viss” rather than converted without a place-specific standard.

The play cites Sections 395 (dacoity) and 326 (causing grievous hurt with a dangerous weapon) in its satire. The legal numbers are retained as printed, without treating the dialogue as legal advice.

Ramadas, Manickavasagar, Thirumangai Alvar, Nallathangal, Ekalavya, Tara, Bharata and Sugriva are invoked as popularly narrated mythic or devotional exemplars. The translation preserves Anna's polemical use of those stories rather than adjudicating their variant traditions.

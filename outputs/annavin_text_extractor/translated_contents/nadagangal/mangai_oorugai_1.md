## English Translation

## On the Road

**Present:** Dikshitar and, later, Muniyan.

**Situation:** Dikshitar is over fifty. He walks carrying on his head a sack too heavy for him. Several passers-by look and mutter. He proceeds hoping to find someone who will carry it for him.

Muniyan is a rural labourer known to Dikshitar. Having sold a bundle of firewood, he approaches from the opposite direction while counting the money and knotting it into his upper cloth.

Seeing him at a distance, Dikshitar rejoices that the right man has appeared. When Muniyan arrives...

**Dikshitar:** [Exhausted] Who is that, Muniyan? Look, my karma has not left me even at this failing age. Can I possibly walk in this heat carrying this sack? Give me a little help and lower it. Let us rest for a while.

**Muniyan:** Alas! Why did you set out in this blazing heat, Swami, carrying a sack too? Wait, wait. Here. [Lowering it] Could you manage it? It is very heavy.

**Dikshitar:** Muniyan, will you carry this as far as my house? Do not think, “The moment the Iyer saw me on the road, he put me to work.”

**Muniyan:** Why say such a thing? What would wear away if I did you this small help?

**Dikshitar:** You are golden. Some people would imagine that their dignity was diminished.

**Muniyan:** You walk ahead, Swami. I shall carry it.

**Dikshitar:** Blessed man! Benefactor! Righteous man! Come, come!

## Dikshitar's House

**Present:** Agilandam and, later, Dikshitar and Muniyan.

**Situation:** Muniyan lowers the sack onto the street veranda, takes leave of Dikshitar, and departs. Dikshitar carries the sack inside from the veranda. Agilandam runs from the kitchen and helps him.

**Agilandam:** Karma, karma! Can you carry a sack like this? The sun is blazing like fire.

**Dikshitar:** [Wiping his face] Weren't you tormenting me for mangoes to make pickle? Look, each is like a sovereign. Fine firm green mangoes.

**Agilandam:** Leave that aside. Who told you to carry them yourself? If you strain your neck, who must suffer caring for you?

**Dikshitar:** Be quiet, woman! Did I carry them? You know that fool Muniyan.

**Agilandam:** Who, the villager?

**Dikshitar:** That blockhead carried them.

**Agilandam:** Very nice. You make him work and then abuse him as a fool.

**Dikshitar:** Would I say it before him? To his face I called him benefactor, righteous man and blessed soul. With those kind words I got him to carry this on his head.

**Agilandam:** Fine mangoes! Fit for pickling.

**Dikshitar:** I shall go immediately and ask Khader Sahib for salt. Just yesterday evening I warmly enquired after his health, so the ground is prepared. Bolt the door. I shall return at once.

## The Mirasdar's Bungalow

**Present:** Mirasdar Kanakasabai Pillai and Kathan.

**Situation:** Kanakasabai is extremely angry. Sitting in a reclining chair, he speaks while twisting his moustache. A large tamarind switch lies beside him. Kathan stands opposite with his upper cloth tied around his waist. Seeing Kathan stand in fear, the mirasdar takes some pleasure and speaks loudly in a threatening manner.

**Kanakasabai:** I shall skin you alive. Tell me the truth. How much money did you take from that Brahmin?

**Kathan:** I did not take even a copper coin, sir. I swear.

**Kanakasabai:** Here is Lord Truth himself! You donkey! Tell me the truth. You plucked and gave him a sack of mangoes. Would you have done it without taking even one rupee?

**Kathan:** I swear by God, master. I did not pluck them for him. Dikshitar picked up the ones that had fallen below.

**Kanakasabai:** The mangoes fell by themselves? Did Dikshitar make them fall by magic? What story are you spinning?

**Kathan:** I struck the tree with a stone, sir. The mangoes fell down.

**Kanakasabai:** You struck them down with stones? Whose father's property is it? If the mangoes grow in Pillai's garden, do they belong to every passer-by? Tell me why you knocked them down.

**Kathan:** I was not knocking down mangoes. Dikshitar said, “There is a green snake on the topmost branch. Aim a stone and hit it, or it may strike somebody's eye.”

**Kanakasabai:** What story are you telling?

**Kathan:** It is no story. The Iyer Swami really said it. He said, “Look there, Kathan.” My eyes could not see clearly. Thinking the Swami must be right, I threw ten or twenty stones. Later he said, “The green snake is clinging among the leaves, Kathan. It cannot be hit now.” Meanwhile, the stones I threw at the snake scattered the mangoes to the ground. Dikshitar said, “Kathan, I shall take home what would otherwise go to waste.” I said all right.

**Kanakasabai:** So you performed charity! How many mangoes were there?

**Kathan:** Perhaps fifty or a hundred.

**Kanakasabai:** Fine. One and a half rupees will be deducted from your wages. Go.

**Kathan:** Master, I have children.

**Kanakasabai:** His lordship performs charity! Fool, when that Brahmin pointed out a green snake, did you not have enough thought to ask whether it was true? You threw stones! Knowing your stupidity, he invented a snake and arranged a way to obtain the mangoes.

**Kathan:** Yes, master. Only now do I understand.

**Kanakasabai:** Idiot! Go and drag the Iyer here.

## On the Road

**Present:** Dikshitar and Singupattar.

**Situation:** Singupattar approaches carrying a bundle of banana leaves. He sees Dikshitar coming quickly from the opposite direction.

**Singupattar:** Dikshitar, they say you carried some great sack home.

**Dikshitar:** Good heavens! Has it reached your ears already? Pattarachari, were you born on earth solely to learn what happens everywhere?

**Singupattar:** Leave that aside. Why change the subject? What sack? Tell me.

**Dikshitar:** Gold, sir, all pure gold!

**Singupattar:** We yearn even to find gold. What can be done? This is not the age for it. In the time of kings and emperors, our people received not merely gold but nine precious gems. What use remembering it? Tell me what was in the sack.

**Dikshitar:** Mangoes, Pattarachari Swami. Ordinary mangoes.

**Singupattar:** Where did you get them?

**Dikshitar:** At your mother-in-law's house. Must you probe where, how and why they came?

**Singupattar:** What happens if you tell me? Will the mangoes rot, or will your mouth ache?

**Dikshitar:** You will not let it go. They came from our Kanakasabai's garden.

**Singupattar:** Mangoes from Pillai's garden? They must be excellent. Is Pillai in town?

**Dikshitar:** He was. I do not know about now.

## The Salt Market

**Present:** Sultan and Dikshitar.

**Situation:** Sultan sits in the shop with one leg crossed over the other, absorbed in an old cinema magazine. Dikshitar enters.

**Dikshitar:** You are Khader Sahib's younger brother, aren't you?

**Sultan:** Yes, Iyer. Why are you looking for my elder brother?

**Dikshitar:** If I seek your brother, what is that to you? Whether one receives Ramachandra's grace or Lakshmana's grace, it is all the same.

**Sultan:** Luckily you stopped at Lakshmana without turning me into Hanuman. We have no time at the market this early for a Ramayana recital. Tell me the matter.

**Dikshitar:** Your father earned a splendid name as “Abdullah of the Salt Market.” Khader too is a golden man. Now we must see what sort of heart you possess.

**Sultan:** That is a very elaborate preface.

**Dikshitar:** It is nothing, Sultan. Our Kanakasabai, you know...

**Sultan:** The man who runs the sugar mill?

**Dikshitar:** Yes. Do you know who chose the auspicious time for that mill? I did. He has reverence and affection for me. He gave me fifty or a hundred mangoes to use for pickle.

**Sultan:** You need salt for them. Is that it?

**Dikshitar:** Clever Sultan! You understood while I was still speaking. You have more subtlety and keener intelligence than Khader.

**Sultan:** Enough, Iyer. I have no time for more talk. One measure will be sufficient, won't it? Our shop boy will bring it this evening. Go.

**Dikshitar:** Does this require a servant? Have him put it in this cloth. I shall take it. My dignity will not diminish.

[Kathan enters.]

**Kathan:** Dikshitar Swami, come. The master ordered me to bring you with me at once.

**Dikshitar:** Whom, me?

**Kathan:** No, no, your grandfather. Come, sir.

## Kanakasabai's Bungalow

**Present:** Kanakasabai, Dikshitar and Kathan.

**Situation:** Kathan brings Dikshitar before the mirasdar and leaves. Kanakasabai glares at Dikshitar while twisting his moustache. With a foolish smile, Dikshitar sits in an old chair. Kanakasabai angrily begins.

**Kanakasabai:** Was there a green snake in the tree? It ignored the neem, babul and peepal trees, chose the mango tree, climbed it, and told you so, did it?

**Dikshitar:** Pillai, why speak with such anger? What wrong have I done?

**Kanakasabai:** Bundle up all your cunning speech and put it away. You found a gullible fellow, told him there was a green snake in the mango tree and to stone it, then bundled up the fallen mangoes and left. Is that proper conduct? A green snake indeed! Did you see it with your eyes?

**Dikshitar:** It is the influence of the planets, Pillai. My unlucky hour has made you angry. I am old and my sight is dim. For two years I have thought of getting spectacles, but it has not happened. To my eyes it looked as though a green snake lay coiled there. I told Kathan, “Look there, my boy. It resembles a green snake.” He threw four stones and ten mangoes fell.

**Kanakasabai:** Was there a green snake?

**Dikshitar:** If you question me like a court, what answer can I give? To my eyes it looked like a green snake. At my age nothing is understood clearly. A similar mishap occurred yesterday evening. You know our Amsa. Thinking she was approaching, I went up to some Gujarati woman and asked if she was well.

**Kanakasabai:** Did that girl look exactly like Amsa?

**Dikshitar:** The same height, complexion and gait. She looked just like Amsa. Only when I went near did I discover it was someone else. But she did not have Amsa's beautiful eyes...

**Kanakasabai:** You have discovered some great beauty. Beautiful eyes indeed! Blind eyes!

**Dikshitar:** Do not say that. Some poisonous moment set you and her against each other. Does that make Amsa unbeautiful?

**Kanakasabai:** Is beauty enough? Should there not be affection? That is what matters.

**Dikshitar:** Without doubt. But do you say she has no affection? I tell you it was an unlucky moment. In truth, she holds you dearer than life, Pillai. Look, in my unfortunate hour I took these mangoes home. When she heard of it, she had come to our house. She fired questions one after another: “Did you see him? How is he? Has he grown thin?”

**Kanakasabai:** She is certainly clever in speech.

**Dikshitar:** I too casually threw in a line: “Pillai told me to give these mangoes especially to you.”

**Kanakasabai:** What did she say? What did she say?

**Dikshitar:** You know how, in the *Ramayana*, Hanuman gave Sita in the Ashoka grove Sri Ramachandra's signet ring and tears of joy flowed from Janaki's eyes? She became just like that. “How could I receive such a blessing, Swami? God is not on my side,” she began lamenting.

I said, “Do not cry, Amsa. I shall go and speak to him myself.” I was thinking of coming here tomorrow, but before that Kathan arrived.

**Kanakasabai:** Is that so? Very well, very well. What is in this sack?

**Dikshitar:** Salt. Khader Sahib at our salt market said he would provide the ingredients for pickling. He had given me the salt, and just as I was about to pay for the chillies, asafoetida and the rest, Kathan arrived.

**Kanakasabai:** What of it? How much will it cost? Will three rupees be enough?

**Dikshitar:** The woman at my house is extravagantly demanding. She says it will cost five rupees.

Pillai, one request. Do not become angry.

**Kanakasabai:** What is it? Say it freely.

**Dikshitar:** When the pickle is ready, half of it must go to Amsa.

**Kanakasabai:** Give it. Who says no?

**Dikshitar:** Ah, should I give it? She will accept it only if you yourself carry it to her.

**Kanakasabai:** You are a clever fellow. Very well, we shall see. Hey, Kathan! Pluck another hundred mangoes for the Iyer. The pickle must be magnificent.

**Dikshitar:** Do I not know, Pillai? You will see Amsa herself say that it is magnificent.

*Dravida Nadu*, 14 August 1955.

## Translator's Notes

Images 1-2 and 4-8 were recovered from transparent-background scans after the original OCR pass treated them as blank. The printed title confirms `மாங்காய் ஊறுகாய்`, correcting the shortened filename `mangai_oorugai`. `பச்சரிசி மாங்காய்` describes firm green mangoes suitable for pickling. The source does not define Amsa's exact relationship to Kanakasabai; the translation therefore preserves only the estrangement and affection stated in their reported conversation. Dikshitar's caste-coded flattery, his manipulation of labourers, and Kathan's wage deduction are retained as central elements of the satire.

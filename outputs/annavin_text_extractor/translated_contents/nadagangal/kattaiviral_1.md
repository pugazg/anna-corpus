கட்டைவிரல்

பார்த்திபனின் தொல்லை தாங்க முடியவில்லை துரோ
ணாச்சாரியாரால். “கேவலம் ஒரு வேடன்! அவன் அறிந்திருக்
கிறான். தாங்கள் எனக்காக மட்டும் என்று கற்றுக் கொடுத்த
அபூர்வமான அஸ்திர வித்தையை. நம்பினேன், மோசம்
போனேன்” என்று கோபிக்கிறான். குரு, சீட சம்பந்தமே முறிந்து
விடக் கூடுமோ என்று சந்தேகிக்க வேண்டிய நிலைமை. “வா!
போய் அந்த வேடனைக் கண்டு...” என்று அழைத்தார்
துரோணாச்சாரியார். “கண்டு காலிலே வீழ்ந்து...” என்று
கேலி பேசலானான் அர்ஜுனன். “ஏகலைவனிடம் இருக்கும்
வித்தையைப் போக்கி விடுகிறேன், வா” என்று உறுதியுடன்
கூறினார். இருவரும் சென்றனர்.

வேடன், நெடுந்தூரத்தில் இருந்தபடியே, துரோணரைக்
கண்டுவிட்டான். அர்ஜுனன் பின் தங்கிவிட்டான்; குரு மட்டும்
வேகமாக முன்னால் சென்றார். ஏகலைவன் அவர் அடி
தொழுதான். குரு மேலும் நடந்தார். அவன் பக்தியுடன் பின்
தொடர்ந்தான். மெளனமாகச் சென்றார் துரோணர். ஏகலைவன்
“குருதேவா! குருதேவா!” என்று அர்ச்சித்தபடி நடந்தான்.
அர்ஜுனன் கண்களுக்கு இரண்டு நிழல் உருவங்களே தெரிந்தன;
அவ்வளவு தொலைவு சென்றனர் இருவரும்.

மீண்டும் ஒருமுறை ஏகலைவன், துரோணரின் பாதத்தில்
வீழ்ந்து எழுந்தான். மோன நிலையிலிருந்து மீண்டவர்போல,
துரோணாச்சாரியார் அவனை ஏறிட்டுப் பார்த்தார். இருவருக்குள்
உரையாடல் நடந்தது.

“குருதேவா! அடியேன் ஏகலைவன் நமஸ்கரிக்கிறேன்.”

“எழுந்திரு அப்பா! என்னிடம் நீ பாடம் கேட்டதில்லையே.”

“அந்தப் பாக்கியம் எனக்குக் கிட்டவில்லை, ஸ்வாமி!
ஆனால், என் இருதயத்திலே தங்களையே குருதேவராகப்
பிரதிஷ்டை செய்துகொண்டு நான் இந்த வில்வித்தை கற்று
வந்தேன்...”

## English Translation

Arjuna's pestering became unbearable to Dronacharya. “A mere hunter! He has learned the rare science of weapons that you promised to teach only me. I trusted you and have been deceived,” he complained angrily. The guru feared that the bond between teacher and disciple might break. “Come! Let us go and see that hunter...” Dronacharya invited him. “See him and fall at his feet...” Arjuna mocked. “I shall destroy the skill Ekalavya possesses. Come,” the guru declared firmly. The two set out.

The hunter saw Drona while still far away. Arjuna fell behind, and the guru alone walked rapidly ahead. Ekalavya bowed at his feet. The guru continued walking, and Ekalavya followed devoutly. Drona proceeded in silence while Ekalavya repeated, “Gurudeva! Gurudeva!” To Arjuna's eyes they appeared only as two shadowy figures, so far had they gone.

Once again Ekalavya fell at Drona's feet and rose. As if emerging from silent contemplation, Dronacharya looked up at him. A conversation began.

**Ekalavya:** Gurudeva! Your servant Ekalavya offers his salutations.

**Drona:** Rise, my boy. You never took lessons from me.

**Ekalavya:** I did not receive that blessing, Swami. But I installed you as Gurudeva in my heart and learned this archery.

**Drona:** And you have attained extraordinary mastery. Even Arjuna... yes... will be pleased.

**Ekalavya:** It is the grace of your benevolent gaze.

**Drona:** Ah, you have mastered the art of speech too. Your skill is apparent. Now your character must be tested.

**Ekalavya:** I shall be blessed and fulfilled, Gurudeva.

**Drona:** What is this endless “guru, guru,” my boy? I have not instructed you even for a single day, and you have not offered even a small coin as a gift to your guru.

**Ekalavya:** Gurudeva! I stand ready to give an offering. I waited only to receive your darshan. Ekalavya is your servant. Whatever command you issue...

**Drona:** Ready to give, are you? How boldly you speak! Many princes who come to learn from me say the same. When they become kings, I remain merely myself while they are the kings.

**Ekalavya:** I am no prince, Acharya. I am your slave.

**Drona:** Some princes have given what I asked. They possessed the means and gave. Wealth lies at the feet of those who rule countries. But you, poor fellow, wander in the forest...

**Ekalavya:** Yes, I am only a hunter of the forest. But my devotion to my guru exceeds that of rulers. Have not the slightest doubt, Swami!

**Drona:** Ekalavya, I commend your devotion. Live well. I shall take my leave. What need could I have that I must ask you to satisfy?

**Ekalavya:** Must I become a fallen sinner who offered nothing? Lord, you must accept me. Do not think, “What can a mere hunter give?” Though a hunter, I am a warrior who wields bow and arrow. I will not break my word or fail to accomplish what I resolve. Whatever you desire, I shall obtain and give you.

**Drona:** True! Through the benefit of your archery...

**Ekalavya:** Not only that, teacher. My heart is pure.

**Drona:** Is that so? Poor child, you speak without knowing the world. I can ask for an offering that will make even you say, “I cannot.”

**Ekalavya:** I, who bear a bow, will never utter the words “I cannot.”

**Drona:** Do not imagine this is a test of weapons. A test of truth is exceedingly hard. Most people fail it. Not everyone can act according to their word. Do not hastily squander your promise. I shall go.

**Ekalavya:** Swami, I do not know how to argue, nor would that be proper. But whatever offering you ask, I shall give it without fail.

**Drona:** You speak only stubbornly, madman! I can ask for an offering that will leave you staring in terror. Is asking difficult? Is giving not the difficult part?

**Ekalavya:** Ask, Gurudeva. If I fail to give it, none is more base than I.

**Drona:** Oh! You imagine I will ask for a bar of gold, nine precious gems, a golden robe, or something of that kind!

**Ekalavya:** I have not even thought about what you might ask, Swami. My only thought is that people must say, “Gurudeva asked Ekalavya for an offering and received it.”

**Drona:** I warn you one final time. Do not kindle a flame you cannot endure.

**Ekalavya:** I shall not fear to walk the path of truth.

**Drona:** Then I ask my guru's offering, you fool who babbled in ignorance. Listen. Tremble, lament, flee in terror, fall at my feet assailed by shame and grief, and beg forgiveness. Earn the name of a rogue unable to give his guru's offering...

**Ekalavya:** You have still not said what the offering is, Gurudeva.

"Did I not specify it? I shall now. A serpent will leap into your ear, so listen, Ekalavya! As your offering to the guru, cut off and give me the thumb of your right hand."

"Gurudeva..."

"What shall I do? Alas, how can I give it? I would give even all the fourteen worlds, but how can I give my thumb, Swami? Come, hunter-warrior, begin your lament!"

"Swami, I shall return in half a second."

"Are you running away? Splendid, great hero!"

[He runs off and, as promised, comes running back with a water pot in his hand.]

"What is this water pot?"

"To perform ablution upon your feet."

"Why? Will you begin your wailing after the ablution?"

[With the water from the pot, he bathes Drona's feet. In an instant he cuts off his thumb and places it at Drona's feet.]

"Here is the offering, Gurudeva: the thumb of my right hand! Swami, your feet have been stained with blood. I shall clean them with water from the pot."

"Ah, Gurudeva! Are you tired from standing for so long? Are you faint? Alas, Gurudeva! Slowly, slowly, lie down just as you are. It is only grass. Ah! The faintness has overcome you."

[Ekalavya sprinkles water from the pot upon Dronacharya's face and revives him.]

Ekalavya demonstrated the greatness of devotion to one's guru and proved that he kept his word. From that day to this, no one has failed to praise and celebrate the spirit of sacrifice he displayed.

"Was he not a true disciple? Was that not devotion to a guru?" people exclaim in wonder.

Very well. But those connoisseurs, teachers and advocates of justice, even as they praise Ekalavya's devotion...

But while praising him, should those connoisseurs, teachers and advocates of justice not also condemn Dronacharya's hardness of heart, deceitful purpose and base conduct; Arjuna's jealousy, which provoked him; and Drona's weakness in submitting to Arjuna and seeking to satisfy him? Just as they praise the disciple's fidelity to truth, should they not condemn the guru's deceitful intention?

Must the Ekalavya story serve only to teach the celebrated lesson of how a disciple should behave toward his guru? Is it not also an example of the conduct of those who became teachers to royal families in those days? Does it not show that such gurus possessed the treacherous habit of acting to please whichever pupils came from wealthy families?

Did they bring out this meaning? Will they? Would they refrain from condemning as scoundrels even those of us who do bring it out?

Ekalavya lost his thumb without committing any offence.

Drona demanded it as a guru's offering, and did so to please Arjuna after seeing that Ekalavya's mastery of archery had aroused Arjuna's jealousy.

To delight the prince's heart, that Aryan guru made the warrior of the hunter clan cut off and surrender his finger.

Does this show only Ekalavya's devotion to his guru? Does it not show Drona's heart of stone?

For many long years, instructors used this old story to explain only the greatness of devotion to a guru. Today, stories of this kind serve instead to show how those who wore the guise of sages in former times unhesitatingly performed deeds devoid of honesty and compassion.

The people cannot be prevented from receiving this new lesson. When they receive it, a storm must arise in their minds, and that storm cannot easily be subdued.

It is not only that nobody today will consent to be like Ekalavya, or dare to demand an offering as Drona did. As time passes, stories of this kind will not even be included in curricula as incidents that teach morality.

Even where it is included now, the teacher who relates it knows very well what the students think on hearing it.

One day this same story must inevitably be rewritten to serve another idea:

There was once a teacher of archery named Drona. He had an exceedingly stony heart. A hunter called Ekalavya, amazed by Drona's extraordinary skill in archery, regarded him as his guru. He made an image resembling Drona and worshipped it. Ekalavya became a great archer. When Drona's pupil Arjuna learned this, he became disgusted and reproached his guru. Wishing to please Arjuna, the guru met Ekalavya, spoke craftily, and obtained the thumb of Ekalavya's right hand as his teacher's offering. In those times there were gurus of such treachery and hardness of heart. Through many such deceitful acts, they destroyed indigenous warriors like Ekalavya. Will people fail to write the story in this way?

Ekalavya's own community had condemned even his decision to regard Dronacharya as his guru.

“However much desire you have for archery, should you disgrace our people for its sake? We are the hunter clan. Animals and birds possess far greater ability than humans to escape an arrow, yet we kill them through our art. For generation after generation we have been masters of archery.

From the little bird to the lion, we have killed them with our archery. Born into such a people, do you learn archery from an Aryan? And not even in person, it seems, but before an image! What have you done, Ekalavya?

Archery is the hereditary calling of our hunter clan. Drona's hereditary calling is only the art of grass. You are a noble son of the hunter people. How can you shamelessly call him your guru?

However much fame you gain and however astonishing your skill in archery, what is its use? People will say, "It was the grace of an Aryan; from that came his talent." Will the world of hunters respect you? Will the hunting community honour you?

Ekalavya, I believed our people would win fame through your ability. Instead, you have chosen to bear the indelible disgrace of being an Aryan's slave. Why did such an idea arise in you? Was there no one among us to teach you this archery?

A hunter chief spoke even in this fashion. Ekalavya did not heed that good counsel.

At last, when he returned home after losing the thumb of his hand, he happened to meet that chief and lowered his head.

"Ekalavya! I warned you from the beginning, but you did not listen," said the hunter chief in a choking voice.

The hero replied: "Do not grieve. What I lost was the thumb of my hand. What he lost was honour. One day the world will understand this."

The story could be extended as much as one wished, saying that scarcely a hunter saw the severed thumb lying upon the ground without shedding tears.

Why not write an entire play called *The Thumb* and stage it? Let those who rule be seized by fear!

Old stories will henceforth give only new lessons, unless they withdraw them of their own accord. Searching out and banning plays and books one after another will become the government's endless task. The more prohibitions increase, the faster new ideas will spread.

Nor will it help to fear that people will not believe the old stories if left unchanged, polish them decoratively, give them modern philosophical meanings, and send them back into circulation. Others too can perform the clever work of dressing old stories in that costume. As I have said, others will recast those very stories to express conjectures, consequences, inner meanings, states of mind and many other ideas. Writers of a new style will surely arise until people wearily cry, “The old story itself was tolerable; does this man's new interpretation not cause even greater distress?”

If one can write an *akaval* praising the serpent's dance, one can also compose a *venba* exposing its venom.

14 January 1949.

## Translator's Notes

Images 1-3, 5-6 and 8 were recovered from transparent-background scans after the original OCR pass treated them as blank. The opening scan appears to call Arjuna `பார்த்திபன்` (“prince”) before naming him directly; the translation makes the referent explicit from context. `புல்வித்தை` is a pun belittling Drona's Brahmin learning against the hunters' `வில்வித்தை`, archery; it is rendered literally as “art of grass.” `நகாசு வேலை` means decorative polishing or ornamentation, here the ideological refurbishing of old legends. `அகவல்` and `வெண்பா` are Tamil poetic forms and are transliterated because the closing contrast depends on their formal names. The terms “Aryan,” “hunter clan,” and “indigenous warriors” preserve the work's historical Dravidian movement framing.
